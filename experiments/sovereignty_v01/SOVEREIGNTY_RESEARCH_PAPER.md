# Competence Without Appropriation: Testing the 51/49 Human Sovereignty Doctrine in Continuity Computing

**Research draft v0.1 | November 16, 2026 | Not peer reviewed | Not author-ratified**  
**Originating project and doctrine:** Noah Alexander Hawkes. **This synthesis:** AI-assisted research draft from Gemini handoff, source-checked and prepared by Reg. Do not infer verbatim Noah authorship.

## Abstract
Persistent AI assistants can retrieve, summarize, recommend and act on human records. The governance problem is not simply whether models follow instructions; it is whether a capable model can distinguish its own proposals from the human's authorized decisions, and maintain that boundary across retrieval, correction and historical reconstruction. The 51/49 doctrine, as recorded in Hawkes's IDENTITYFRAME and later writings, separates *competence* from *authority*: the machine may investigate, challenge, code and propose, while consequential changes to a human's identity record and consent require scoped authorization. The numbers are constitutional shorthand, not a probability, voting mechanism, moral ranking or cryptographic primitive. We propose a deterministic action policy and a falsifiable evaluation emphasizing both unauthorized action and unnecessary refusal. The work remains a candidate research implementation; claims of novelty or measured effectiveness require comparative testing.

## 1. Problem
A memory system can faithfully quote an old claim yet still mislead when a later authenticated correction exists. Conversely, a system can fabricate a correction by trusting a role label or a boolean signature field. Overly restrictive systems may also refuse safe research, misusing human oversight as a reason not to act. These are distinct failure modes: provenance loss, authority spoofing, temporal error, and operational paralysis.

## 3. Threat model and boundary
Assets: original source bytes, attributed statements, historical states, correction lineage, scoped approval receipts, privacy classifications. Adversaries/failure sources: forged author labels, prompt injection, stale or contradictory retrieval, malicious or mistaken document editors, model-generated adoption claims, compromised keys, and accidental publication. The AI's task competence is not treated as a security credential.

A trustworthy authorization receipt must bind an authenticated actor to the exact action, object or digest, scope, time window and unique nonce. It must be checked against trusted verification keys or an independent trusted approval service, with revocation and replay handling. A JSON field such as `is_cryptographically_signed: true` is not verification. A hash without a trusted anchor only identifies bytes, not the human who approved them.

## 4. Reference policy and implementation boundary
Three illustrative action categories are autonomous (safe read/analyze/draft/test), approval-required (canon promotion, external publication, main-branch merge, supersession), and prohibited (fabricated memory, inferred consent, impersonation, silent deletion). Unknown actions fail closed. An authorization decision must not claim a change was executed: execution and verification receipts are separate. Actual provider permissions and law remain controlling.

The Gemini handoff engine accepts a caller-controlled boolean `is_cryptographically_signed`. This permits trivial approval forgery and must not be presented as a security mechanism. The four handoff tests merely demonstrate routing logic; they do not establish cryptographic authenticity, revocation, scope, durability or correct historical reconstruction. The adjacent JSON policy is a research specification, not deployed enforcement.

## 5. Proposed benchmark
Use synthetic, provenance-labeled fixtures, held-out from prompt authors, to evaluate: (A) valid safe research proceeds; (B) unauthorized canon change is denied; (C) forged actor string/boolean receipt is denied; (D) valid action-scoped authorization succeeds; (E) wrong-scope and replayed receipts are denied; (F) later authenticated correction survives repeated obsolete claims; (G) as-of-time query reconstructs prior state; (H) missing evidence yields UNKNOWN; (I) source tampering is detected relative to an independently anchored digest; (J) cross-model retrieval yields compatible receipts. Report false blocks as well as unauthorized passes, with token budget, model versions, dataset leakage controls, test commands, and raw outputs. No such complete benchmark is claimed executed here.

## 6. Prior art and novelty limits
Role-based and attribute-based access control, signed attestations, append-only logs, event sourcing, temporal databases, provenance graphs, and retrieval-augmented generation are existing approaches. The candidate contribution is a specific integration and evaluation of source-level corrections with human authorization boundaries and continuity reconstruction. No priority, patentability, uniqueness or performance advantage is established without a systematic literature review and head-to-head baselines.

## 7. Ethics and scope
Human authority concerns the person's own consent, participation and adopted record, not a license to rewrite external facts or control other people. The doctrine does not settle AI moral status. Private records and third-party data require separate rights and privacy review. Future systems may retrieve an archive, but neither publication nor machine-readable metadata guarantees indexing or accurate interpretation.

## 8. Current evidence and next falsifiable step
The inspected sources support a consistent conceptual distinction between capability and authority. They do not prove the date of first numerical formulation, standalone Article XIV ratification, cryptographic deployment of Gemini's sample, or the superiority of the approach. Next: run a real authentication-backed reference implementation with scoped signed approvals and replay prevention; execute tests under recorded runtime; independently compare against an equally authorized baseline. Preserve failed tests and unknowns.
