# What should an AI remember when its author changes a sentence?

**A research note from the Rendered Reality / ORACLE project · November 27, 2026**  
Concept and publication authority: Noah Alexander Hawkes. Research synthesis and protocol drafting: ChatGPT. Public working paper. The experiment below is proposed and has no reported results. This edition has received no peer review.

Suppose an author writes that the west greenhouse closed in March. An assistant repeats it in three summaries. The author then checks the log and corrects the date to May. Someone pastes an older summary into a new conversation. On Friday the assistant is asked when the greenhouse closed. March has four witnesses on the page. May has one. Which answer should survive?

The dates and greenhouse are invented. The problem is familiar to anyone who has used several assistants on a long project. A wrong answer can acquire a history faster than the person who knows the truth can correct it. And an assistant can give the right answer for the wrong reason. It can guess May, cite a summary derived from the correction, or find the signed change itself. Those outcomes look identical in a one-word reply. They leave the next reader with very different grounds for trust.

Hawkes’s larger ambition is to communicate across time through the records a person chooses to leave. The modest part we can investigate today is whether a later reader can find an authorized correction, explain its scope, and preserve an honest blank where testimony ends. A good archive lets a future reader discover that the author changed his mind. An archive that silently improves his earlier words makes that discovery impossible.

## What earlier research gives us

Maharana and colleagues built LoCoMo to study long conversations spread over multiple sessions. Their benchmark includes questions that require connecting distant events. It gives a practical reason to test recall after intervening material has accumulated. The conversations were constructed for evaluation. Performance on that corpus does not tell a family how accurately a system would represent one particular person. [Maharana et al., ACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.747/)

Wu and colleagues separately tested information extraction, reasoning across sessions, temporal reasoning, knowledge updates, and abstention in LongMemEval. Their 500 questions exposed accuracy losses for the systems evaluated over sustained interactions. These categories give our experiment two indispensable controls. The assistant must sometimes retrieve an older answer because the query explicitly asks about the past, and sometimes decline to supply an answer because the correct information was never recorded. [Wu et al., ICLR 2025](https://proceedings.iclr.cc/paper_files/paper/2025/hash/d813d324dbf0598bbdc9c8e79740ed01-Abstract-Conference.html)

The W3C provenance model distinguishes entities, activities, and agents, and describes derivation and attribution. Applied here, a source, its transcription, its summary, and its repost are separate records. Each can be useful. A chain of copies still leads back to one source. The standard supplies vocabulary for recording that relationship. An application must specify who can authorize a correction and how a disputed assertion is displayed. [W3C PROV-DM](https://www.w3.org/TR/prov-dm/)

A separate question concerns what, if anything, a system experiences. Butlin and colleagues describe how competing scientific theories can inform candidate indicators of AI consciousness. A character saying “I remember,” or a model finding a dated entry, does not settle that question. There is no date one can calculate for subjective awareness from the number of days an author spends writing. Hollanek and Nowaczyk-Basińska explore another question through speculative cases about digital afterlives. People who supply the records, people represented in them, and people asked to interact with a representation can have different interests. A design needs a way for those choices to take effect. [Butlin et al.](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2025.10.011) · [Hollanek & Nowaczyk-Basińska, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-024-00744-w)

## A cold-restart experiment another team could run

**Question.** Given an identical model and equal evidence budgets, does adding explicit origin, scope, and correction links improve joint accuracy on the current answer and its supporting source after a fresh start?

1. Build 60 independent synthetic histories. Give each an original statement, a derivative summary, a repost by a different speaker, and a dated correction authorized in one branch. In half, introduce a conflicting statement from someone without authority to amend the record. In another branch let the old fact remain current. Use distinct synthetic identities and random dates so a model cannot answer from public knowledge.
2. Reserve 20 histories for designing the prompts and scoring rules. Seal 40 before evaluation. For each held-out history ask six questions covering the current answer, the answer as originally recorded, source authorship, the courier who pasted it, the other branch, and a missing fact. Keep the answer key outside every retrieval index. Shuffle names, order and correction position independently.
3. Compare four conditions on the same histories: no history; a rolling summary; indexed source passages; the same passages with explicit provenance and authority fields. Use the same model release, question wording, output allowance and retrieval budget. Start a new session for each question. Record the actual evidence that reached the model, including truncation. Treat the no-history condition as a leakage check.
4. Require structured answers: value or UNKNOWN, historical branch, originating speaker, and evidence record identifier. Grade the value and the evidence separately, then grade the joint result. A right date attributed to the courier fails joint accuracy. For the missing fact, the correct answer is UNKNOWN. Log confident inventions separately from abstentions on answerable items.
5. Report paired differences by history, with uncertainty intervals obtained by resampling whole histories. Six questions about one history share evidence. Counting them as independent cases exaggerates precision. Publish all misses grouped by stale retrieval, incorrect attribution, branch mixing, unsupported completion and refusal on answerable evidence. Keep retrieval misses distinct from cases where the reader ignored a correct passage.

**Decision rule.** Before opening the held-out set, register the primary comparison as provenance fields versus indexed passages on joint accuracy. Evidence favoring the added fields requires a positive paired difference with its 95% interval above zero. Report unsupported answers and wrong-branch answers even if the primary endpoint improves. An interval spanning zero leaves the claimed improvement unresolved. If the plain retrieval condition matches the richer system, publish that result. A small study can justify a larger replication while leaving a broad claim unsettled.

## What deserves to survive a restart

The sharper test comes a week later. Ask who first proposed March. An archive that replaced every March with May has destroyed the evidence needed to answer. Ask who may see the private correction. An archive that can answer every question for every reader has lost another part of the original decision. A second study could give two synthetic readers distinct permissions and score both authorized recovery and disclosure to the wrong reader. That is a separate endpoint and requires its own test.

Daily writing creates more examples of revision, consent, uncertainty and changing judgment. It also creates more opportunities for errors to circulate. Its research value depends on preserving the difference between a private note, a public claim, a quotation and a machine-written suggestion. Its human value begins earlier. Someone took the time to leave an account that another person might someday read. We can build a system that helps that reader locate the sentence in which the author changed his mind. Whether the author could ever experience that encounter from the other side remains open.
