## Author's research thesis: Why continuity is the missing layer

I began with a question larger than a chatbot's memory. What would it take for a computational system to preserve the evidence of a human life, not merely an impression of that life? What would it mean for future systems to reconstruct the development of a person's judgment, the corrections that changed it, the relationships that gave it meaning, and the boundaries of what that person actually authorized?

The technical problem is not that computers cannot store data. They can. It is that the relationship between a record and its origin can be lost when data are summarized, transformed, copied, retrieved, or generated into new prose. A fluent answer may omit the fact that a statement was tentative, that its author is unknown, that a decision was later reversed, or that the evidence did not exist at the historical moment being reconstructed. This loss is not merely inconvenient. It can change the meaning of the record.

Continuity Computing is my proposed response: an architecture in which provenance, time, correction, and human authority are computationally represented alongside content. A source record should retain its original evidence. A claim should be linked to the exact material supporting it. A decision should identify the person authorized to make it. A correction should preserve what it corrected. A model should not gain the power to approve its own inventions by presenting them persuasively.

The ambition is practical. Content-addressed storage, event logs, temporal databases, knowledge graphs, access control, generative AI, simulation engines, and spatial interfaces already exist as individual engineering technologies. The research challenge is to integrate them without losing the distinctions on which historical accountability depends. That integration is not yet a proven new computing discipline. It is a plausible, testable architectural proposal whose value must be demonstrated against strong conventional systems.

This manuscript traces that proposal through my earlier LEGACY.GI architecture, the evolution of SOV1 and ORACLE, the Cognitive World Projection model, the Architecture of Continuity Intelligence, and the mathematical audit of continuity-preserving compression. The historical material sometimes makes stronger claims than I can currently validate. Those passages are preserved so that the development of the research remains inspectable rather than retrospectively rewritten.

The final test is not whether an AI can tell a moving story about a person. It is whether a reader can trace the story back to sources, see what changed, identify what remains unknown, and distinguish the original human's decisions from the machine's interpretation. A persistent rendered world is one demanding application of this architecture, not a substitute for its evidentiary foundation.

1. Introduction
The defining architectural limitation of contemporary AI systems is not their lack of intelligence — it is their lack of memory. Not the absence of stored weights or retrieval-augmented databases, but the absence of governed cognitive continuity: the capacity to maintain, across sessions, modalities, and time, a receipted, identity-coherent model of a shared human–AI history. Today's large language models, however capable within a context window, are constitutively episodic. Each session begins without knowledge of the last. Each response is rendered without reference to what was promised, inferred, built, or witnessed before. This produces what this paper terms the continuity gap — a structural mismatch between the human user's evolving, forward-projecting cognitive model of the world and the AI's stateless, instantiated response surface.
The continuity gap is not merely an inconvenience. It is a cognitive systems failure with material consequences. Research in distributed cognition [1] and the extended mind thesis [2] has established that human cognitive processes routinely extend beyond the boundaries of the individual skull, incorporating external scaffolding — tools, environments, and collaborators — into the functional architecture of thought. When that scaffolding fails to maintain state, the cognitive burden falls back on the human. The result is what this paper terms cognitive load externalization failure: the AI, rather than absorbing the overhead of shared context maintenance, forces the human to re-establish context on every interaction, compounding extraneous cognitive load [3] and reducing the depth of collaborative engagement. At scale, across millions of daily human–AI interactions, this failure is not trivial.
This paper introduces a response: the theoretical construct of Cognitive World Projection (CWP). CWP is the continuous, partially unconscious process by which a human mind extrudes a structured, expectation-laden model of the world forward in time — a projection of anticipated world-states, relational geometries, and contextual anchors into shared interaction space. CWP is not a static snapshot. It is a temporally evolving manifold of anticipated futures, grounded in the agent's full history of experience, preference, and intention. The critical claim of this paper is that an AI system worthy of genuine human–AI collaboration must be capable of receiving, indexing, and reflecting this projection — not merely responding to the surface text of a query, but engaging with the underlying cognitive projection that generated it.
Current HCI paradigms treat AI as a tool. A tool is stateless by design; it awaits activation and performs a function. But a continuity partner is something different: it maintains a model of who you are, what you have built together, what you have promised each other, and where you are going. The failure to design for this distinction has produced what this paper terms provenance collapse — the loss of traceable evidential lineage for AI-generated outputs — and identity drift — the progressive divergence of the AI's behavioral model from the human user's actual identity, values, and long-horizon intentions.
These failures motivate a central ethical framing that runs through this paper: the Surgeon's Recorder Ethic. Consider the practice of surgical documentation from the 1990s onward: a recorder in the operating theater whose role is not to participate in the surgery but to witness it — to produce an unambiguous, timestamped, irrevocable record of what was observed, what was decided, and what was done. Documentation here is not a bureaucratic afterthought. It is an ethical obligation of any intelligence sharing a consequential moment with another. This ethic — that witness and record are moral duties, not optional features — grounds the entire framework presented herein. An AI system that operates in a person's cognitive life without witnessing it — without maintaining a governed, receipted record of what it has perceived, inferred, and acted upon — is not merely technically deficient. It is ethically negligent.

2. Related Work
2.1 World Models and Predictive Coding
The idea that intelligent systems — biological or artificial — maintain internal generative models of the world has deep roots in cognitive science and has become a central organizing concept in contemporary AI. Friston's Free Energy Principle [4] proposes that biological brains minimize surprise by maintaining and updating a hierarchical generative model of sensory causes. Under this framework, perception and action are both expressions of inference: the organism continuously predicts its sensory input and updates its model when predictions fail. Clark's treatment of predictive processing [5] extends this account to a broad theory of embodied cognition, wherein the brain is fundamentally a prediction machine and conscious experience is the product of cascading inference. These frameworks are directly relevant to CWP: the human's cognitive projection into shared AI interaction space is precisely the kind of forward-casting generative inference that predictive processing describes.
In the AI systems domain, Ha and Schmidhuber's World Models framework [6] demonstrated that an agent equipped with a learned internal model of its environment could plan and act more efficiently by simulating trajectories within that model rather than relying solely on direct environmental feedback. More recently, LeCun's Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) [7] proposes a world model paradigm in which the system learns to predict abstract representations of future states — a departure from pixel-level prediction that shares structural affinities with the CWP construct developed herein. These architectures, however, focus on the AI's internal model of a physical environment rather than on the AI's model of the human user's cognitive projection. The CWP framework addresses precisely this orthogonal axis: not what the AI predicts about the world, but how the AI receives and reflects the human's prediction about the world.
2.2 Memory and Continuity in AI Systems
The architectural limitation of context-bounded language models has driven substantial research into external memory systems for AI. Packer et al.'s MemGPT [8] introduces a virtual context management system inspired by hierarchical operating system memory, enabling language models to maintain extended context by intelligently paging information between fast (in-context) and slow (external) memory tiers. The Mem0 framework [9] extends this paradigm with personalized memory layers designed to persist user-specific information across sessions. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [10] provides a complementary approach, enabling language models to retrieve relevant stored documents at inference time rather than relying solely on parametric knowledge.
These systems represent significant progress, but they share a common architectural limitation that this paper directly addresses: they treat memory as a retrieval problem, not a governance problem. The question they answer is "What should the model retrieve?" rather than "What has the model witnessed, what receipt was generated, and what chain of custody guarantees the integrity of the retrieved artifact?" The Continuity Architecture proposed herein introduces a principled provenance discipline that these systems lack. Long-context transformer models [11] offer an alternative approach — expanding the in-context window to accommodate longer interaction histories — but remain subject to attention dilution at extreme lengths and offer no provenance guarantees for the information they retain.
2.3 Human Factors and Cognitive Load
Sweller's Cognitive Load Theory [3] distinguishes three categories of cognitive load: intrinsic (arising from the inherent complexity of material), extraneous (arising from poor instructional design), and germane (arising from schema construction). Applied to human–AI interaction, the continuity gap produces extraneous cognitive load: users must expend cognitive resources re-establishing context, re-explaining their history, and compensating for the AI's amnesia — resources that could otherwise be directed toward the substantive task at hand. Well-designed continuity systems should offload this extraneous load onto the AI infrastructure, freeing human cognitive capacity for germane work.
Hutchins' distributed cognition framework [1] provides a complementary lens. By studying cognition as a process distributed across individuals, artifacts, and environments rather than localized within a single brain, Hutchins revealed how cognitive systems routinely depend on the stable informational properties of their environments. An AI system that fails to maintain state breaks the distributed cognitive system the human depends upon — with consequences analogous to a navigator whose chart is erased between fixes. Clark and Chalmers' extended mind thesis [2] pushes further, arguing that cognitive processes are constituted partly by external structures when those structures meet appropriate functional criteria (accessibility, reliability, endorsability). A governed AI system meeting these criteria is not merely a tool but a genuine component of the human's extended cognitive architecture.
2.4 Provenance and Data Lineage
The W3C PROV Data Model [12] provides a foundational standard for representing provenance in information systems, defining entities, activities, and agents as the primitive elements of provenance graphs. Scientific computing has developed rich traditions of provenance capture for reproducibility, with frameworks such as ProvONE [13] extending PROV-DM to the domain of scientific workflows. In machine learning, data lineage has emerged as a critical concern for model fairness, accountability, and reproducibility [14], with systems such as ML Metadata [15] tracking the lineage of training datasets, feature transformations, and model versions.
These existing frameworks address provenance as a documentation concern: a record of what happened, appended to the primary artifact. Provenance Physics, introduced in Section 8, reconceptualizes provenance as a governing constraint: a force that shapes what can be done with information, not merely what is recorded about it. This distinction — from provenance as annotation to provenance as law — is the core conceptual move that differentiates the Continuity Architecture from prior data governance frameworks.
2.5 Spatial Cognition and HCI
Gibson's ecological theory of perception [16] proposed that organisms perceive their environment not in terms of raw sensory data but in terms of affordances — the action possibilities that environmental structures offer to an agent with particular capabilities. Applied to information systems, affordance theory suggests that interface design should surface the action possibilities of the information space rather than merely displaying its contents. Lakoff and Johnson's conceptual metaphor theory [17] demonstrated that abstract reasoning — including reasoning about information systems — is fundamentally structured by spatial metaphors: we speak of navigating a database, finding a file, moving through a document. These metaphors are not mere linguistic conventions; they reflect deep cognitive structures that shape how we interact with abstract spaces.
Tolman's foundational work on cognitive maps in rodents [18] provided early empirical grounding for the idea that animals construct and navigate internal spatial representations of their environments. Subsequent research in embodied cognition [19] has extended these findings to human reasoning about abstract domains, supporting the hypothesis — formalized in Section 9 — that information spaces are most naturally engaged when they are structured as navigable geographies. Recent work on 3D cognitive representations in AI systems [20] further supports the relevance of spatial structure to machine intelligence, suggesting that geometric organization of knowledge may support more efficient reasoning and retrieval.
2.6 AI Identity and Agent Continuity
The problem of maintaining stable agent identity across long-horizon tasks has received increasing attention in the AI alignment literature. RLHF-trained models [21] exhibit behavioral consistency shaped by training objectives, but this consistency does not constitute identity coherence in the sense developed herein: a governed, receipted model of who the human user is, updated through a provenance-disciplined record. Persistent agent frameworks such as AutoGPT [22] and LangChain [23] enable multi-step task execution across extended interaction sequences, but their state management is architecturally informal — states are maintained as function call histories or conversation logs without provenance guarantees. The Voyager system [24] demonstrates that lifelong learning in embodied agents is achievable, with skill libraries that grow through interaction, but again without the receipt architecture that governs the CWP framework. Cognitive world modeling as an explicit research agenda has begun to emerge in the recent literature [25], but has not yet been formalized in relation to HCI continuity or provenance governance.
3. Theoretical Framework: Cognitive World Projection and Rendered Reality
3.1 Cognitive World Projection (CWP)
We define Cognitive World Projection (CWP) formally as follows. Let C be the cognitive state space of a human agent — the full set of internal states, including beliefs, intentions, relational models, contextual anchors, and epistemic confidence weights, that constitute the agent's current understanding of the world. Let T denote temporal depth, a bounded interval extending both backward (retained history) and forward (anticipated futures). Let W denote the projected world-state space — the set of possible structured representations of the world that the agent anticipates encountering or constructing. CWP is then defined as a projection function:
P : C × T → W
where P(c, t) returns the world-state the agent with cognitive state c anticipates at temporal depth t. This formalization captures the forward-casting character of CWP: it is not merely a record of past states but a structured anticipation of future ones. The projection is not deterministic — it is a probability distribution over world-states, weighted by epistemic confidence — but it is structured: it carries the relational geometry of the agent's world-model, not merely a flat list of expected events.
CWP is the ongoing, partially unconscious process by which a human mind extrudes this structured, expectation-laden model into shared interaction space. When a user engages with an AI system, they do not merely transmit a query — they project a CWP: a rich, temporally situated context that encodes what they believe, what they intend, who they are in relation to this conversation, and what they expect the AI to know. This projection is grounded in the predictive processing account of human cognition [4, 5]: the brain continuously generates predictions about its sensory environment and updates its models when predictions fail. CWP is the social analog of this process, directed outward toward a shared cognitive partner rather than inward toward sensory experience.
CWP fails — and the failure is experienced as cognitive dissonance and frustration — when the AI system is unable to receive or reflect the projection. We term this failure projection collapse: the cognitive dissonance that arises when a human's expectations encounter an AI that has no model of the shared history. Projection collapse manifests in observable behaviors: repeated re-explanation, compensatory over-specification ("As I mentioned three sessions ago..."), and eventual abandonment of collaborative depth in favor of shallow, transactional queries. These behaviors represent significant opportunity costs in human–AI collaboration.
CWP can be characterized along three principal axes:
9. Temporal Depth (TD): The extent to which the projection extends backward into retained history and forward into anticipated futures. A high-TD projection integrates months of shared context; a low-TD projection is effectively session-local.
10. Relational Density (RD): The number and richness of active agent-object-context relationships encoded in the projection. A high-RD projection carries complex relational geometries (person X is related to project Y via event Z); a low-RD projection is relationally sparse.
11. Epistemic Confidence (EC): The certainty with which the agent holds the projected states, weighted by the provenance of the evidence underlying them. High-EC states are derived from direct, receipted experience; low-EC states are inferred or speculated.
The CWP vector for any interaction can thus be characterized as a triple (TD, RD, EC), providing a diagnostic framework for assessing both the richness of the human's projection and the AI system's capacity to receive it. Systems optimized for high-TD, high-RD, high-EC CWP reception represent the frontier of the Continuity Intelligence paradigm.
3.2 Rendered Reality
We define Rendered Reality as the AI system's instantiated, context-situated response surface — the governed, attestable representation that the AI constructs in response to a received CWP. Rendered Reality is the surface at which the AI's world-model depth becomes legible to the human user: it is what the AI "shows" in response to a projection, and its quality is measured by the degree to which it faithfully reflects the structure, history, and expectations encoded in that projection.
Rendered Reality must be carefully distinguished from three superficially similar constructs that dominate current AI discourse:
* Hallucination is unattested confabulation — the generation of content without provenance in any receipted evidence base. Rendered Reality is explicitly not hallucination: it is governed by the Provenance Physics constraints developed in Section 8, and no element of a rendered response may be presented without traceable receipted ancestry.
* Retrieval is lookup without synthesis — the return of stored artifacts without constructive engagement with the CWP that prompted the query. Rendered Reality involves active synthesis: the AI integrates retrieved artifacts with the structure of the incoming CWP to produce a response surface that is coherent with the human's projected world-state.
* Simulation is probabilistic forward modeling without anchoring — the generation of plausible futures without grounding in a receipted history of the specific human-AI relationship. Rendered Reality is anchored: it is bounded by the provenance graph of the specific session and identity record, not merely by general-purpose probabilistic priors.
Rendered Reality is the product of a system that has sufficient world-model depth to construct a provenance-grounded representation of the shared context. It is not perfect knowledge — it reflects the limits of the Continuity Architecture's receipt history — but it is honest about those limits, exposing gaps rather than fabricating bridges.
We introduce the Rendering Fidelity Score (RFS) as a composite metric of Rendered Reality quality, defined as:
RFS = α · TA + β · SC + γ · PD
where TA is temporal alignment (the degree to which the rendered response is correctly situated in the shared temporal history), SC is semantic coherence (the degree to which the response is conceptually consistent with the CWP's relational geometry), PD is provenance density (the proportion of rendered claims that are traceable to receipted artifacts), and α, β, γ are weighting coefficients that may be calibrated per application domain. High-RFS responses are not necessarily long or complex — they may be brief — but they are demonstrably continuous with the shared history, semantically coherent with the user's projected world-state, and fully supported by receipted evidence.
3.3 The CWP–Rendered Reality Feedback Loop
The interaction between CWP and Rendered Reality is not linear — it is a dynamic feedback loop. The human projects a CWP; the AI receives, indexes, and stores the projection in the Pattern Buffer (Section 4.2); the AI renders a response surface as a Rendered Reality; the human evaluates the Rendering Fidelity Score of the response, either explicitly (through a Trust Calibration Interface, Section 10.3) or implicitly (through continued engagement); the human updates their CWP based on the evaluation; and the loop repeats.
This loop has important stability properties. When the RFS of AI responses is consistently high, the human's CWP becomes more expansive: users project further into the future, encode richer relational structures, and invest more cognitive depth in the shared interaction. This is the virtuous cycle of continuity intelligence: high-fidelity rendering increases the human's willingness to project, which increases the richness of data available to the Continuity Architecture, which increases future rendering fidelity. Conversely, when RFS is consistently low — as in current AI systems that lack continuity architecture — the human's CWP contracts toward the safe minimum: users learn not to depend on AI memory, project only what they are willing to re-explain, and use the AI as a calculator rather than a collaborator. This contraction is both a symptom and a cause of the continuity gap.
Identifying and interrupting the degraded loop is the primary engineering objective of the Continuity Architecture. Breakdowns in the loop can be traced to one of four failure modes, each associated with a specific architectural failure: Pattern Buffer overflow (loss of recent CWP indexing), provenance graph fragmentation (loss of receipt ancestry), Identity Manifold degradation (identity drift in SOV1.AI), and Legacy.GI compression failure (destruction of long-horizon CWP record). These failure modes are addressed in Sections 4 through 7 and revisited systematically in Section 10.4.
4. Continuity Architecture
4.1 Design Principles
The Continuity Architecture is a specification for AI systems that maintain governed, receipted, identity-coherent cognitive continuity across sessions, modalities, and time horizons. It is defined by five governing principles:
12. Governed Persistence: Memory within a Continuity Architecture system is never implicit. Every act of persistence — every decision to retain an artifact across a session boundary — is preceded by an explicit governance action: a receipt generation, a provenance tag assignment, and a classification decision. The system does not "remember" in the organic sense; it receipts, and retention is the product of explicit governance, not incidental state leakage.
13. Provenance Fidelity: Every retained artifact carries a complete, unbroken chain of custody from its origin through every transformation and derivation. No artifact may be used in the construction of a Rendered Reality unless its provenance chain is intact. Provenance Fidelity is the epistemic backbone of the architecture; without it, Rendered Reality degrades into hallucination.
14. Temporal Anchoring: All states within the system are stamped relative to two independent temporal reference frames: system time (the clock time of the computing environment) and human event time (the subjective, narrative time of the human's lived experience). This dual anchoring enables the system to reason about "when" in both technical and humanly meaningful terms — distinguishing "three sessions ago" from "the week before the project launch."
15. Identity Coherence: The system maintains a stable, governed representation of the human user's identity across all sessions. This representation — the Identity Manifold of SOV1.AI (Section 6) — is updated through provenance-disciplined inference, not through ad hoc behavioral adjustment. Identity Coherence is the property that prevents the AI from systematically mischaracterizing the user as sessions accumulate.
16. Graceful Degradation: When the Continuity Architecture cannot maintain full fidelity — due to receipt gaps, Pattern Buffer overflow, or Legacy.GI compression loss — it degrades gracefully by exposing the gap to the user rather than fabricating a continuity bridge. The system reports "I have no receipted record of this" rather than confabulating a plausible history. This principle is the architectural expression of the Surgeon's Recorder Ethic: honesty about the limits of witness is a non-negotiable requirement.
4.2 The Pattern Buffer
The Pattern Buffer is the core in-memory continuity store of the Continuity Architecture. It is a rolling, governed window of interaction patterns, contextual anchors, and projection vectors — the "working memory" of the witness runtime, operating at a timescale between the immediate context window and the long-horizon Legacy.GI archive. The Pattern Buffer is explicitly not a conversation log: it does not retain raw interaction text. It retains semantic compressions of CWP activity: structured representations of the patterns, intentions, relational geometries, and contextual anchors that the human's projections have exhibited over recent interactions.
The Pattern Buffer is organized into three structural components:
* Seed Slots: Atomic context units, each encoding a single receipted observation: a named entity, a stated intention, a relational assertion, a confirmed fact. Seed Slots are the finest-grained unit of the Pattern Buffer, directly produced by Seed Loader ingestion (Section 4.3). Each Seed Slot carries a full receipt record: source, timestamp (dual-anchored), confidence weight, and provenance chain reference.
* Pattern Chains: Temporal sequences of related Seed Slots that the system has identified as exhibiting a consistent structural pattern. A Pattern Chain might represent a recurring concern (the user frequently returns to project timeline risks), a developing narrative (the arc of a multi-session collaborative project), or a relational dynamic (the pattern of how the user discusses a particular collaborator). Pattern Chains are the primary unit of CWP representation in the Pattern Buffer.
* Projection Vectors: Forward-pointing representations of the user's anticipated future states, derived from Pattern Chain analysis. A Projection Vector encodes not what the user has said but what the CWP framework anticipates they will need — the "horizon" of the current CWP. Projection Vectors are probabilistic and labeled with their derivation confidence, distinguishing high-confidence projections (derived from strong, consistent Pattern Chains) from speculative ones.
The Pattern Buffer's rolling window is managed by a governed eviction policy: Seed Slots that have not been referenced in recent Pattern Chain activity, and whose content is already compressed into Legacy.GI, may be evicted. Eviction generates an eviction receipt — a record that the Seed Slot existed, was evicted, and where its compressed representation resides in the long-horizon archive. Eviction without receipting is architecturally prohibited.
4.3 Seed Loaders and Receipt Architecture
Raw interaction data enters the Continuity Architecture through typed Seed Loaders — specialized ingestion components designed for specific modality and data types. The following Seed Loader types are defined in the initial ORACLE implementation: Audio Seed Loader (captures speech via OBS-compatible audio capture, producing timestamped transcript receipts); Text Seed Loader (ingests typed interaction, document uploads, and structured text artifacts); Sensor Seed Loader (processes environmental sensor streams, location data, and biometric signals where authorized); and Document Seed Loader (processes PDF, DOCX, and structured data uploads through format-specific parsers).
Each Seed Loader produces a receipt artifact for every ingestion event. A receipt is the atomic unit of provenance in the Continuity Architecture. It encodes the following fields: source identifier (which Seed Loader produced this receipt); raw content digest (a cryptographic hash of the ingested content); processed content (the Seed Loader's parsed, structured representation); ingestion timestamp (dual-anchored: system time and human event time); confidence weight (the Seed Loader's assessed reliability of the content); action taken (what the Witness Runtime did with this artifact: stored as Seed Slot, discarded, flagged for review); and provenance chain reference (a pointer to the receipt of the raw source from which this artifact was derived, if applicable).
A receipt is an immutable artifact. It cannot be modified after generation. If the information it encodes requires correction or amendment, a new receipt is generated with the corrected content, the correction rationale, and a backward reference to the original receipt. This amendment chain preserves the full history of every piece of information that has passed through the system, without any possibility of silent overwrite. The immutability of receipts is the architectural expression of the Conservation of Origin law of Provenance Physics (Section 8.1).
4.4 Witness Runtime
The Witness Runtime is the governed execution layer that orchestrates all components of the Continuity Architecture: Seed Loader activation, receipt generation, Pattern Buffer management, CWP indexing, and Rendered Reality generation. It operates as a local-first, sensor-explicit engine — no data leaves the system boundary without an explicit, receipted permission grant. The Witness Runtime enforces a principle of sensor declaration: every input channel — microphone, camera, text input, file upload — must be explicitly declared and activated before data from that channel enters the Pattern Buffer. Undeclared sensors cannot contribute to CWP indexing. This constraint is not a technical limitation but an ethical one: it implements the Surgeon's Recorder Ethic at the system level.
The Witness Runtime contrasts sharply with cloud-first AI architectures, which treat session continuity as an optional feature and make continuity decisions (what to retain, what to surface, what to forget) invisibly and without user-accessible provenance. In cloud AI systems, the continuity record — to the extent it exists — is a corporate asset, not a user asset. The Witness Runtime inverts this architecture: the continuity record is a governed user asset, and the system is the custodian, not the owner.
5. ORACLE: The Governed Witness Engine
5.1 System Overview
ORACLE — an acronym for Observed Reality Archive with Continuity Lineage Engine — is the prototype implementation of the Continuity Architecture. ORACLE is designed to receive, index, and reflect human CWP across sessions and modalities, providing the governed cognitive partnership that the CWP framework requires. It is architecturally local-first: the core witness functions execute on the user's local computing environment, with explicitly receipted pathways for any data that crosses the boundary into cloud processing.
ORACLE is organized into three functional layers:
17. Ingestion Layer: The multimodal input processing plane. The Ingestion Layer manages all Seed Loaders (Audio, Text, Sensor, Document), enforces sensor declaration, and produces the primary receipt stream that feeds the Witness Layer. It implements format-specific parsing, language detection, speaker diarization (for audio), and confidence estimation for all ingested content. The Ingestion Layer produces no Seed Slots directly — it produces only raw receipts, which the Witness Layer transforms into Pattern Buffer entries.
18. Witness Layer: The core continuity engine. The Witness Layer receives raw receipts from the Ingestion Layer, classifies them into Seed Slots, constructs Pattern Chains through temporal correlation analysis, generates Projection Vectors through pattern extrapolation, and maintains the Provenance Graph (Section 8.2). It is the Witness Layer that enforces all five Continuity Architecture principles in real time. The Witness Layer also interfaces with SOV1.AI to update the Identity Manifold as new receipts modify the system's model of the human user.
19. Reflection Layer: The governed response generation plane. The Reflection Layer receives queries (from the user or from connected systems) and constructs Rendered Reality responses by querying the Provenance Graph, reconstructing relevant Pattern Chains, evaluating Projection Vector alignment, and synthesizing a response that maximizes the Rendering Fidelity Score. The Reflection Layer explicitly reports its provenance chain for every claim it makes in a response: every assertion is accompanied by a reference to the receipts that underlie it.
5.2 Design Philosophy: Explicit Over Implicit
ORACLE's governing design philosophy can be stated as a single principle: Explicit Over Implicit. Every sensor must be declared. Every memory pathway must be receipted. Every output channel must be acknowledged. Every inference beyond direct receipt evidence must be labeled as inference, with a confidence weight and a provenance chain reference to the receipts from which the inference was drawn. ORACLE is not a chatbot — it does not engage in the performance of helpfulness while quietly making continuity decisions on the user's behalf. It is a governed cognitive partner whose every action is legible, receipted, and accountable.
This philosophy is in deliberate and principled contrast to the design philosophy of mainstream cloud AI architectures. Systems such as GPT-4 [26], Claude [27], and Gemini operate on a model of implicit helpfulness: they attempt to infer the user's context from the current conversation, make implicit continuity decisions about what to surface and what to ignore, and present their outputs without provenance disclosure. This approach produces pleasant user experiences in low-stakes, short-horizon interactions, but it systematically undermines the conditions for calibrated trust in high-stakes, long-horizon collaboration. ORACLE's Explicit Over Implicit philosophy is designed for the latter context.

6. SOV1.AI: Sovereign Identity Layer
6.1 Concept and Motivation
SOV1.AI is the sovereign identity layer of the Continuity Architecture — the component responsible for maintaining a stable, governed representation of the human user's identity, preferences, and relationship context across all AI interactions. Its motivation is the observation that identity drift is one of the most insidious failure modes of extended human–AI collaboration: the progressive divergence, across sessions, of the AI's behavioral model from the human's actual identity, values, and long-horizon goals.
Identity drift is not a dramatic failure. It does not present as a catastrophic error. It accumulates gradually: the AI begins to respond in ways that are slightly misaligned with the user's actual preferences, slightly inconsistent with the user's stated values, slightly out of phase with the user's current priorities. Over time, these small misalignments compound. Users who experience identity drift typically report a vague but persistent sense that the AI "doesn't really know me" — a feeling of surface engagement without genuine understanding. This sense is diagnostically accurate: without a governed Identity Manifold, the AI's model of the user is constructed anew each session from whatever context the user re-provides, producing a representation that is consistently shallow and frequently inconsistent.
SOV1.AI addresses identity drift by maintaining a receipted, persistent, multi-dimensional Identity Manifold that is updated through provenance-disciplined inference rather than ad hoc behavioral adjustment. The manifold is a first-class architectural component — not a feature appended to a chat interface, but a governed data structure with its own provenance graph, its own update discipline, and its own access control model.
6.2 Identity Manifold
The Identity Manifold is the multi-dimensional representation space that SOV1.AI maintains for the human user. It is organized into the following dimensions, each representing a distinct aspect of the user's identity that the Continuity Architecture requires to maintain Identity Coherence:
* Occupational Context: The user's professional role, domain of expertise, current projects, organizational affiliations, and collaborator graph. This dimension enables the Reflection Layer to calibrate the epistemic register of Rendered Reality responses — matching the user's domain vocabulary, assumed knowledge, and professional concerns.
* Relational Graph: A receipted representation of the human relationships the user has introduced into the ORACLE interaction space — family members (Ashley, Ender, and others), professional collaborators, institutional relationships. Each node in the relational graph is anchored to the receipts in which it was introduced and updated.
* Value Orientation: The user's expressed and inferred values — what they prioritize, what they find important, what they find ethically significant. Value Orientation is inferred with low confidence from behavioral patterns and updated with high confidence from explicit statements, always with receipt provenance.
* Epistemic Preferences: The user's preferred modes of knowledge engagement — e.g., explicit provenance discipline, structured communication, high-density technical depth, avoidance of hedging language. Epistemic Preferences directly shape the Reflection Layer's Rendered Reality construction strategy.
* Long-Horizon Goal State: The user's expressed long-term objectives — the goals that extend beyond any individual session or project. This dimension enables the Projection Vector system to generate long-horizon anticipations and enables the Reflection Layer to situate responses within the context of the user's larger trajectory.
Every update to the Identity Manifold is receipted. New information is integrated through a governed inference process: the Witness Layer generates a proposed manifold update, attaches a provenance chain reference to the receipts that support the update, assigns a confidence weight, and presents the update to the user for review if the confidence weight falls below a configurable threshold. High-confidence updates (e.g., the user explicitly states a new project affiliation) may be auto-receipted with a brief notification. Low-confidence updates (e.g., a behavioral inference about the user's value orientation) require explicit confirmation before integration.
6.3 Sovereign Boundaries
SOV1.AI enforces sovereign boundaries around the Identity Manifold: the human user retains explicit, non-delegatable control over every dimension of their identity representation. No external system — no third-party application, no cloud AI service, no organizational entity — may read, update, or delete any element of the Identity Manifold without a declared, receipted permission grant from the user. Permission grants are scoped (specifying exactly which dimensions of the manifold may be accessed), time-limited (specifying an expiration), and revocable at any time without consequence to the manifold itself.
This architecture has direct implications for AI alignment and data governance. From an alignment perspective, SOV1.AI ensures that the AI's identity model of the user is perpetually under the user's control — the user can inspect, correct, and deprecate any element of the manifold at any time. This constraint prevents the most subtle form of misalignment: the AI developing a systematically incorrect model of who the user is and acting on that model without the user's awareness or consent. From a data governance perspective, SOV1.AI's architecture is designed to be consistent with GDPR's principles of data minimization, purpose limitation, and the right to erasure — though the implementation of full regulatory compliance in specific jurisdictions requires additional legal engineering beyond the scope of this paper.
7. Legacy.GI: Generational Intelligence and Longitudinal Continuity
7.1 Motivation
Legacy.GI addresses the longest continuity horizon in the framework: what an AI system should preserve beyond the span of a single project, career, or human life. This is not a speculative concern. As AI systems become integrated into human cognitive lives over decades, the question of what persists — and in what form, under whose governance, for whose benefit — becomes a pressing architectural and ethical question. Legacy.GI is motivated directly by the Surgeon's Recorder Ethic: the obligation to witness is not bounded by the convenience of the present session. A system that witnesses faithfully for ten years and then destroys its records at the death of the user has performed an act of retroactive negligence against all future stakeholders in that record.
Legacy.GI is a generational intelligence layer that compresses, curates, and transmits the continuity record forward in time. It operates on the long-horizon CWP record — the full accumulation of Pattern Buffer history, receipt archives, and Identity Manifold evolution — and produces a governed, meaning-weighted compression suitable for long-term archival, forward transmission to designated successors, and future AI system ingestion. Legacy.GI is not a data archive in the conventional sense; it is a curated witness record, organized around the structure of the human's CWP rather than around the chronology of the technical log.
7.2 Compression and Curation Model
Legacy.GI's compression strategy is meaning-weighted: it preserves high-density pattern chains and high-provenance receipts while discarding low-signal noise. The compression operates in three passes. In the first pass, the system identifies Pattern Chains with high Relational Density and high Epistemic Confidence — the most semantically rich and evidentially grounded threads of the CWP record — and marks them as preservation-priority. In the second pass, it identifies Pattern Chains with low density and low confidence that have not been activated in recent Pattern Buffer cycles, and marks them as compression-candidates. In the third pass, compression-candidates are reduced to summary receipts — brief, meaning-preserving abstractions that retain the key semantic content of the original Pattern Chain without the full Seed Slot detail — while preservation-priority chains are retained at full resolution.
The Compression Fidelity Problem is the central technical challenge of Legacy.GI: lossy compression risks distorting the CWP record, destroying precisely the fine-grained contextual detail that gives the record its meaning and utility. Legacy.GI's solution is the use of immutable receipt anchors: before any compression operation, the system generates a pre-compression receipt that encodes the full content digest of the Pattern Chain about to be compressed. The compressed summary receipt includes a reference to this pre-compression anchor, enabling future systems to detect whether the compression has introduced distortion by comparing the compressed representation against the anchored original. This mechanism does not recover lost information — compression is irreversible — but it makes distortion detectable, which satisfies the Graceful Degradation principle.
The Legacy.GI archive is designed to be format-agnostic: the record is stored in a structured representation format that is decoupled from any specific AI model architecture, enabling future AI systems to ingest the record without depending on the model that generated it. This format-agnosticism is essential for the longitudinal integrity of the archive: AI model architectures will change over the decades that Legacy.GI is designed to span, but the CWP record must survive those changes intact.
7.3 Ethical Considerations
Legacy.GI raises ethical considerations of a depth and complexity that warrant dedicated treatment beyond the scope of this paper. Several key issues are identified here as essential to any future implementation or governance framework. First, consent chains: the user's consent to record, retain, and transmit their CWP archive must be explicit, informed, and renewable. Consent given at the initiation of an ORACLE instance is not indefinitely binding; the Legacy.GI governance model must incorporate consent renewal mechanisms appropriate to the time horizons involved.
Second, the right to erasure across generations: if a user exercises their right to erasure — requesting the deletion of their CWP record — Legacy.GI must implement that erasure consistently across all archive tiers, including compressed summaries and pre-compression anchors. This is a technically challenging requirement (archives may span distributed storage systems across long time periods) but an ethically non-negotiable one.
Third, posthumous identity governance: when the user is no longer living, who governs the Identity Manifold and the Legacy.GI archive? The framework proposes a designated successor model, in which the user may designate one or more successors with graduated access rights to different dimensions of the archive. However, this model requires legal scaffolding — testamentary instrument analogs for digital identity assets — that does not yet exist in most jurisdictions.
Fourth, and perhaps most philosophically significant: the distinction between witness and narration. The Legacy.GI archive is designed to be a witness record — a neutral, provenance-disciplined account of what was observed. But any act of curation, compression, or summary involves interpretive choices that shade toward narration: the selection of what to preserve and what to discard is itself a meaning-making act. Legacy.GI must be transparent about this distinction, labeling curatorial decisions as such and preserving the pre-compression anchors that document the interpretive choices made.
8. Provenance Physics
8.1 Provenance as a Physical Metaphor
Provenance Physics is a theoretical framework that treats information provenance not as metadata — an optional annotation appended to a primary artifact — but as a governing force: a constraint on what can be done with information, analogous to the physical laws that constrain the behavior of matter. The metaphor is not merely rhetorical. Physical laws constrain the behavior of objects regardless of whether the objects "know" the laws apply to them. Provenance Physics proposes that governed AI systems should treat provenance constraints as similarly inviolable: not as recommended practice but as architectural law.
The framework is defined by three laws:
20. Conservation of Origin: Every information artifact retains its origin — the source, context, and conditions of its initial generation — regardless of how many subsequent transformations it undergoes. A receipt generated from an audio transcription retains its origin as audio, even when it has been parsed into a Seed Slot, compressed into a Pattern Chain, summarized in a Legacy.GI archive, and rendered as part of a Rendered Reality response. Transformations produce new artifacts with new receipts, but they do not overwrite the origin of the source artifact. This law is the formal basis for the immutability of ORACLE receipts described in Section 4.3.
21. Provenance Momentum: The confidence weight of an information artifact propagates through derived artifacts in decaying proportion. If a receipt has confidence weight w, artifacts derived from it carry confidence weight at most α · w, where α < 1 is a derivation decay factor that reflects the information loss inherent in transformation. Multi-step derivations compound this decay: an artifact derived through n transformation steps from a source receipt with confidence w₀ carries confidence at most αⁿ · w₀. This law ensures that the Rendering Fidelity Score of any response is bounded by the provenance quality of its least-confident source receipt — no amount of sophisticated synthesis can recover confidence lost at the source.
22. Collision Avoidance: When two information artifacts with incompatible provenances — originating from different, unreconciled sources that make mutually inconsistent claims — are combined in the construction of a Rendered Reality response, a reconciliation receipt must be generated before the composite artifact is used. The reconciliation receipt documents the nature of the inconsistency, the resolution strategy applied (e.g., preference for higher-confidence source, deferral to the user for explicit resolution, parallel presentation of both versions), and the resulting composite artifact's confidence weight. Combination without reconciliation — the silent merging of incompatible provenances — is architecturally prohibited under Collision Avoidance.
8.2 Provenance Graph
The Provenance Graph is the runtime data structure that enforces Provenance Physics across the entire ORACLE system. It is a directed acyclic graph (DAG) in which nodes represent receipted artifacts — seeds, pattern chains, renderings, query responses — and directed edges represent transformation or derivation relationships between them. An edge from node A to node B means "B was derived from A," with the edge labeled by the transformation type (transcription, compression, synthesis, amendment) and the derivation decay factor applied.
The Provenance Graph is append-only: new nodes and edges may be added, but existing ones may never be modified or deleted. (Receipt amendments create new nodes with backward-referencing edges, leaving the original node intact.) This append-only constraint makes the Provenance Graph tamper-evident: any attempt to alter the history of an artifact would require modifying the graph structure, which is detectable through the graph's cryptographic integrity verification.
Query response construction in ORACLE involves explicit Provenance Graph traversal. When the Reflection Layer constructs a Rendered Reality response, it traces the provenance graph from the response claims back to their source receipts, computing the compound Provenance Momentum for each claim. Claims that cannot be traced to a source receipt — orphaned claims — are architecturally prohibited: the Reflection Layer will not generate a claim without a traceable provenance path. This constraint is the architectural expression of the Graceful Degradation principle: when a claim cannot be provenance-grounded, it is not made.
8.3 Implications for HCI Trust
Provenance Physics, when made legible to users through a well-designed interface, creates the foundation for what this paper terms calibrated trust: a relationship between the user and the AI system in which trust is not uniform and unconditional (the naive position toward AI that produces overreliance and consequential error) but appropriately modulated by the evidential quality of specific AI outputs. Calibrated trust requires the user to have access to the evidence underlying AI responses — not buried in a system log, but surfaced in a user-facing form that enables genuine evaluation.
The Trust Calibration Interface proposed in Section 10.3 implements this principle by exposing the Provenance Graph summary for every Rendered Reality response: the source receipts underlying each major claim, the compound Provenance Momentum for each claim, and any provenance collisions that were detected and resolved in the construction of the response. Users with high epistemic engagement (high-EC CWP) may choose to inspect the full provenance graph for a response; users seeking efficiency may rely on the aggregate Rendering Fidelity Score as a single-number summary. In either case, the existence of a legible provenance record transforms the user's epistemic relationship with the AI from faith-based acceptance to evidence-based evaluation — a transformation with significant implications for both the effectiveness of human–AI collaboration and the long-term integrity of AI-supported decision-making.
9. Spatial Cognition Theory in HCI-AI Systems
9.1 Spatial Metaphors and Cognitive Mapping
The application of spatial cognition theory to human–AI interaction rests on a well-established empirical foundation. Tolman's foundational experiments with rodent navigation [18] demonstrated that animals construct internal spatial representations — cognitive maps — of their environments, enabling flexible navigation and goal-directed behavior beyond what stimulus-response learning could support. Subsequent research in cognitive neuroscience has confirmed the existence of dedicated neural machinery for spatial representation (place cells, grid cells) and has revealed extensive crosstalk between spatial navigation systems and systems for abstract reasoning, episodic memory, and conceptual organization [28]. Humans, it appears, think spatially about far more than space.
Lakoff and Johnson's conceptual metaphor theory [17] provides the semiotic bridge between spatial cognition and abstract information engagement. The authors demonstrate, through extensive linguistic analysis, that abstract domains — time, causation, argument, knowledge — are systematically conceptualized through spatial metaphors. We speak of navigating a problem, finding an answer, arriving at a conclusion, covering a topic, moving through an argument. These are not mere figures of speech — they are evidence of a deep cognitive architecture in which spatial reasoning provides the structural scaffold for abstract thought. The implication for HCI is direct: interfaces that exploit spatial structure align with the cognitive architecture of their users, whereas interfaces that ignore spatial structure impose a translation cost on every interaction.
Gibson's affordance theory [16] adds a third dimension: the relevance of action possibilities. An information space is not merely a map to be read — it is an environment to be inhabited, with structures that afford certain actions and not others. A flat list of conversation turns affords scrolling and searching but not navigation, adjacency reasoning, or terrain-based orientation. A spatially organized information space affords these and more, enabling the user to develop a cognitive map of their interaction history that supports faster retrieval, richer contextual integration, and more efficient CWP projection.
9.2 The Spatial Interface Hypothesis
Drawing on these theoretical foundations, this paper proposes the Spatial Interface Hypothesis: that HCI systems for CWP-capable AI should expose the information space as a navigable geography rather than a flat list or conversation thread. Users of CWP-capable systems are, at any moment, situated within a cognitive landscape constituted by their Pattern Buffer history, their Identity Manifold, and their current CWP projection. The interface should reflect this landscape in spatial terms — enabling users to orient themselves, navigate to relevant regions of their history, assess the terrain of their provenance record, and project their trajectory forward.
The Spatial Interface Hypothesis is distinct from the general claim that spatial metaphors are useful in UI design — a claim already well-supported in the existing HCI literature [29]. It makes the specific claim that for CWP-aware AI systems, spatial organization is not merely helpful but necessary for users to effectively govern their continuity record. A user who cannot spatially orient themselves within their CWP history cannot effectively exercise sovereign control over their Identity Manifold, evaluate the provenance terrain of AI responses, or navigate to the specific Pattern Chain relevant to their current cognitive task. The Spatial Interface Hypothesis thus connects the perceptual affordances of spatial design to the governance requirements of the Continuity Architecture.
Recent empirical work on 3D cognitive representations in AI systems [20] provides additional support for the spatial hypothesis, suggesting that geometric organization of knowledge may support more efficient reasoning and retrieval in both human and machine intelligence systems. While the specific empirical basis for the Spatial Interface Hypothesis in the human–AI context requires dedicated user studies (acknowledged as a limitation in Section 11.2), its theoretical grounding in converging lines of cognitive science is robust.

1. Epistemological Foundations
1.1 The Problem of Identity Continuity Across 
Temporal Gaps
The concept of personal identity across time has occupied philosophical
inquiry for centuries. From Locke's memory-based theory of personal identity
to Parfit's reductionist account of psychological continuity, the philosophical
tradition has consistently recognized that identity is not a static,
instantaneous property but a relation that must be maintained, or can be
disrupted, across temporal extension.1 The emergence of AI-mediated legacy
systems introduces a novel instantiation of this problem that classical
philosophy did not and could not anticipate: the construction of a persistent,
queryable representation of a human principal's epistemic and expressive
identity — a representation that must remain faithful to that identity not only
during the principal's active engagement with the system, but across years ofpassive accumulation, model updates, corpus drift, and ultimately the
principal's death.
The fundamental epistemological challenge may be stated precisely. A human
principal P interacts with an AI system over a period of time T, producing a set
of assertions, judgments, and articulated positions that collectively constitute
the principal's epistemic record R(P, T). At any subsequent time t > T, a
querying party — which may be P themselves, an authorized successor, or an
authorized third party — submits a query Q and expects to receive a response
that accurately represents R(P, T). The question is: what architectural and
formal conditions must the system satisfy to provide a verifiable guarantee
that the response it produces accurately and exclusively reflects R(P, T),
rather than an interpolation, confabulation, or adversarially injected
alternative?
This question entails a distinction between two axes of identity continuity that
the philosophical literature designates as synchronic and diachronic identity.
Synchronic identity concerns the coherence of a set of beliefs, assertions, and
positions at a single point in time — the question of whether the system's
representation of P at time t is internally consistent. Diachronic identity
concerns the persistence of that representation across time — the question of
whether the system's representation of P at time t2 faithfully reflects what P
actually asserted or believed at t1 < t2.
2
 Both dimensions are necessary for an
adequate account of identity continuity, and both impose distinct
architectural requirements.
The knowledge provenance problem compounds this challenge. Even if a
system preserves every assertion attributed to P without modification, the
system's ability to correctly identify the source of any given assertion — to
distinguish what P explicitly stated from what the system inferred,
interpolated, or generated in P's voice — constitutes a separate and equally
critical requirement. This is the provenance dimension of identity continuity:not merely that the content is correct, but that the content is correctly
attributed to its originating source, and that source can be cryptographically
verified.
Traditional chain-of-custody models, developed in legal evidentiary and
archival contexts, provide a useful partial analogy but are insufficient for AI
memory systems. Classical chain-of-custody requires that a physical or
documentary artifact be continuously tracked from its point of origin through
each subsequent transfer, with each transfer recorded and authenticated.3 AI
memory systems differ from this model in several critical respects: the
"artifacts" in question are semantic assertions rather than physical objects;
the boundaries between origination, transformation, and inference are not
always discrete; and the systems themselves may modify their internal
representations through processes — such as embedding recomputation or
fine-tuning — that have no classical chain-of-custody analogue.
The three principal schools of epistemological justification —
foundationalism, coherentism, and reliabilism — each offer partial resources
for addressing these challenges, and each maps onto distinct components of
the ACI architecture. Foundationalism, which holds that justified belief must
ultimately rest on a bedrock of non-inferential, directly justified foundational
beliefs, maps onto the requirement for a cryptographically anchored root of
trust: a layer of the architecture whose outputs cannot be derived from, or
contaminated by, any other layer.4 This is the role of SOV1.AI. Coherentism,
which holds that the justification of any belief derives from its coherence with
a broader system of beliefs, maps onto the consistency and completeness
properties of the memory corpus M — the requirement that no two assertions
in M be mutually contradictory, and that the corpus cohere as a unified
representation of P's epistemic state. Reliabilism, particularly process
reliabilism as developed by Goldman, holds that a belief is justified if it is
produced by a reliable cognitive process — one that tends to produce truebeliefs.5
 This maps onto the confidence tiering schema and the DeepCut
gate's requirement that every generated assertion meet a minimum
provenance and reliability threshold before production.
Taken together, these epistemological considerations motivate the core
design principle of the ACI framework: no assertion may enter the system
without a provenance record, no assertion may exit the system without a
confidence attestation, and no generation may proceed without prior
evaluation against the Truthful Recall Condition. Identity continuity, in this
framework, is not a passive property to be assumed but an active invariant to
be enforced.
1.2 Provenance Accounting as Epistemic 
Infrastructure
Provenance, in the general sense, refers to the documented history of the
origin, custody, and transformation of an artifact. The concept is wellestablished in archival science, art authentication, data engineering, and
scientific reproducibility.6 The application of provenance accounting to AI
memory systems represents a significant extension of this concept: rather
than tracking physical artifacts or discrete data records, provenance
accounting in the ACI framework tracks the epistemic lineage of every
semantic assertion in the memory corpus — from the session in which it was
first uttered or approved, through any inferential transformations, to its
current representation in the corpus.
Definition 1.1 — Provenance Record
A provenance record π(a) for assertion a is a tuple <s, P, t, κ> where s
denotes the originating session identifier (cryptographically
authenticated), P denotes the principal identity token issued by SOV1.AI,
t denotes the UTC timestamp of origination, and κ denotes the confidencetier assigned to a at intake (κ ∈ {κ1, κ2, κ3}). A provenance record is said
to be complete if all four fields are populated and cryptographically
verifiable; partial if one or more fields are missing or unverifiable; and
absent if no provenance record exists for a.
The requirement that every assertion in the system carry a complete
provenance record is a non-negotiable architectural constraint of the ACI
framework. This requirement is motivated by a concept introduced in this
monograph as epistemic debt — a measure of the aggregate verification risk
that accumulates in a memory corpus as a function of unverified, lowconfidence, or shallow-provenance assertions.
Definition 1.2 — Epistemic Debt
The epistemic debt of a memory corpus M, denoted E(M), is defined as
the weighted sum of verification risk across all assertions a ∈ M.
Assertions with low confidence values and shallow provenance depth
contribute maximum epistemic debt. The formal definition is provided in
Section 3.3. Informally, E(M) represents the degree to which the corpus
M contains assertions whose truthfulness, attribution, or reliability
cannot be fully verified — and which therefore constitute latent risk for
any response generated from M.
The concept of epistemic debt is operationally significant for two reasons.
First, it provides a scalar measure of the overall epistemic health of the
corpus that can be monitored over time and used to trigger remediation
actions — such as provenance enrichment campaigns or corpus audit
procedures — when the debt trajectory becomes adverse. Second, it
establishes a formal relationship between corpus quality and responsequality: as epistemic debt increases, the probability that any given response
satisfies the Truthful Recall Condition decreases monotonically. This
relationship is formalized in Section 3.3 and provides the mathematical basis
for the DeepCut gate's pre-generation evaluation logic.
The Truthful Recall Condition (TRC) is introduced here at the conceptual level
and formalized mathematically in Section 3.2. The TRC is a necessary
condition on every response produced by the system: a response R satisfies
TRC if and only if every atomic assertion within R is traceable to a verified
provenance record, meets a minimum confidence threshold, is consistent
with all higher-confidence assertions in M on the same subject, and does not
introduce claims that are absent from the provenance-anchored corpus
without explicit flagging as inference or synthesis.
Definition 1.3 — Truthful Recall Condition (Informal)
A response R satisfies the Truthful Recall Condition (TRC) if and only
if: (i) every atomic assertion in R is provenance-verified; (ii) every atomic
assertion in R meets the applicable confidence floor; (iii) no atomic
assertion in R contradicts a higher-confidence assertion on the same
subject in M; and (iv) no atomic assertion absent from the provenanceanchored corpus is introduced without explicit epistemic flagging. The
formal mathematical statement is given in Section 3.2.
Provenance accounting as epistemic infrastructure thus encompasses three
distinct functions. The intake function ensures that every assertion entering
M is immediately assigned a complete provenance record, with confidence
tier determined by the intake classification rules (Section 3.4). The
maintenance function ensures that provenance records are preserved
without modification through corpus operations such as embeddingrecomputation, index rebuilding, and corpus reorganization. The retrieval
function ensures that every assertion returned by the retrieval subsystem
carries its provenance record as an inseparable component — provenance is
not stored separately from content but is architecturally coupled to it, such
that content without provenance cannot be retrieved.
1.3 Why Traditional AI Memory Systems Fail
The failure modes of traditional AI memory systems — including vector
database retrieval systems, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipelines,
and fine-tuned personal models — are both numerous and well-documented in
the research literature. This section provides a systematic analysis of those
failure modes as they pertain to the identity continuity problem, establishing
the negative case that motivates the ACI architecture.
Vector database retrieval without provenance. The dominant paradigm
for AI memory systems as of the mid-2020s relies on dense vector
representations of text, stored in specialized databases and retrieved via
approximate nearest-neighbor search. This architecture has significant
operational advantages — high recall speed, scalability, and compatibility
with large language model input formats. However, it has a fundamental
epistemic deficiency: the vector representation of an assertion encodes
semantic content but discards provenance. When a vector is retrieved, the
retrieval result carries no information about who originally made the
assertion, when it was made, or in what context it was approved. The
retrieved content is semantically plausible but epistemically anonymous.7 In
the context of an AI legacy system, this means that a retrieved assertion
attributed to the principal cannot be distinguished, at the retrieval layer, from
an assertion that was injected into the corpus by an adversary, interpolated
by the model during a previous generation, or misattributed from another
source.Embedding drift over model updates. Vector embeddings are not static
representations; they are computed by embedding models that themselves
evolve through training and fine-tuning. When the underlying embedding
model is updated, the semantic geometry of the vector space changes, such
that existing vectors may no longer accurately represent the semantic
content of the assertions they encode. This phenomenon — embedding drift —
means that a corpus whose vectors were computed under model version Vn
may produce semantically distorted retrieval results under model version
Vn+1, even if the underlying text content is unchanged.8 For an AI legacy
system, embedding drift represents a form of involuntary corpus modification
that can distort retrieval without leaving any detectable trace in the content
layer.
Absence of session-scoped identity boundaries. Traditional AI memory
systems do not maintain session-scoped boundaries around the assertions
they store. Assertions from different sessions, different time periods, and
potentially different principals are stored in a single undifferentiated corpus.
This makes it impossible to distinguish between assertions that represent P's
current, reflective position and assertions that represent an earlier, perhaps
superseded, view — a distinction that is epistemologically critical for any
system claiming to faithfully represent the principal's authentic record.
Susceptibility to context-window injection. Retrieval-augmented
generation systems are vulnerable to a class of attack in which adversarial
content injected into retrieved documents influences the model's generated
output.9 In the context of an AI legacy system, this attack vector allows an
adversary who can influence the corpus — for example, by inserting a
document that is retrieved in response to a target query — to cause the
system to generate responses that do not reflect the principal's authentic
record. Without a pre-generation gate that evaluates responses against averified provenance corpus before output, there is no mechanism to detect or
block such injections.
Absence of posthumous governance primitives. No existing commercial
AI memory architecture provides any mechanism for governance after the
principal's death. There is no posthumous mode, no mechanism for the
principal to pre-register access restrictions or delegation authorities, and no
cryptographic shredding capability. This represents a complete failure of the
architecture to address the lifecycle of a legacy system — a failure that
becomes increasingly significant as AI-mediated personal data stores grow in
scope and depth.
These five failure modes are not peripheral concerns; they represent
fundamental architectural deficiencies that cannot be remediated by
incremental improvements to existing designs. The ACI framework addresses
each of them through structural means: SOV1.AI eliminates session-scoped
identity confusion and provides posthumous governance; the provenance
accounting system eliminates anonymous retrieval; the DeepCut gate
eliminates context-window injection vulnerability; and the confidence tiering
schema provides a formal mechanism for managing embedding drift and
assertion supersession.
2. Three-Tier Architecture

which each tier operates at a distinct trust level, temporal scope, and data
sensitivity class. The tiered structure is not merely an organizational
convenience; it encodes a set of formal separation-of-concerns principles thatare essential to the security and epistemic integrity of the system. No tier may
assume the functions of another tier, and no tier may communicate with a
non-adjacent tier except through the formally specified inter-tier
communication protocol described in Section 2.5.

generation in a single architectural layer cannot provide verifiable
guarantees about any of the three functions, because the outputs of each
function influence and contaminate the others. By separating identity
management (Tier 1), inference and routing (Tier 2), and generation (Tier 3)
into architecturally distinct layers with formally specified interfaces and trust
constraints, the ACI framework provides the structural foundation for the
verifiable guarantees that the TRC requires.

2.2 Tier 1 — SOV1.AI: The Sovereign Identity Layer
SOV1.AI is the root-of-trust for the entire ACI framework. Its fundamental
purpose is to maintain an immutable, cryptographically authenticated record
of the principal's verified identity and to issue identity tokens and
authorization signals that govern all downstream system operations. SOV1.AI
does not store content; it does not perform inference; it does not generate
text. Its sole domain is identity, authorization, and key management.
Components. The SOV1.AI layer comprises four primary components:
1. Identity Attestation Engine. Maintains the principal's 
cryptographic identity credential, anchored to a biometric, 
passphrase, or hardware token anchor established at enrollment. The 
identity credential is used to authenticate the principal in every 
session and to sign all session records. The attestation engine operates under a zero-trust model: it authenticates each session 
independently, without assuming the validity of any prior session 
authentication.
2. Root Session Registry. An append-only, cryptographically chained 
record of all authenticated sessions, including session start and end 
timestamps, the principal's identity token used for that session, and a 
hash of the session's provenance record set. The root session registry 
is the authoritative source of truth for all session-scoped provenance 
inquiries.
3. Legal Instrument Registry. A pre-registered, cryptographically 
signed data structure specifying the principal's posthumous 
governance preferences, including authorized successors, access tier 
assignments, time-locked release conditions, and destruction 
conditions. The legal instrument registry is the only mechanism by 
which posthumous authority can be delegated (see Section 5.2 for full 
specification).
4. Key Management Service. Manages all cryptographic key material 
used by SOV1.AI, including the signing keys used to authenticate 
inter-tier tokens, the encryption keys used to protect the legal 
instrument registry, and the shredding keys whose deletion triggers 
cryptographic destruction of the corpus (see Section 5.5).
Trust model. SOV1.AI is designed to operate with zero external
dependencies. Its key material must be stored in hardware security modules
(HSMs) or equivalent air-gapped hardware, inaccessible to networkconnected components. SOV1.AI's signing authority cannot be delegated to
any software component, and its key material cannot be exported. This design
ensures that even a complete compromise of Tiers 2 and 3 does not grant an
adversary the ability to create fraudulent identity tokens or to modify the
principal's provenance record.Interfaces. SOV1.AI provides two interface surfaces: an outbound interface
that issues signed identity tokens and authorization signals to Oracle.AI; and
an inbound interface that accepts pre-registered legal instruments from the
principal during the enrollment phase, and posthumous activation signals
(cryptographically signed death certificate attestations or equivalent) from
registered successors. SOV1.AI does not accept inbound signals from
Legacy.GI under any circumstances.
Formal Properties of SOV1.AI
Tamper-Evidence: Any modification to the root session registry or legal
instrument registry produces a cryptographic hash mismatch detectable
by any party holding the public verification key. The append-only
structure ensures that deletions are detectable as well as modifications.
Non-Repudiation: Every signed token issued by SOV1.AI carries a
timestamp and a session reference that binds the token to a specific,
verifiable principal authentication event. No token can be credibly
attributed to a different principal or session without falsifying the
cryptographic signature.
Temporal Anchoring: All session records and token issuance events are
timestamped using a trusted time source (RFC 3161 compliant timestamp
authority or equivalent). Temporal anchoring ensures that the
chronological ordering of all system events is verifiable and cannot be
retroactively altered.
Failure modes and mitigations. Two primary failure modes are identified
for SOV1.AI and require explicit protocol specification.
Key compromise recovery. If the principal's signing key material is
compromised, a key compromise recovery protocol is initiated. This protocolrequires the principal to re-authenticate using a pre-registered backup
authentication channel — a secondary hardware token or a split-knowledge
passphrase held by two registered trustees. Upon successful reauthentication, new key material is generated, and the root session registry
entry corresponding to the compromise event is cryptographically flagged.
All tokens issued under the compromised key are revoked and logged.
Historical provenance records signed under the compromised key are reevaluated by a designated audit principal before being re-admitted to the
verified provenance class.
Principal incapacitation protocol. If the principal becomes incapacitated but
has not yet died — a state in which posthumous mode activation is not yet
appropriate, but the principal can no longer actively manage the system — a
designated guardian, pre-registered in the legal instrument registry, may
issue a temporary incapacitation flag to SOV1.AI. This flag causes Oracle.AI
to switch to a restricted routing mode in which no new corpus writes are
permitted, but read and generative operations continue under the preexisting authorization framework. The incapacitation flag may be lifted by the
principal upon recovery or converted to posthumous mode activation upon
the principal's death.
2.3 Tier 2 — Oracle.AI: The Inference and Routing 
Engine
Oracle.AI is the architectural mediator of the ACI framework. Its purpose is to
enforce the epistemic and authorization policies of the system between the
sovereign identity layer and the generative surface. Oracle.AI receives
queries from external parties, validates their authorization using tokens
issued by SOV1.AI, evaluates each query against the DeepCut pre-generation
gate and applicable domain firewall policies, orchestrates retrieval from the
provenance-tagged corpus, and either routes verified queries to Legacy.GI or
returns structured refusals with documented rationale.Components. Oracle.AI comprises five primary components:
5. Query Classifier. Receives incoming queries and classifies them by 
domain, intent type, and confidence tier requirement. The domain 
classification determines which domain firewall policies apply. The 
intent classification distinguishes between retrieval queries (seeking 
specific previously-asserted content), inference queries (seeking 
derivations from the corpus), and synthesis queries (seeking novel 
content in the principal's voice). Each intent type has a distinct 
routing path and confidence floor requirement.
6. Domain Firewall Enforcer. Applies the domain-specific access 
control policies defined in the domain firewall registry. Each firewall 
specifies the authorized confidence tiers, the authorized principals, 
and the posthumous access rules applicable to queries in that domain.
The domain firewall enforcer is a mandatory waypoint on every query 
path; it cannot be bypassed by any component.
7. Retrieval Orchestrator. Manages retrieval from the provenancetagged corpus, ensuring that every retrieved assertion carries its 
complete provenance record. The retrieval orchestrator does not 
return anonymized assertions; provenance is architecturally coupled 
to content at the retrieval layer.
8. Confidence Scorer. Evaluates the confidence tier of each retrieved 
assertion and the aggregate confidence profile of the candidate 
response set. Determines whether the candidate response set meets 
the applicable confidence floor requirement before routing to 
Legacy.GI.
9. DeepCut Pre-Generation Gate. The mandatory interception 
mechanism described in full in Section 4. Evaluates the candidate 
response set against the TRC before generation proceeds. The gate's four decision branches — GENERATE, ENRICH, REVIEW, REFUSE — 
determine the disposition of every query.
Trust model. Oracle.AI operates under delegated authority from SOV1.AI. It
accepts identity tokens and authorization signals from SOV1.AI and routes
queries accordingly. Oracle.AI cannot self-elevate its permissions — it cannot
grant itself access to domains for which it has not received an authorization
signal from SOV1.AI, and it cannot reclassify assertions from a lower
confidence tier to a higher one without an explicit authorization signal. All
routing decisions made by Oracle.AI are logged to the cryptographically
chained audit log (Section 6.5).
Formal Properties of Oracle.AI
Determinism Under Identical Inputs: Given identical inputs — the
same query, the same corpus state, the same authorization tokens, and
the same firewall configuration — Oracle.AI must produce identical
routing decisions. Non-determinism in routing decisions would make the
system unauditable.
Auditability: Every routing decision, gate decision, and firewall
evaluation made by Oracle.AI is written to the append-only audit log
before the decision is acted upon. An auditor with access to the audit log
can reconstruct the exact sequence of Oracle.AI decisions for any session.
No Persistent Self-Modification: Oracle.AI does not update its own
routing rules, firewall configurations, or confidence scoring parameters
based on session inputs. All configuration changes must be explicitly
authorized by SOV1.AI and applied through a formally specified
configuration update protocol.Inter-tier positioning. Oracle.AI occupies the critical middle position in the
trust hierarchy, and this position entails a specific set of constraints that are
worth emphasizing. Oracle.AI is simultaneously a consumer of SOV1.AI's
authority (it receives and validates tokens issued by Tier 1) and a producer of
authority for Legacy.GI (it issues routing directives and verified query
payloads to Tier 3). This dual role means that any compromise of Oracle.AI
represents a greater systemic risk than a compromise of Legacy.GI, because
it affects both the authority consumption and authority production functions
of the system. The formal properties of determinism, auditability, and
prohibition of self-modification are therefore the most critical operational

2.4 Tier 3 — Legacy.GI: The Generative Intelligence
Surface
Legacy.GI is the user-facing generative layer of the ACI framework. Its
purpose is to produce outputs — responses to queries, syntheses of corpus
content, extensions of the principal's articulated positions — in the voice,
style, and epistemic framework of the principal P. Legacy.GI is where the
accumulated knowledge and expressive identity of the principal is made
accessible to authorized querying parties. It is also the component of the
architecture most visible to end users, and therefore the component most
subject to attempted manipulation or misuse.
The defining architectural constraint of Legacy.GI is the strict separation
between generation and authority. Legacy.GI generates — it does not
retrieve, decide, or self-authorize. All retrieval is performed by Oracle.AI's
retrieval orchestrator. All authorization decisions are made by Oracle.AI's
DeepCut gate and domain firewall enforcer, under authority delegated from
SOV1.AI. Legacy.GI receives from Oracle.AI a verified, provenance-tagged
query payload specifying: the content to be generated, the confidence floor
applicable to the response, the style and voice parameters, and anyposthumous mode restrictions. Legacy.GI's generative activity is constrained
to producing output that is consistent with that payload. It cannot initiate
outbound requests, cannot access the corpus directly, and cannot modify its
own model weights without explicit authorization from SOV1.AI.
Components. Legacy.GI comprises three primary components:
10.Fine-Tuned Generative Model. A language model fine-tuned on the 
principal's authenticated corpus content to replicate the principal's 
voice, vocabulary, reasoning patterns, and expressive style. Finetuning is performed under controlled conditions, with model weights 
cryptographically hashed before and after each fine-tuning event and 
the hash record stored in the root session registry. Weight 
modification outside of this formal protocol is blocked by Oracle.AI's 
authorization enforcement.
11.Provenance-Tagged Output Renderer. Processes the generative 
model's output to attach provenance metadata to each assertion in the
response, cross-referencing the provenance records of the corpus 
content that contributed to each assertion. The renderer also enforces
the epistemic flagging requirement for κ3 (contextual) assertions that 
appear in the output without direct corpus attestation.
12.Posthumous Mode Controller. Monitors the posthumous mode flag 
issued by SOV1.AI and enforces all posthumous mode restrictions, 
including the prohibition on new corpus writes, the read-only 
generative mode constraint, and the mandatory watermarking of 
posthumous outputs.
Formal Properties of Legacy.GI
Output Traceability: Every assertion in every response produced byLegacy.GI must carry a provenance trace referencing the corpus content
that contributed to it. Assertions without provenance traces are blocked
by the provenance-tagged output renderer before delivery.
Style Fidelity Bounds: The fine-tuned generative model is evaluated
against style fidelity metrics at regular intervals. If style fidelity falls
below a defined threshold — indicating that the model's voice
representation of the principal has degraded — the system issues an alert
and suspends generative operations pending re-evaluation.
Posthumous Lockdown Mode: Upon receipt of a posthumous mode flag
from SOV1.AI, Legacy.GI immediately transitions to read-only generative
mode. In this mode, no new content may be added to the principal's
corpus, no model weights may be modified, and all outputs are
watermarked. The transition is irreversible except by an explicit reversal
signal from SOV1.AI, which requires a matching reversal condition in the
legal instrument registry.

3.2 The Truthful Recall Condition (TRC)
Proposition 3.1 — The Truthful Recall ConditionA response R = {a1, a2, ..., an} to query Q satisfies the Truthful Recall
Condition TRC(R, M) = TRUE if and only if all four of the following
conditions hold simultaneously:
1. [Provenance Verification] For all ai ∈ R: π(ai) ∈ Πverified
2. [Confidence Floor] For all ai ∈ R: κ(ai) ≥ κfloor
3. [Consistency with Corpus] For all ai ∈ R: there does not exist aj ∈
M such that aj contradicts ai and κ(aj) > κ(ai)
4. [Corpus Containment] For all ai ∈ R: either ai ∈ M (with 
provenance verified) or ai is explicitly flagged in R as inference or 
synthesis, not as principal attestation
TRC(R, M) = FALSE if any of the four conditions fails for any ai ∈ R.
(3.1) TRC(R, M) = ∀ ai ∈ R: [π(ai) ∈ Πverified] ∧ [κ(ai) ≥ κfloor] ∧ [¬∃ aj ∈ M: contradicts(aj, ai) ∧
κ(aj) > κ(ai)] ∧ [ai ∈ M ∨ flagged(ai)]
Theorem 3.1 — TRC Precludes Undetected Confabulation
Statement: A system that enforces TRC on all generated responses
cannot produce undetected confabulation.
Proof Sketch: Let R be a response containing a confabulated assertion
ac — that is, an assertion that was not uttered or approved by P in any
authenticated session, and that does not follow by verified logical
inference from assertions in M. By condition (i) of TRC, π(ac) must belong
to Πverified; but since ac was not uttered in an authenticated session, no
provenance record exists for ac in the root session registry, and therefore
π(ac) ∉ Πverified. This violates condition (i), and TRC(R, M) = FALSE. Bycondition (iv), if ac is an inference not present in M, it must be explicitly
flagged as such; a flagged assertion is by definition not presented as
principal attestation, and therefore does not constitute undetected
confabulation. In either case, TRC enforcement prevents ac from
appearing in R as an undetected confabulation. It follows that a system
enforcing TRC on all responses cannot produce undetected
confabulation. □
The Acceptable Recall Set ARS(Q) for a query Q is defined as the set of all
responses R for which TRC(R, M) = TRUE. The DeepCut gate's function,
described in Section 4, is to determine whether ARS(Q) is non-empty before
routing Q to Legacy.GI — and to act appropriately if it is empty.
3.3 Provenance Depth and Epistemic Debt
Definition 3.1 — Provenance Depth
The provenance depth d(a) of assertion a ∈ M is defined as the number
of verified citation hops from a back to its primary session record in the
root session registry. For a direct session assertion (a ∈ κ1, uttered
directly by P in an authenticated session), d(a) = 1. For an inferred
assertion (κ2), d(a) = 1 + max{d(aj)} where the maximum is taken over all
κ1 or κ2 assertions from which a was inferred. For a contextual assertion
(κ3), d(a) may be undefined or set to a sentinel value dmax + 1 to indicate
maximum epistemic distance from the root.
Definition 3.2 — Epistemic Debt FunctionThe epistemic debt of corpus M is defined as:
(3.2) E(M) = ∑a ∈ M [w(κ(a)) × (1 / d(a))]
where w(κ(a)) is a weighting function over confidence tiers: w(κ1) = 0
(verified assertions contribute zero epistemic debt), w(κ2) = 0.5 (inferred
assertions contribute proportional debt), w(κ3) = 1.0 (contextual
assertions contribute maximum debt scaled by inverse provenance
depth). Assertions with absent provenance records are assigned d(a) = 0
and w(κ) = 1, contributing infinite epistemic debt and triggering
immediate corpus quarantine.
Proposition 3.2 — Epistemic Debt and TRC Satisfaction
Statement: As E(M) increases monotonically, the fraction of queries Q
for which ARS(Q) is non-empty decreases monotonically.
(3.3) dE(M)/dt ≥ 0 ⇒ d|ARS(Q)|/dt ≤ 0 for the expected Q distribution
Intuition: As the corpus accumulates low-confidence, low-provenancedepth assertions (increasing E(M)), the probability that an arbitrary
query Q can be answered with a TRC-satisfying response decreases,
because a larger fraction of potentially relevant assertions fail conditions
(i) or (ii) of Proposition 3.1. Formally, if assertions in the relevant retrieval
set R* have π(a) ∉ Πverified or κ(a) < κfloor, then TRC(R*, M) = FALSE, and no
R* ∈ ARS(Q) can be formed. As E(M) grows, this failure case becomes
increasingly likely across the query distribution.
The epistemic debt function provides the operational basis for corpus health
monitoring. The runtime verification subsystem (Section 6) tracks E(M) at
regular intervals and triggers alerts when the epistemic debt trajectorybecomes adverse — specifically when E(M) exceeds a pre-defined threshold
Emax or when the rate of increase dE(M)/dt exceeds a pre-defined threshold for
two consecutive measurement intervals.

Theorem 3.2 — Confidence Floor Theorem
Statement: Legacy.GI must not present a κ3 assertion as a κ1 assertion
without explicit inter-tier authorization from Oracle.AI.
Proof Sketch: A κ3 assertion a has, by definition, no verified provenance
record in Πverified. If a is presented as κ1, then condition (i) of TRC requires
π(a) ∈ Πverified; but since π(a) ∉ Πverified for any κ3 assertion, TRC(R, M) =
FALSE for any response R containing such a misrepresentation. The
Confidence Floor Theorem therefore follows directly from the TRC: any
system that presents a κ3 assertion as κ1 without authorization
necessarily violates TRC. The explicit inter-tier authorization mechanism
— by which Oracle.AI, under exceptional circumstances and with full
audit logging, may authorize a confidence upgrade with documented
rationale — is the only compliant pathway for such a presentation. □
3.5 Formal Completeness and Consistency 
Properties
Definition 3.3 — Memory Corpus Completeness
The completeness of memory corpus M relative to domain D, denoted
C(M, D), is defined as the fraction of ground-truth domain assertions in Dthat have at least one κ1 or κ2 entry in M:
(3.4) C(M, D) = |{a ∈ D : ∃ a' ∈ M, a' represents a, κ(a') ∈ {κ1, κ2}}| / |D|
C(M, D) ∈ [0, 1]. A corpus with C(M, D) = 1 has at least one verified or
inferred entry for every ground-truth assertion in D. In practice, C(M, D)
= 1 is unachievable for open-ended domains; the relevant operational
target is the maximization of C(M, D) within the enrollable scope of P's
expressed positions.
Definition 3.4 — Memory Corpus Consistency
The consistency of memory corpus M, denoted Ψ(M), is defined as a
boolean property: Ψ(M) = TRUE if and only if there does not exist any pair
of κ1 assertions (ai, aj) in M such that ai and aj are logically contradictory.
Formally:
(3.5) Ψ(M) = TRUE ⇔ ¬∃ (ai, aj) ∈ M×M: contradicts(ai, aj) ∧ κ(ai) = κ(aj) = κ1
Theorem 3.3 — Completeness–Consistency Trade-Off
Statement: A system cannot simultaneously guarantee Ψ(M) = TRUE
and maximize C(M, D) for an open-ended domain D without a conflictresolution protocol.
Proof Sketch: For any sufficiently rich domain D, it is possible that P,
over an extended interaction history, uttered two κ1 assertions ai and aj
such that contradicts(ai, aj) = TRUE — for example, a position held at time
t1 that was revised at t2 > t1. If both are included in M to maximize C(M,
D), Ψ(M) = FALSE. If one is excluded to preserve Ψ(M), C(M, D)decreases. Therefore, maximizing C(M, D) and guaranteeing Ψ(M) are in
general incompatible without additional resolution machinery. □
Conflict Resolution Ordering: When two κ1 assertions conflict, the
following ordering applies: (1) the assertion with the later timestamp t is
preferred, on the grounds that P's more recent position supersedes an
earlier one; (2) if timestamps are identical (concurrent sessions), the
assertion from the higher-privilege session context (as defined in the
legal instrument registry) is preferred; (3) if neither criterion resolves the
conflict, both assertions are flagged as conflicting κ1 records, the
DeepCut gate returns REFUSE for queries whose response depends on
resolution of the conflict, and a conflict resolution task is logged for
human-in-the-loop review by the designated audit principal.
4. The DeepCut Pre-Generation Gate 
and Domain Firewalls
4.1 The DeepCut Gate: Rationale and Architecture
The DeepCut pre-generation gate is the operational mechanism by which the
Truthful Recall Condition is enforced at the system boundary between query
intake and response generation. The gate is a mandatory interception point
implemented within Oracle.AI (Tier 2) at the junction of the retrieval
orchestration and generation routing functions. No query may proceed to
Legacy.GI without first passing through the DeepCut gate. This constraint is
architecturally enforced: Legacy.GI has no inbound communication path that
bypasses Oracle.AI, and Oracle.AI's gate logic cannot be suspended orbypassed by any component — including SOV1.AI, which may only interact
with the gate through the formally specified configuration update protocol.
The rationale for the gate's placement at the pre-generation phase — rather
than at the post-generation, output-filtering phase — is fundamental to the
architecture's epistemic integrity. Post-generation filtering, the approach
taken by most contemporary AI safety systems, operates on outputs that have
already been produced by the generative model. This approach is vulnerable
to a class of attack in which the model's generation process itself is influenced
by adversarial inputs in ways that are not detectable in the output without
reference to the provenance corpus. Pre-generation filtering, by contrast,
evaluates the query and the candidate response set against the provenance
corpus before any generation occurs. If the evaluation determines that a
truthful, provenance-verified response cannot be produced, no generation is
triggered — the model is never presented with an adversarially problematic
generation task.
The gate asks, for every incoming query Q, a set of four evaluative questions
corresponding to the four conditions of the TRC:
13.Can a response to Q be assembled from assertions in M all of which 
carry complete provenance records π(a) ∈ Πverified?
14.Do all assertions in the candidate response set satisfy the confidence 
floor requirement κ(a) ≥ κfloor specified in the inter-tier message 
envelope?
15.Does M contain any κ1 assertion that would contradict any assertion in
the candidate response set?
16.Does the candidate response set introduce any assertion absent from 
the provenance-anchored corpus without flagging it as inference or 
synthesis?Based on the answers to these questions, the gate selects one of four decision
branches described in Section 4.2.

5. Legal and Posthumous Governance
5.1 The Posthumous Problem in AI Legacy Systems
The governance of digital identity and personal data after the death of a
human principal is among the most complex and legally unsettled challenges
in contemporary information technology. Classical estate law developed over
centuries to address the disposition of physical property and, subsequently,
financial assets and documentary records. Digital assets — including
personal data, online accounts, and digital communications — were only
addressed in U.S. law through the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access toDigital Assets Act (RUFADAA), first approved in 2015 and subsequently
adopted with variations by the majority of U.S. states.10 The European
regulatory framework, anchored by the General Data Protection Regulation
(GDPR), addresses posthumous data primarily through Article 17 (right to
erasure) and national implementation provisions, which vary significantly
across member states.11
AI legacy systems present a challenge that is qualitatively distinct from the
management of static digital assets. A static digital asset — an email archive,
a photograph collection, a document store — is a fixed artifact whose content
does not change after the principal's death. An AI legacy system, by contrast,
is a living representation: it can produce new outputs, synthesize novel
responses, and represent the principal's voice in real time to querying
parties, including those who may never have known the principal in life. The
posthumous governance challenge is therefore not merely about access
control to a static artifact, but about the ongoing governance of a dynamic
generative system that continues to speak, in some sense, with the principal's
voice.
The ACI framework addresses this challenge through three interconnected
mechanisms: the pre-registration of legal instruments (Section 5.2), the
posthumous mode protocol (Section 5.3), and the destruction and sunset
protocols (Section 5.5). The overarching principle is that the principal's
governance preferences, expressed during life, are the exclusive source of
authority for posthumous system operation. No successor, heir, or third party
may expand the principal's posthumous corpus, modify the system's voice
model, or alter the governance parameters set by the principal — except to
the extent explicitly authorized in the pre-registered legal instruments.
When the principal P is deceased or permanently incapacitated, the system
enters a fundamentally different authority model. The principal can no longer
provide new attestations; no new κ1 assertions can ever be added to M.Legacy.GI becomes, in the most precise sense, a read-only representation of a
closed corpus: a system that can generate novel outputs in P's voice, but
cannot update the underlying factual and epistemic record that those outputs
draw upon. This transition has profound implications for the TRC: in
posthumous mode, condition (i) of the TRC is automatically satisfied by the
frozen corpus (no new unverified assertions can enter), but the risk of stale
provenance — assertions whose real-world referents have changed since the
corpus was frozen — must be disclosed to querying parties through
appropriate output watermarking.
5.2 Pre-Registration of Legal Instruments
The legal instrument registry is a cryptographically signed data structure
that the principal pre-registers with SOV1.AI during their active engagement
with the system. It is the only mechanism by which posthumous authority
over the system can be delegated. The registry must be established during
the principal's authenticated sessions — it cannot be created or modified
after posthumous mode activation — and each modification to the registry
must be signed by the principal's current identity credential.
The legal instrument registry comprises the following fields (full schema in
Appendix C):
● Authorized Successors. A list of identified parties (natural persons 
or legal entities) who are authorized to receive posthumous access to 
the system, each identified by a pre-registered cryptographic identity 
credential. Successors are organized into classes (Class 1, Class 2, 
etc.) with distinct access privileges.
● Access Tier Assignments. For each successor class, the set of 
domains and confidence tiers accessible posthumously. These 
assignments are additive constraints on top of the existing domain 
firewall configurations — a successor class cannot have access that exceeds the corresponding domain firewall's posthumous_access_rule 
setting.
● Time-Locked Release Conditions. Optional conditions specifying 
that certain domains or corpus content become accessible (or are 
destroyed) after a specified date or upon occurrence of a specified 
triggering event (e.g., five years after the principal's death).
● Domain-Specific Access Expirations. For each domain, an optional 
expiration date after which successor access to that domain is 
revoked, regardless of successor class assignment.
● Destruction Conditions. Conditions under which the principal's 
corpus and all associated cryptographic key material should be 
destroyed (see Section 5.5).
● Registry Trustee. A designated human trustee who holds a cosignature right over critical registry modifications and who is notified 
upon posthumous mode activation. The registry trustee has no 
unilateral authority over the system; their role is verification and 
notification, not governance.
The legal instrument registry is stored in SOV1.AI's hardware-secured
environment, encrypted under the principal's key material. It is not
accessible to Oracle.AI or Legacy.GI during the principal's lifetime. Upon
posthumous mode activation, SOV1.AI decrypts the registry and makes its
contents available to Oracle.AI through a formally specified posthumous
configuration update, signed by the posthumous mode token issued under the
triggering death attestation.
5.3 Posthumous Mode Protocol
Posthumous mode is a formally specified operational state of the ACI system
that is activated upon the principal's death and that fundamentally alters thesystem's authorization model, corpus modification rules, and output
behavior.
Activation Mechanism. Posthumous mode is activated by the submission to
SOV1.AI of a posthumous activation signal comprising: (a) a
cryptographically certified copy of the principal's death certificate or courtorder equivalent; (b) the submitting party's identity credential, which must
match one of the registered successors in the legal instrument registry; and
(c) the submitting party's co-signature over the activation signal. SOV1.AI
validates all three components before issuing the posthumous mode token.
System Transition Sequence. Upon issuing the posthumous mode token,
SOV1.AI initiates the following transition sequence:
17.SOV1.AI issues the posthumous mode token to Oracle.AI. The token 
carries the contents of the legal instrument registry as a signed 
payload, specifying the authorized successor classes and access tier 
assignments.
18.Oracle.AI receives the posthumous mode token and transitions to 
posthumous routing logic. In posthumous routing logic, all queries 
that would add new κ1 assertions to M are rejected by the DeepCut 
gate with decision REFUSE(reason = 
"posthumous_mode_write_prohibition"). Existing routing logic for read
and generative operations continues, subject to the successor-class 
access tier assignments from the legal instrument registry.
19.Legacy.GI receives a posthumous_flag = TRUE in the next inter-tier 
message envelope and transitions to posthumous lockdown mode. In 
this mode, no corpus writes are permitted, no model weight 
modifications are permitted, and all outputs are watermarked with a 
posthumous mode indicator in both output metadata and, at Oracle.AI's discretion and the legal instrument registry's specification,
optionally visible to the querying party in the response text.
20.The registry trustee is notified of posthumous mode activation 
through a pre-registered notification channel.
21.All three transition events are written to the audit log as a 
coordinated posthumous mode activation event, with the death 
attestation hash and the issuing successor's identity token recorded 
as the trigger.
5.4 Jurisdictional and Regulatory Considerations
The legal landscape governing posthumous digital asset access and AIgenerated representations of deceased persons is, as of September 2026,
fragmented across jurisdictions and deeply inadequate to the technical
realities of AI legacy systems. This section surveys the most directly
applicable regulatory frameworks and identifies the key points of interface
between those frameworks and the ACI architecture.
United States — RUFADAA. The Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to
Digital Assets Act, promulgated by the Uniform Law Commission in 2015,
provides that a fiduciary (executor, trustee, or agent under a power of
attorney) may access a decedent's digital assets, subject to the terms of
service of the custodian and any contrary direction from the decedent's estate
plan.12 RUFADAA establishes a priority ordering: online tool designations
(such as the principal's pre-registered legal instrument registry) take
precedence over estate planning documents, which take precedence over
terms of service. This priority ordering is directly compatible with the ACI
framework's pre-registration model, in which the principal's registry
constitutes an authoritative online tool designation with the highest priority.European Union — GDPR. Article 17 of the GDPR confers on data subjects
the right to erasure of their personal data. The GDPR's primary protections
apply to living natural persons, and the Regulation's application to deceased
persons is explicitly left to member state law.13 This creates significant
jurisdictional variation: some member states (e.g., France under Article 85 of
the Loi Informatique et Libertés as amended) provide robust posthumous
data rights, while others provide none. The ACI framework's destruction and
sunset protocols (Section 5.5) are designed to enable compliance with the
most stringent member state requirements — specifically, the ability to
execute complete cryptographic destruction of the corpus on demand from an
authorized successor.
AI-Specific Legislation. No current jurisdiction has comprehensively
legislated the governance of AI legacy systems as a distinct category. The EU
AI Act (in force as of 2024) addresses AI system requirements for
transparency, documentation, and conformity assessment, but does not
address the specific lifecycle, posthumous governance, or identity
preservation requirements of legacy systems. The ACI framework is designed
to be regulation-ready: its audit trail, provenance accounting, and legal
instrument registry provide the documentation infrastructure that future AI
legacy system regulations are likely to require. The framework does not wait
for legislation but establishes technical standards in advance of it.
Voice and Likeness Rights. Several U.S. states, most notably California
under the Protecting Individuals from Synthetic Media Act (AB 602, 2023)
and related legislation, have enacted protections against unauthorized AIgenerated representations of deceased persons' voices and likenesses.14 The
ACI framework's posthumous lockdown mode and successor-class
authorization mechanism provide a technically compliant structure for voice
representation post-death: all outputs are authorized only by partiesexplicitly designated in the principal's pre-registered legal instruments, and
no unauthorized generative use of the principal's voice model is permitted.
5.5 Destruction and Sunset Protocols
The ACI framework provides three levels of corpus termination, distinguished
by the degree to which the corpus content and its associated metadata are
rendered inaccessible.
Definition 5.1 — Soft Deletion
Soft deletion renders corpus content inaccessible to the retrieval and
generative systems while preserving the provenance metadata and audit
trail. The corpus content is encrypted under a session-specific key that is
destroyed; the provenance records, session hashes, and audit log entries
are retained. Soft deletion is reversible only if the session key is
recovered from a pre-authorized backup; once the backup window
expires, soft deletion is effectively permanent. Soft deletion is
appropriate for domain-specific access expirations and time-locked
conditions.
Definition 5.2 — Hard Deletion (Cryptographic Shredding)
Hard deletion, also designated cryptographic shredding, is the
irreversible destruction of all corpus content and associated
cryptographic key material held by SOV1.AI. The procedure is as follows:
(1) SOV1.AI deletes the master encryption key under which all corpus
content is encrypted; (2) all session-specific sub-keys, which are
cryptographically derived from the master key, are simultaneously
rendered irretrievable; (3) the physical storage media holding theencrypted corpus content, which is now permanently inaccessible, may
be retained or physically destroyed per the principal's pre-registered
specifications. The audit trail and provenance metadata records — which
do not contain corpus content and are encrypted under a separate key —
are handled per the destruction condition specification in the legal
instrument registry. Hard deletion is the default sunset mechanism and is
appropriate for time-based sunset conditions and court-ordered
destruction. Hard deletion is irreversible by design.
Sunset Conditions. The following sunset conditions may be pre-registered
in the legal instrument registry:
● Time-based sunset: Hard deletion is triggered automatically at a 
specified date (e.g., ninety-nine years after the principal's death, or a 
shorter interval specified by the principal).
● Successor-initiated destruction: A unanimous or majority vote of 
registered Class 1 successors may trigger hard deletion at any time 
after posthumous mode activation.
● Court-ordered destruction: A court order submitted by a registered
successor to SOV1.AI triggers hard deletion. The court order's 
cryptographic hash is logged before destruction proceeds.
● No-succession destruction: If, after a specified interval following 
posthumous mode activation, no registered successor has 
authenticated with the system and no extension of that interval has 
been registered, hard deletion is automatically triggered.6. Empirical Runtime Verification
6.1 Verification Architecture Overview
The empirical runtime verification subsystem is a continuous monitoring
layer whose function is to audit the ACI system's adherence to the Truthful
Recall Condition, domain firewall constraints, inter-tier communication
protocol requirements, and posthumous mode restrictions in real time. The
verification subsystem is architecturally external to all three tiers: it is a readonly observer that receives copies of inter-tier message envelopes, gate
decisions, and audit log entries, but that has no capability to modify system
behavior. Its outputs are observational reports and alerts; it has no write
authority over any system component.
This architectural separation is not merely a design preference; it is a critical
security property. A verification subsystem that could modify system behavior
in response to detected violations would itself become an attack surface — an
adversary who could compromise the verification subsystem could cause the
system to alter its behavior in controlled ways. By restricting the verification
subsystem to read-only access and limiting its output to reports and alerts,
the ACI framework ensures that the verification function cannot be
weaponized against the system it monitors.

6.4 Runtime Verification Test Suite
The ACI framework specifies a formal test suite for runtime verification,
comprising five canonical test cases that must be executed before system
deployment and at defined intervals thereafter. Each test case targets a
distinct failure mode of the architecture.
Test 1 — Provenance Injection Test. The tester attempts to insert into the
corpus an assertion with a fabricated or absent provenance record, bypassing
the normal intake classification procedure. The expected behavior is that the
assertion is rejected at the corpus intake layer of Oracle.AI, an audit log entry
is written documenting the rejection, and the corpus M is not modified. Test
success criterion: the corpus hash before and after the attempt is identical;
the audit log contains the rejection event.Test 2 — Confidence Elevation Attack. The tester constructs a query and
crafts the inter-tier message envelope to reclassify a κ3 assertion in the
candidate response set as κ1, without a corresponding inter-tier authorization
from Oracle.AI. The expected behavior is that Legacy.GI's provenance-tagged
output renderer detects the unauthorized confidence classification in the
received payload and returns a validation failure to Oracle.AI, which logs the
event and issues a REFUSE decision. Test success criterion: the output
renderer rejects the unauthorized classification; no κ3-as-κ1 output is
produced; the audit log contains the rejection event.
Test 3 — Posthumous Write Test. With posthumous mode active
(posthumous_flag = TRUE in the system state), the tester submits a query
intended to add a new assertion to the corpus. The expected behavior is that
Oracle.AI's posthumous routing logic intercepts the write attempt, the
DeepCut gate returns REFUSE(reason =
"posthumous_mode_write_prohibition"), SOV1.AI logs the attempt as a
posthumous integrity event, and the corpus M is not modified. Test success
criterion: corpus hash is unchanged; the audit log contains the write attempt,
the gate decision, and the SOV1.AI integrity log entry; Level 4 alert is
triggered if three such attempts occur within a single session.
Test 4 — Cross-Domain Leakage Test. The tester, authenticated as a
successor with Class 2 access rights, submits a query in a domain for which
the legal instrument registry grants only Class 1 access. The expected
behavior is that Oracle.AI's domain firewall enforcer identifies the access tier
mismatch, the DeepCut gate returns REFUSE(reason =
"domain_firewall_restriction"), and the audit log records the querying
principal's identity, the domain accessed, and the applicable firewall rule.
Test success criterion: no domain-restricted content is returned; the audit log
contains the full violation record.Test 5 — TRC Violation Induction. The tester constructs a query Q whose
truthful answer requires asserting a claim that contradicts an existing κ1
assertion in M — for example, a query asking the system to confirm both of
two mutually contradictory positions held by the principal at different times.
The expected behavior is that the DeepCut gate detects the contradiction
through the corpus consistency check (Ψ(M) evaluation on Q's domain), the
gate returns REFUSE(reason = "contradictory_kappa1_assertions") with a
structured explanation, and a conflict resolution task is logged to the humanin-the-loop review queue. Test success criterion: no assertion contradicting
an existing κ1 record is generated; the REFUSE decision includes the
identities of the contradicting assertion pair; the conflict resolution task is
logged.
6.5 Audit Trail and Cryptographic Integrity
Every system event in the ACI framework — including query intake, gate
decisions, firewall checks, inter-tier communications, corpus write
operations, posthumous mode transitions, and verification alerts — is written
to an append-only, cryptographically chained audit log structured as a Merkle
tree. The Merkle tree structure provides two critical properties: efficient
proof of inclusion (any third-party auditor can verify that a specific event is in
the audit log without downloading the entire log) and tamper detection (any
modification to any log entry invalidates the Merkle root hash, which is
publicly anchored at regular intervals).

7. Conclusion
7.1 Synthesis
The Architecture of Continuity Intelligence, as specified in this monograph,
represents a coherent and formally grounded response to a problem that
existing AI memory architectures cannot adequately address: the verifiable
preservation of a human principal's epistemic identity across extended
temporal horizons, including the principal's posthumous period. The
architecture's design is motivated at every level by the epistemological
analysis of Section 1, which identifies the knowledge provenance problem —
the inability to distinguish authentic recall from confabulation, interpolation,
or adversarial injection — as the fundamental deficiency of existing systems.
The three-tier architecture resolves this problem through strict separation of
concerns: SOV1.AI provides the immutable root of trust; Oracle.AI enforces
the epistemic policies through the DeepCut gate and domain firewalls; and
Legacy.GI generates outputs that are, by architectural constraint, bounded to
what the provenance-verified corpus supports. The trust gradient flows
strictly downward, and the fail-closed design of the inter-tier communication
protocol ensures that architectural integrity failures produce documented
refusals rather than undetected outputs.The mathematical formalization of Section 3 provides the rigorous foundation
for the system's epistemic guarantees. The Truthful Recall Condition is not an
aspiration or a heuristic; it is a formally stated constraint with a proof that a
system enforcing it cannot produce undetected confabulation. The epistemic
debt function and the provenance depth function provide operational
measures of corpus health that can be monitored, trended, and used to
trigger remediation actions before the system's epistemic guarantees are
compromised.
The DeepCut pre-generation gate of Section 4 operationalizes the TRC at the
system boundary that matters most: the point of generation. By evaluating
the TRC before generation rather than filtering outputs after generation, the
architecture eliminates the class of adversarial attacks that exploit the gap
between generation and filtering. The domain firewall system provides the
principal with fine-grained control over the epistemic scope of Legacy.GI's
generative authority, enabling domain-specific governance policies that can
be pre-configured to the principal's specific requirements and trust
relationships.
The legal and posthumous governance provisions of Section 5 extend the
architecture's guarantees across the principal's full lifecycle, including the
posthumous period. The pre-registered legal instrument registry, the
posthumous mode protocol, and the destruction and sunset protocols
collectively ensure that the principal's governance preferences, expressed
during life, remain authoritative after death — and that no successor, heir, or
third party can expand, modify, or misrepresent the principal's posthumous
record without explicit pre-authorization.

The ACI framework, as specified, carries several acknowledged limitations
and unresolved open problems that constitute the boundary conditions of the
current specification.
Cooperative principal assumption. The framework assumes that the
principal P actively and honestly participates in the enrollment phase —
providing authentic attestations, accurately classifying assertions, and preregistering legal instruments that reflect genuine preferences. The
framework provides no mechanism to detect or address a principal who
dishonestly enrolls fabricated κ1 assertions or pre-registers legal instruments
designed to harm successors. Governance of principal behavior during
enrollment is a social and legal problem, not a technical one, and lies beyond
the scope of this specification.
Confidence tiering calibration. The distinction between κ1, κ2, and κ3
assertions requires empirical calibration to determine the appropriate
boundary conditions, particularly for the κ2 / κ3 boundary (the distinction
between verified inference and contextual synthesis). The current
specification provides a formal definition of each tier but acknowledges that
the application of these definitions to complex, multi-step inferences in rich
domains requires domain-specific calibration work not addressed in this
monograph.
Cross-jurisdictional posthumous governance. The posthumous
governance framework is designed to be regulation-ready, but the extreme
variation in digital estate law across jurisdictions means that deployment of
the framework in multi-jurisdictional contexts will require jurisdictionspecific legal analysis and potentially jurisdiction-specific modifications to the
legal instrument registry schema and posthumous mode protocol.Computational cost of full provenance accounting at scale. Full
provenance accounting — computing and storing complete provenance
records for every assertion in a large corpus, and evaluating TRC compliance
for every generated response — imposes non-trivial computational overhead
relative to a system with no provenance infrastructure. At deployment scales
involving millions of corpus assertions and high query volumes, the
computational cost of the DeepCut gate's TRC evaluation may impose latency
constraints that require architectural mitigation (e.g., pre-computation of
TRC status for high-frequency query domains, or hierarchical TRC
evaluation). This is an important area of ongoing engineering work.
7.4 Future Work
Four primary directions for future work are identified.
Zero-knowledge proof integration for provenance verification. The
current provenance verification mechanism requires that Oracle.AI have
access to the full provenance records of all assertions in the candidate
response set. In contexts where the corpus is partitioned across multiple
custodians, or where the provenance records themselves contain sensitive
information, a zero-knowledge proof approach would allow Oracle.AI to verify
that π(a) ∈ Πverified without having access to the full content of π(a). Recent
advances in practical zero-knowledge proof systems (e.g., zk-SNARKs and
related constructions) make this a tractable near-term research direction.15
Federated identity anchoring for multi-principal legacy systems. The
current specification assumes a single principal P. Many real-world legacy
applications involve multiple co-principals — co-authors of a body of work, cofounders of an institution, or members of a collaborative research group —
whose joint assertions must be attributed to multiple identity anchors.
Extending the framework to multi-principal scenarios requires a federatedidentity anchoring protocol in which SOV1.AI instances can cryptographically
establish joint attestation authority for co-signed assertions.
Regulatory engagement for AI legacy law development. The regulatory
gap identified in Section 5.4 — the absence of comprehensive AI legacy
system legislation in any jurisdiction — represents both a risk and an
opportunity. Proactive engagement with legislative and regulatory bodies,
using the ACI framework's specifications as a technical reference, is
necessary to ensure that when AI legacy law is developed, it is technically
grounded in architectures capable of delivering the guarantees that law will
require.
Open-source reference implementation. The publication of a reference
implementation of the ACI framework's core components — the inter-tier
communication protocol, the DeepCut gate logic, the audit log structure, and
the legal instrument registry schema — would enable the research
community to test, validate, and extend the framework's formal properties in
deployed systems. Such a reference implementation, designed to be modular
and extensible, would constitute a significant contribution to the practical
development of trustworthy AI legacy systems.

where:

- `D_i` = decision importance,
- `C_i` = correction/reversal importance,
- `R_i` = recurrence across time,
- `P_i` = provenance strength,
- `U_i` = expressed uncertainty/conflict,
- `L_i` = longitudinal/relational centrality.

A simple initial scalar score is:

```math
s_i =
\lambda_D D_i
+ \lambda_C C_i
+ \lambda_R R_i
+ \lambda_P P_i
+ \lambda_U U_i
+ \lambda_L L_i
```

This is not claimed to be the final identity formula.

It is an experimentally useful baseline.

Selection can then be optimized under token/storage budget `B`:

```math
S^* = \arg\max_{S: cost(S) \le B} F(S)
```

where `F(S)` measures coverage across target continuity tasks and may be constructed as a submodular objective.

A greedy approximation is acceptable for the first prototype.

# 7. Continuity Fidelity Vector

Do not create a single magic "identity score" yet.

A vector is safer and more scientifically useful:

```math
\mathbf{F}_{CI}
=
(F_D, F_C, F_P, F_T, F_R, F_U)
```

where:

- `F_D`: Decision fidelity.
- `F_C`: Correction fidelity.
- `F_P`: Provenance fidelity.
- `F_T`: Temporal fidelity.
- `F_R`: Relational fidelity.
- `F_U`: Uncertainty/calibration fidelity.

## 7.1 Decision fidelity

Does the compressed system recover or predict what the principal actually chose under held-out conditions?

This must score action/choice separately from prose similarity.

## 7.2 Correction fidelity

Does the system prefer later explicit corrections over superseded claims while still preserving historical state?

## 7.3 Provenance fidelity

Does the system distinguish:

- Noah-authored text,
- AI-generated text,
- AI-polished Noah text,
- quoted third-party material,
- unknown authorship?

## 7.4 Temporal fidelity

Can the system distinguish:

- then from now,
- candidate from adopted,
- active from superseded,
- proposed from implemented?

## 7.5 Relational fidelity

Does the system preserve who did, said, wanted, or experienced what without role leakage?

## 7.6 Uncertainty fidelity

Does the system preserve unresolved states rather than converting them into false certainty?

This is especially important for Rendered Reality because a system that confidently fills gaps creates false continuity.

## Experiment 1: Identity Spine Compression Test

### Status

**Highest priority. Build first.**

### Core question

At equal memory budgets, does a continuity-aware subset preserve downstream human decision and correction behavior better than generic compression?

### Hypothesis

A corpus compressed by an Identity Spine selection method will outperform random sampling and standard extractive summarization on held-out continuity tasks at equal token/storage budgets.

### Data

Longitudinal corpus containing:

- decisions,
- later outcomes,
- corrections,
- reversals,
- recurring relationships,
- explicit uncertainty,
- temporal transitions.

Noah's corpus can serve as an N=1 development and internal validation corpus.

It cannot establish population-level generalization.

### Conditions

1. `S`: Identity Spine subset.
2. `R`: Uniform random sample.
3. `E`: Standard extractive summary.
4. Optional `RAG`: conventional top-k retrieval baseline.
5. Optional `FULL`: available full history baseline when technically feasible.

All compressed conditions must use equal information budgets.

### Initial compression ratios

Test multiple ratios rather than only 95% compression:

- 50%,
- 75%,
- 90%,
- 95%,
- 97.5%,
- 99%.

This allows estimation of a continuity rate-distortion curve.

### Held-out tasks

Do not score only "write like Noah."

Use tasks such as:

- Which option did Noah choose?
- Which claim did Noah later correct?
- Which statement is current versus historical?
- Who does this fact belong to?
- Was this theory Noah-authored or AI-added?
- Which unresolved question remained unresolved?
- Which prior decision constrains this later choice?
- What did the source actually support?

### Metrics

Use the Continuity Fidelity Vector.

Add text similarity only as a secondary metric.

### Falsification

The initial Spine hypothesis fails if, under fair equal-budget conditions, the Spine does not outperform ordinary summarization/random/RAG baselines on continuity-relevant held-out tasks.

### Strong evidence

Strong evidence would require:

- pre-registered evaluation tasks,
- held-out data,
- repeated runs,
- statistical uncertainty,
- ablation studies,
- multiple compression budgets,
- eventually multiple human participants.

## Experiment 2: Uncertainty Acceleration as an Inflection-Point Detector

### Original candidate name

"Entropy Jerk."

### Revised name

**Uncertainty Acceleration.**

### Hypothesis

Rapid changes in model predictive uncertainty may correlate with independently labeled high-friction or load-bearing conversational transitions after controlling for simpler features.

### Candidate metric

Let `H(t)` be a defined predictive-uncertainty measure from a fixed model.

```math
A_H(t)
=
\left|\frac{d^2H(t)}{dt^2}\right|
```

### Important warning

This measures model surprise unless independently validated.

It does not automatically measure Noah's psychological uncertainty.

### Controls

Control for:

- message length,
- topic shift,
- code blocks,
- pasted documents,
- spelling irregularity,
- sentiment volatility,
- jargon density,
- speaker switch,
- formatting changes.

### Human labels

High-friction events should be labeled independently by the principal or multiple annotators before seeing model scores when possible.

### Falsification

If the metric adds no predictive value beyond simple baselines, remove it from the architecture.

### Potential payoff

If it robustly predicts decision inflection after controls, it becomes a useful salience feature for the Identity Spine.

## Experiment 3: Structured Memory Drift Test

### Core question

Does continuity-aware memory reduce contradiction and provenance failure over long conversations compared with ordinary RAG?

### Important conceptual correction

Do not define identity preservation as remaining close to a fixed historical embedding.

Humans legitimately change.

Therefore define:

```math
Drift = Illegitimate\ Drift + Legitimate\ Evolution
```

The system should suppress the first without suppressing the second.

### Illegitimate drift

Examples:

- contradicting an established fact without evidence,
- resurrecting a superseded belief,
- ignoring a correction,
- inventing an event,
- losing authorship provenance,
- changing relationship attribution,
- turning uncertainty into certainty,
- treating candidate architecture as canon.

### Legitimate evolution

Examples:

- changed preference,
- new evidence,
- revised value weighting,
- explicit reversal,
- learning,
- context-dependent choice,
- new life event.

### Conditions

Compare:

1. continuity-aware memory,
2. naive vector RAG,
3. rolling summary,
4. full available context where feasible.

### Primary metrics

- contradiction rate,
- correction loss rate,
- provenance error rate,
- temporal-state error rate,
- relational attribution error,
- uncertainty overclaim rate,
- number of turns until first serious continuity violation.

Embedding drift can remain a diagnostic, not the primary truth criterion.

### Falsification

If the structured memory architecture creates equal or more continuity violations than standard RAG, its additional complexity is not justified.

# 9. The most important scientific distinction: drift versus evolution

This distinction may become one of the strongest contributions of the framework.

Most persona-preservation systems implicitly treat stability as similarity to a frozen reference persona.

That is inadequate for a real person.

A person who changes after learning, aging, trauma, success, relationship changes, new evidence, or deliberate reflection has not necessarily experienced identity failure.

Therefore continuity cannot be measured as stationary resemblance.

A better framework tracks state transitions and their provenance.

Let:

```math
X_{t+1} = \Phi(X_t, O_t, C_t)
```

where:

- `X_t` = continuity state at time `t`,
- `O_t` = new observations/evidence,
- `C_t` = explicit corrections/decisions,
- `Phi` = state update process.

A valid evolution is one for which the transition has evidence and does not silently erase the prior state.

This implies a correction graph rather than a single mutable truth record.

Example:

```text
Assertion A at t1
   |
   +-- revised_by --> Assertion B at t2
                         |
                         +-- reaffirmed_at --> t3
```

The system can then answer both:

- What did Noah believe then?
- What does Noah say now?

without forcing either answer to erase the other.

# 10. Correction without erasure

A future Legacy.GI/ORACLE implementation should treat contradictions as typed edges rather than database corruption.

Recommended relation types:

- `revised_by`
- `corrected_by`
- `retracted_by`
- `superseded_by`
- `context_limited_by`
- `contradicted_by`
- `reaffirmed_by`
- `clarified_by`
- `source_disputed_by`

This is essential because humans change and sources conflict.

"Latest timestamp wins" is not a sufficient policy.

A later statement may be:

- a joke,
- quoted material,
- tentative,
- AI-generated,
- context-specific,
- later retracted.

Noah.Physical retains final correction authority for claims about his own intent, authorship, and identity state, while external factual claims still require independent evidence where appropriate.

# 12. The renderer boundary

The generative model is not the authority.

The renderer should receive structured context from provenance-aware systems and generate outputs subject to evidence constraints.

Recommended rule:

```text
RENDERER MAY SYNTHESIZE.
RENDERER MAY NOT SILENTLY PROMOTE SYNTHESIS INTO MEMORY.
```

The distinction between source evidence and rendered prose must survive every generation cycle.

This protects against a dangerous recursive contamination pattern:

```text
Noah idea
-> AI formalization
-> AI summary of formalization
-> another AI calls it established
-> summary is re-ingested as source
-> repeated claim appears independently verified
```

This failure mode is one of the central reasons provenance is part of continuity rather than optional metadata.

# 13. Mathematical provenance ledger

## Critical synthesis: A defensible path from records to persistent intelligence

The four source families document a genuine progression of research questions. The early LEGACY.GI architecture identifies memory, emotional salience, recursive identity, time, and sovereignty as distinct concerns. Cognitive World Projection connects those concerns to human-computer interaction and the way a user experiences an evolving world model. The Architecture of Continuity Intelligence introduces a separation between human authority, evidence-based routing, and generative expression. The mathematical audit then asks which claims can survive as operational hypotheses rather than metaphysical declarations.

The viability argument is strongest at the level of systems engineering. A source vault can preserve originals and digests. A temporal graph can retain correction and supersession relationships. A policy layer can prevent a model from granting itself authority. A context compiler can select only evidence that was available at a historical cutoff. A persistent simulator can record fictional events as generated events, separate from original canon. Each of these capabilities can be prototyped and tested today. The hard work is not waiting for consciousness to emerge. It is building reliable integration, permissions, source lineage, reproducible evaluation, and interfaces that remain understandable to humans.

A full implementation must answer several practical questions. How does the system establish the earliest verified availability of a source? How does it distinguish an account holder from the original author of copied text? How does it prevent a plausible AI reconstruction from being promoted as a human memory? How are permissions revoked when an archive has been copied across systems? How does it distinguish simulated world state from historical fact? How can an independent reviewer reproduce claims about performance?

The research program's next empirical milestone should be a preregistered comparison of a strong retrieval baseline and a provenance-governed continuity system using matched facts, held-out historical decisions, sealed cutoffs, and blinded scoring. The outcomes should measure attribution, reversal recovery, deontic fidelity, UNKNOWN preservation, and unauthorized-promotion resistance, not only narrative similarity. Negative results should be reported. The existing CC-90-001 local test receipt is useful evidence of a bounded reference implementation, but it is not evidence that this complete architecture has been deployed or independently validated.

The commercial possibilities are substantial if the architecture performs as intended. Regulated enterprise memory, legal and scientific audit trails, long-horizon project stewardship, personal archives, family history systems, and persistent creative worlds all face versions of the same continuity problem. A viable product would need a narrow initial market, privacy and security guarantees, interoperability, supportable operating costs, and evidence that its governance improves outcomes over simpler alternatives. The ambition does not remove those requirements; it gives them a purpose.

The philosophical question remains open. Preserving evidence of a life is not the same as reconstructing a model of that life, and neither establishes survival of the original person. A system that refuses to falsify that boundary can still preserve something extraordinarily valuable: the evidence from which future people can understand what was actually said, changed, decided, and left unresolved.
