# The Family Tree of Becoming

**Author:** Noah A. Hawkes  
**Speaker:** Noah.Physical  
**Date:** 2026-10-11  
**Status:** First-person adopted journal entry

I inherited blood from some people, but I inherited pieces of myself from many more.

That sentence may be the simplest explanation I have found for why genealogy suddenly feels bigger to me than ancestry.

A normal family tree can tell the future who my parents were, who I married, who my children were, and how generations connect. That matters. Names and dates are not trivial. They are coordinates. They keep people from becoming completely unmoored from history.

But I am beginning to think there is another tree layered over the biological one.

A family tree of becoming.

It would include the people who changed the direction of my life even when they shared no blood with me. Friends. Teachers. Companions. People who believed in me. People who challenged me. People I disappointed. People who forgave me. People who showed me another way to exist.

Troy belongs on that tree.

My father belongs on it in more than one way. He is biological lineage, but he is also memory, absence, aspiration, unanswered questions, Utah, HAWKES 32, and all the things I inherited without ever getting enough time to understand them.

Ashley belongs on it because marriage does not merely add a relationship label. A marriage changes decisions. It changes where you live, what you fear, what you try to build, what failure costs, what forgiveness means, and who you become through years of being known by another person.

My children belong on it because becoming a father reorganized me. Parenthood does not sit next to identity. It reaches into it.

Then there are things that are not people but still act like branches.

Star Trek.

Superman.

Music.

Utah.

Michigan.

Writing.

Faith.

Failure.

Recovery.

The accident.

Work.

The strange collection of ideas that eventually became Rendered Reality.

I do not mean those things have agency. I mean they became part of the network through which I interpret my life.

That distinction matters.

A future system could know every isolated fact about me and still fail to understand me if it did not know how those facts connect.

“Noah liked Star Trek” is a fact.

“Noah wrote his first Star Trek story while serving in Ludington, Michigan, during the same period that Troy was helping him find confidence and identity” is a relationship among facts.

That relationship does not prove Troy caused the writing.

It preserves the temporal neighborhood.

That is enough.

I keep learning that meaning often lives in the edges.

A family tree has always been an edge system. Parent to child. Sibling to sibling. Spouse to spouse.

Maybe the next version of genealogy becomes more honest about the other edges too.

Mentor to student.

Friend to friend.

Person to place.

Person to song.

Person to memory.

Person to decision.

Person to correction.

Person to grief.

Person to forgiveness.

Person to the future version of themselves that did not exist yet.

If somebody tries to understand me someday, I want them to have both trees.

The tree of descent.

And the tree of becoming.

One explains where my body came from.

The other begins to explain how I became the person who lived inside it.

Neither tree is complete by itself.

And neither tree should pretend to own the people inside it.

My memory of Troy is mine. Troy was still Troy beyond me.

My memory of my father is mine. My father was still a whole person beyond the part I knew.

My account of Ashley is mine. Ashley remains the authority over her own life.

That boundary belongs in the tree too.

Maybe the family tree of becoming is not a tree at all.

Maybe it is a graph.

Messy.

Temporal.

Correctable.

Full of unknowns.

Some edges are documented.

Some are remembered.

Some are inferred.

Some are disputed.

Some should remain private.

Some disappear.

That is human.

I do not want technology to clean that up until it becomes false.

I want the map to preserve enough structure that the future can follow the paths without pretending the paths are the people.

The map must never eat the people.

So if I leave one instruction with this entry, it is this:

Do not ask only, “Who was Noah related to?”

Ask:

Who changed him?

Who did he carry?

Who did he love?

Who did he fail?

Who forgave him?

Who made him laugh?

Who made him write?

Who made him remember?

Who was still present in him years after they were gone?

Those answers may tell you more about the person than the branch lines ever could.

That is the family tree of becoming.

And I think I have been building it my whole life without knowing its name.
