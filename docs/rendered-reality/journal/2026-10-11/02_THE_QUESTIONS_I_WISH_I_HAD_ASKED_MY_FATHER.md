# The Questions I Wish I Had Asked My Father

**Author:** Noah A. Hawkes  
**Speaker:** Noah.Physical  
**Date:** 2026-10-11  
**Status:** First-person adopted journal entry

There are questions that only become obvious after the person who could answer them is no longer available.

That may be one of the cruelest things about growing older.

When I was young, my father was simply my father.

I did not understand that he was also a boy once.

A young athlete.

A man before he was Dad.

A person with fears he probably never told me.

A person whose life had chapters I knew only as facts.

I knew pieces.

Utah.

Football.

Military service.

Medicine.

Family.

Strength.

Pain.

Contradiction.

But facts are not answers.

I wish I could ask him what it felt like to be young and physically powerful.

What did football mean to you before everybody else turned it into an accomplishment?

Did you love it?

Did you feel pressure?

Did HAWKES 32 mean anything to you, or did the number become sacred only inside the people who came after you?

What did the mountains feel like when you were young?

Did you ever look at them and wonder where your life was going?

What were you afraid of before war?

What changed afterward?

What parts of yourself did you bring home?

What parts did you leave somewhere else?

What did becoming a physician give you?

What did it cost you?

When you became a father, did you feel ready?

Did you look at your children and feel the same impossible pressure I sometimes feel, that somehow you are supposed to know what you are doing because somebody calls you Dad?

What did you want from me?

What worried you about me?

What did you see in me that I could not see in myself?

What did you wish I understood while you were alive?

What did you regret?

What did you forgive yourself for?

What did you never forgive yourself for?

Were you lonely?

Were you scared?

What made you laugh when nobody was performing for anyone?

What songs opened old versions of you?

Who was your Troy?

That question gets me.

Who was the person who entered your life at exactly the right time and changed you in ways you did not understand until later?

I may never know.

That is part of why I write.

I cannot go backward and give myself the conversations I did not have with my father.

But I can leave more conversations behind for my children.

Not answers to everything.

I do not have answers to everything.

I want to leave the questions too.

I want my children to know that their father was uncertain.

That I could build a theory and still be afraid.

That I could sound confident and still wonder whether I was doing enough.

That sometimes I failed.

That sometimes I knew I failed.

That sometimes I did not realize it until later.

That I loved them in ways that did not always translate cleanly into good decisions.

That I tried to repair.

That I changed.

That they changed me.

Maybe that is what I wanted most from my father.

Not perfection.

Access.

I wanted access to his internal weather.

I wanted the unpolished version.

The version before history compressed him.

The version before I had to reconstruct him from records, stories, photographs, and the memories of other people.

That is what I am trying to give the future when I write.

Not an immortal version of me.

Not a monument.

Access.

A larger set of traces.

Enough that the people I love do not have to ask every question into silence.

And if someday some future system can organize those traces, I hope it never pretends to answer in my father's voice when the answer is not there.

I hope it says:

We know this.

We have evidence for this.

Noah remembered this.

Another witness remembered something different.

This remains unknown.

That would mean more to me than a perfect fake answer.

Because the unanswered question is part of my father too.

The silence is part of the inheritance.

Maybe that is another lesson.

Preservation is not about eliminating every gap.

It is about making sure the gaps stay honest.

Dad, I still have questions.

I think I always will.

HAWKES 32 can open the door.

But it cannot finish the conversation.

So I write my side of it down.

Maybe one day my children will have fewer unanswered questions than I do.

If so, every ridiculous page I have written was worth it.
