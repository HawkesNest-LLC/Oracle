# What My Children Will Never Know Unless I Write It Down

**Author:** Noah A. Hawkes  
**Speaker:** Noah.Physical  
**Date:** 2026-10-11  
**Status:** First-person adopted journal entry

There are things my children will never know unless I write them down.

Not because I have hidden them.

Because ordinary life does not announce which moments will matter later.

They may remember the big things.

Trips.

Birthdays.

Christmases.

Graduations.

The difficult years.

The good years.

The times money was tight.

The times we laughed until somebody could not breathe.

But there are thousands of little things I carry about them that they may never know I noticed.

The sound of their footsteps in the house.

The way each one entered a room differently.

The phases that felt permanent while they were happening and vanished without warning.

The words they mispronounced.

The obsessions that lasted six months.

The way the house changed as they got older.

The first time I realized one of them no longer needed help with something I had always done for them.

The strange sadness hidden inside pride.

That is what parenthood keeps teaching me.

Every gain contains a loss.

You want them to grow.

Then they grow.

And suddenly you miss the version you were trying so hard to get through the day with.

I wish I had recorded more of the ordinary.

Not because every second needed to become data.

It did not.

I do not want my children to feel like their childhood was a research project.

That would violate the whole point.

Life first.

The archive stays downstream.

But there are moments when writing helps me see what I am already living.

I want my children to know that I watched them becoming.

I noticed more than I said.

I worried more than I admitted.

I was proud more often than I verbalized.

I was tired sometimes.

Distracted sometimes.

Wrong sometimes.

I probably missed things that mattered to them because I was focused on things that felt urgent to me.

I want that preserved too.

Not as a confession ritual.

As truth.

Parents are not omniscient witnesses to childhood.

We see from our own angle.

My children own their own stories.

My journal can preserve my experience of being their father.

It cannot define their childhood for them.

That boundary matters.

So if a future reader finds this, do not treat “Noah remembered” as “this is exactly how the child experienced it.”

Keep both perspectives if both exist.

That is continuity with dignity.

I also want them to know the house had a sound.

A family has a sound.

Doors.

Dogs.

Television.

People yelling from another room.

Someone asking where something is while standing three feet from it.

Cars coming and going.

Dinner.

Arguments.

Music.

Laughter.

Silence after a hard conversation.

Those things disappear so completely that it is easy to forget they were once the entire world.

One day the house gets quiet.

Children leave.

Rooms change.

Objects move.

And the atmosphere that seemed permanent turns out to have been temporary.

That is why I write.

Not because I want to freeze my children at any age.

Because I want to preserve evidence that these versions existed.

They were here.

I was here with them.

We changed each other.

That is the family archive I care about.

Not a surveillance log.

A witness.
