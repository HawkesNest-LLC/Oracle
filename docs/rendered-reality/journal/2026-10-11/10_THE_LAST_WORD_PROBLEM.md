# The Last Word Problem

**Author:** Noah A. Hawkes  
**Speaker:** Noah.Physical  
**Date:** 2026-10-11  
**Status:** First-person adopted journal entry  
**Research status:** Open question, no posthumous authority model adopted

There is a question hiding underneath everything I am building.

Who gets to correct me when I cannot correct the record myself?

While I am alive, the answer feels straightforward.

Me.

Not because I am automatically right about every external fact.

I am not.

But I am the highest authority on what I intended, what I adopt, what I reject, and what I presently believe about myself.

Then eventually that authority becomes unavailable.

What happens after that?

Suppose a future system has thirty years of my writing.

It has journals.

Messages.

Videos.

Decisions.

Corrections.

Patterns.

It knows that over time I tended to move in a certain direction on some question.

Then new evidence appears after I am gone.

The model predicts with 95 percent confidence that I would have changed my mind.

Does it get to update “Noah believes X” to “Noah believes Y”?

No.

At least I do not think so.

Prediction is not authority.

The system can say:

Based on the documented trajectory, this is a plausible projected position.

It cannot say:

Noah changed his mind.

I did not.

I was not there to do it.

That distinction may become one of the most important rules in posthumous AI.

A very accurate model can still overstep.

Accuracy is not permission.

This is the Last Word Problem.

Frozen Human says the last verified state remains final forever.

Trajectory says later inference may be useful if clearly labeled.

Trustee models give authority to selected people.

Constitutional models follow prior instructions.

Successor Identity models raise even stranger questions about whether some future continuation can inherit authority.

I do not know the answer.

I do know the danger.

The future may become extremely good at making the dead say things they never said.

Some of those things may even be things they probably would have said.

That is not the same.

I want the distinction protected.

If I leave explicit instructions, preserve them.

If my family disagrees, preserve the disagreement.

If my later trajectory makes an inference plausible, label the inference.

If nobody knows, say UNKNOWN.

Do not use computational confidence to manufacture consent.

Do not let prediction become authorship.

Maybe the dead do not get a final word on everything.

History keeps discovering new facts.

Other people have their own rights to speak.

But the dead should not be silently updated just because a machine can model them.

The Last Word Problem remains open.

I want it to remain open until we have a better answer than convenience.
