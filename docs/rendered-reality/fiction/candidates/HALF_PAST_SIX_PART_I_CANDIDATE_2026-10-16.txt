CHAPTER ONE
The Wrong End of Tuesday

At six minutes to seven the rice began to stick.

Ama heard it before she smelled it. The lid had stopped rattling. That was how she knew. For the first ten minutes after everyone was supposed to be home, the pot had sounded almost impatient. Now it made a small dry noise whenever she moved it, as if the bottom were being rubbed with paper.

She switched the ring off.

Her mother had said, Keep it warm. She had not said how long. Adults left that part out of instructions and later behaved as if the answer had been printed on the wall.

Ama took down four bowls, then put one back. She stood looking at the remaining three. Her great-grandmother liked the blue bowl. Kojo liked the one with the repaired edge because he claimed the repair looked like a lightning strike. Her mother said none of the bowls belonged to anybody, a rule she herself abandoned whenever someone used her mug.

She took the fourth bowl down again.

The little display beside the kitchen doorway said FAMILY SUPPER 18:30. Under it were four names. Four neat green circles indicated that nobody had canceled.

The display had not heard her mother swear when she found the note.

It had not seen Efua pull on a cardigan without getting her arm into the sleeve, or heard her say, If either of them comes here, keep them here. It had not watched Ama become the person responsible for knowing where everybody wasn't.

It was a very clean display.

Ama touched the edge of it.

"They're not here," she said.

The household system waited for a command.

"I wasn't talking to you."

She put a little water into the pot. Steam came up with the smell of the damage. She replaced the lid and left it alone.

Outside, someone on Deck Nine was moving furniture. The legs scraped twice, stopped, scraped again. A man said, Higher on your side. A woman replied that it was higher on her side and that this had been the problem the entire way from the lift.

Ama went to the door.

Two neighbors had a narrow cabinet halfway across the corridor. Its drawers were tied shut with yellow cord. The woman had one foot braced against the opposite wall and the expression of someone who had been told a thing would take five minutes.

"Is your grandmother in?" she asked.

"No."

"Good. She'll tell us we're carrying it wrong."

"You are," Ama said.

They both looked at her.

She pointed at the cabinet's short feet. "Take the bottom drawer out. She puts the stones in there."

The man lowered his end.

"What stones?"

"The ones so it doesn't tip."

They set the cabinet down. The woman untied the cord, opened the drawer, and stared into it.

"Of course she does."

Ama almost smiled. Then the household comm sounded behind her, and she left the neighbors discovering Rose's geology.

Her mother's face appeared on the wall.

"He's with me," Efua said immediately. "Kojo's with me."

Ama let out a breath so hard it seemed to have been waiting somewhere outside her chest.

"And Nana?"

"We're looking. She's been seen. Keep the food."

"It's sticking."

"Turn it off."

"I did."

"Good girl."

Ama looked away.

Efua noticed. Even in a hurry, her mother sometimes noticed the exact thing Ama wished she wouldn't.

"Thank you," she said instead. "Don't wait to eat."

The connection ended.

Ama stood in the kitchen with permission to have supper and no desire to begin it.

She opened the first drawer beneath the counter. Rose kept old fasteners in a tin, paper in a stack beneath the tin, and pencils in a cup marked TEA, which was why nobody ever found anything in this kitchen unless they already knew where it was.

Ama took out a sheet and wrote:

IF YOU CHANGE A PLAN TELL SOMEBODY.

She looked at the sentence. She crossed out SOMEBODY and wrote THE PERSON.

Then she put the paper beside the four bowls.

At the doorway, the supper display still showed four green circles.

She turned its light off.


CHAPTER TWO
Lists

Merlin had almost finished entering the afternoon's corrections when Efua Osei came into the reading room carrying a padd and a folded piece of paper.

The last correction concerned a date copied correctly from a source that had itself been wrong. He had spent twenty minutes explaining this to a man who regarded correct copying as a defense against every possible accusation.

Merlin was glad to see a familiar face until he saw hers.

"My son is missing," Efua said. "My grandmother is missing. And I have two records that disagree about where they are."

He pushed his notebook aside.

"Since when?"

"Since half past six. Since six. That's the problem."

She put the padd down hard enough to make it slide. Merlin caught it before it reached the edge.

The household recording was brief. Rose's voice promised to collect Kojo from the holodeck doors at half past six, as she did on club Tuesdays. She reminded him not to hurry if the engineering club ran late. There was a scrape, a spoon against something, then the recording ended.

The paper said:

Kojo. Meet me by the big window at six. We'll go straight to supper. Don't wait at the holodeck.

"Where did you find this?" Merlin asked.

"Under the fruit bowl. At four. I thought she'd already told him. Then he didn't come home. Neither of them did."

"Have you checked both places?"

"Twice."

Merlin touched the desk comm. Before he could speak, the reading-room door opened and Ashley Hawkes came through with a folder under her arm.

"Captain," he said, "we have two residents unaccounted for."

Ashley set down the folder.

Efua began with the paper. Ashley let her finish the first sentence, then asked for names, descriptions, last confirmed locations, and how to reach the household. Her voice made no distinction between this and the work she had come to do. Merlin found himself grateful for that, and then ashamed that he was grateful.

Ashley opened a restricted duty channel. The holodeck attendant had seen Kojo. He had waited outside the club entrance and left at twenty to seven, heading toward the core lifts. A Promenade officer was sent to the window. Another checked the route between the lifts and Deck Nine.

"Nobody broadcasts a family argument," Ashley said. "Find them. Tell me they're safe."

Efua sat at last.

"What do the records prove?"

Merlin looked at the note. The handwriting resembled the other samples deposited with the family material. He could make several observations. They would not do what she needed.

"The recording preserves her saying it. The note resembles her handwriting. I have found no sign of alteration in either. That still leaves us needing to ask her."

"That's not an answer."

"It's what I can establish."

Efua pressed the heel of her hand against her forehead.

The boy arrived while Merlin was checking the recording's source receipt. His club bag struck the door frame and spun him halfway around.

"I went where she said."

Efua stood so quickly her chair hit the wall.

She held him for three seconds, perhaps four. Kojo endured the first two and began objecting during the third.

"Mum. I'm here."

"I can see that."

"Then why are you squeezing?"

She let him go and put both hands on his shoulders.

"Where?"

"The window. Then the doors. Then here."

"Who told you the window?"

"Nana. At breakfast. She said she might change it because the light was better, and she'd put a note in my bag if she did."

Efua looked down at the paper.

Kojo followed her gaze.

"Is that it?"

"Yes."

"It wasn't in my bag."

"No."

"I looked."

"I believe you."

He visibly relaxed at that. Merlin noticed how much work those three words did, and did not enter the observation anywhere.

"I thought maybe it fell out," Kojo continued. "So I tried the window anyway. Till twenty past. Then I thought might had finished, so I went to the proper place. Then the club man said everyone went home."

The comm interrupted him.

"Promenade duty. Location confirmed. Resident responsive. Assistance declined."

Efua closed her eyes.

"Tea carrier," the officer added. "Two cups. She's waiting beside the window."

Merlin entered the report. Beneath it he wrote, Resident remains at location by choice.

Ashley read over his shoulder.

"Declined assistance," she said. "She didn't decline her family."

He looked at his sentence. It had seemed a useful completion when he wrote it.

He drew a line through it, leaving the words legible.

Kojo leaned against his mother. Now that he had been found, his exhaustion was becoming everyone's business.

"I'm hungry."

"Ama's keeping supper," Efua said.

Ashley asked him what he had eaten since lunch.

"There were things at club."

"What things?"

"Wire. Mostly."

Ashley picked up his bag.

"Come with me. Your mother needs ten minutes."

Efua looked at the captain, then at the paper.

"I want her brought here," she said. "I want them both on the desk and I want somebody to tell her she can't do this."

Ashley paused at the door.

"Do you want someone to ask her to come, or do you want me to order her?"

Efua's mouth opened. She looked at Kojo.

"Ask," she said.

"I can have the officer ask."

"No."

Ashley waited.

"I'll go."

"I'll keep him here until you call."

The door closed behind Ashley and Kojo. Efua remained standing.

"You can keep a copy," she told Merlin, indicating the note. "I want to take the paper."

He scanned it and showed her the result. She checked both sides, although there was nothing on the back.

"And you can put it beside the recording."

"Yes."

"Even if she tells me something different up there?"

"Then they're still related records."

Efua took the note.

"You don't need me to come back and explain it?"

"Only if you want to."

She nodded once and left.

Merlin sat with the recording and the scan. In the corridor Kojo was asking Ashley whether captains got larger portions. Ashley said captains had to eat vegetables first. Kojo said he had not asked to be promoted.

For the first time all evening, Merlin smiled.


CHAPTER THREE
The Window

The Promenade officer found Rose beside the window, holding two cold cups in a cardboard carrier.

"Mrs. Osei?"

"That depends on whether you're selling something."

"Your family is looking for you."

"Are they?"

For a moment the question had nothing defensive in it. Then she straightened.

"I'm looking for them."

He touched his combadge. Rose lifted a hand, almost touching his wrist, then lowered it.

"Tell them I'm here. Don't tell everyone."

The officer stepped away to speak quietly. Through the duty channel I heard the short report reach Ashley. Location confirmed. Resident responsive. Assistance declined.

My attention was already in the station's public systems, assisting the search at her request. I had access to the duty report and a public-space image sufficient to confirm that the officer had found the right person. I did not open a household feed. When Rose looked toward the nearby terminal, I let its small availability light show.

"Would you like me to stay until they arrive?" the officer asked.

"No."

He began to leave.

"Unless you were already going to stand somewhere."

He stopped beside the window.

For a while they watched a workbee cross Jupiter's reflected light. A row of docking lights ran along the structure beneath it. The nearest blinked out, then returned. A worker in an orange pressure suit was moving between two service panels.

Rose lifted a cup, discovered how cold it was, and put it down.

"My husband used to bring them here," she said.

The officer waited.

"Their parents. Before supper. He believed looking at something large improved their manners."

"Did it?"

"They were quiet while they looked. That was enough for him."

She moved the carrier between them.

"Take one. They're both dreadful now."

He accepted a cup.

The report had gone up the channel. It would not carry any of this.

Rose glanced at the terminal again.

"Commander?"

"Here, Mrs. Osei."

"Are they angry?"

"Efua is coming. Kojo is safe with Captain Hawkes."

She rubbed her thumb along the carrier's repaired handle.

"I meant after," she said.

"After what?"

"The doors. I would collect him at the doors and then we'd come here. You can tell her that."

"Would you like me to pass those words on?"

"Yes."

I sent the message to Ashley's channel as Rose's statement, with the time it had been given. She relayed it to Efua while Efua was in the lift.

Rose watched the workbee disappear beneath the docking structure.

"The window was supposed to be easier," she said.

I asked whether she needed anything before Efua arrived.

"A few minutes ago would be useful."

The officer looked down into his cold tea.

"I can get you a fresh cup."

"That will have to do."

He had not yet gone when Efua emerged from the lift. She recognized the carrier before she recognized him. Rose saw the paper in her hand.

"Oh," she said.

The officer lowered his cup.

"Thank you for staying with her," Efua told him.

"I didn't ask him to," Rose said.

"No, ma'am."

That earned him a look which softened before Efua could decide what it meant.

"My son is eating with Captain Hawkes."

"Good. He'll tell her the portions are small."

"He was missing."

Rose turned toward her fully.

"I was at the doors," Efua said. "Then here. Then back at the doors. I didn't know where either of you had gone."

"I thought he'd come straight here."

"You told him not to wait."

"Yes."

Rose said it quickly, relieved to have reached a part they agreed on.

Efua held out the note.

"And you told the Commander you meant to collect him first."

Rose took it. She held the paper at the distance she used for menus, then brought it closer.

The officer shifted his weight.

"Can I get either of you something warm?"

"Please," Efua said.

Rose nodded without looking up.

He took the carrier with him. It left a damp rectangle on the bench between them.

"Those are my words," Rose said.

"I know."

"I didn't mean for him to be wandering."

"I know that too."

The workbee returned above the docking structure. Its lights moved steadily across the glass.

"I meant it after," Rose said.

Efua pointed to the first line.

"Six."

Rose read it again.

For a few seconds Efua heard the small noises her grandmother made when an appliance had apparently changed its controls without consulting her. A breath through the nose. Her tongue against a tooth.

"I was going to do the doors," Rose said. "Then I thought we'd lose the window."

"You could have told me."

"You were working."

"So you changed it."

"Yes."

Rose sat down. Efua sat too, leaving the wet rectangle between them.

"I wrote six because I wanted six," Rose said. "He'd come out, we'd have time here, then supper. I remember thinking that."

"And later?"

"I don't know."

Efua waited.

"Later I'd been standing here a long time, and I knew what you'd say."

"What would I say?"

"That I can't change things without telling people."

"You can't change where a child gets collected without telling the people collecting him."

"I wrote it down."

"Under the pears."

"I didn't plan for the pears to conceal it."

Despite herself, Efua laughed. It came out sharply enough to hurt.

Rose looked at her, hopeful.

"I'm still angry."

"I noticed."

"You don't get to make me laugh and have that finish it."

"All right."

Rose flattened the note against her knee. There was another sheet tucked beneath her hand.

"What's that?"

"Something I was going to send you."

"Can I see?"

Rose hesitated, then handed it over.

Efua. I said I would fetch him. I don't know why you thought

The sentence stopped there. Below it, after a large blank space, Rose had written:

Where is everybody?

Efua looked toward the public comm terminal beyond the seats.

"Why didn't you call?"

"I thought if I left, he'd arrive."

"It's ten steps."

"I know how far it is."

"Then why?"

Rose reached for the paper. Efua gave it back.

"I was going to tell you I'd done exactly what I said. I started writing it. Then I couldn't finish the sentence."

The officer returned with fresh tea and three cups. He set them down without asking which woman was right.

Rose moved her bag off the bench.

"Will you sit?"

"For a minute."

Efua took a cup and found she needed both hands around it.

"Next Tuesday," she said, "if we change the collection, one of us gets an answer from the other."

Rose nodded.

"And if there's no answer, the old arrangement stands."

"What if the club changes its hours?"

"Then we sort that out too."

"That needs writing down."

"We can write it when we get home."

Rose looked at the two sheets on her knee.

"Are these going to your Ensign?"

"The first one already has a copy there."

"And this?"

"It's yours."

Rose folded the unfinished letter along its existing crease. She tucked it into her bag.

"I'd like to tell him myself."

"Kojo?"

Rose nodded.

Efua wanted to tell her what to say. She drank her tea instead.

Across the Promenade, a family emerged from the lift carrying swimming towels. One child had fallen asleep against his father's shoulder. The other was objecting to being made to walk.

Rose watched them pass.

"He used to carry you like that."

Efua leaned back against the bench. Her grandmother's shoulder touched hers.

I had let the terminal light go dark while they spoke. Rose looked at it and said my name again.

"Here."

"Where's my boy now?"

With Ashley's confirmation, I told them he was back in the reading room, fed and drawing.

Rose began the careful business of getting up.

"Somebody take me to him before the Captain feeds him again."

Efua picked up the carrier. This time Rose let her.


CHAPTER FOUR
The Extra Bowl

Kojo was drawing a door in Jupiter when Rose returned.

He had been trying to explain the drawing to Ashley. The door was for a person who lived inside the storm and came out only when the station faced the right way. Ashley had asked how anyone got through the atmosphere. Kojo said that was why the person had a door.

Rose stood in the reading-room entrance until he noticed her.

"There you are," he said.

Efua put the carrier down. She did not answer for either of them.

"I changed the plan," Rose said.

Kojo pointed at the paper in her hand. "That was supposed to go in my bag."

"Yes. It was."

"I checked the little pocket too."

"I left it in the kitchen. You didn't do anything wrong by not finding it."

He looked at her carefully.

"I went to both."

"I know."

"Was that right?"

Rose glanced at Efua. Efua waited.

"I don't know what I'd have done," Rose said at last. "I should have made sure you knew where I was."

Kojo considered this. Then he held up the covered dish Ashley had brought from the mess.

"There's food."

Rose accepted it as if it were something he had made himself.

Merlin finished linking the recording and the scan. The later statement transmitted through the duty channel was marked as a later statement. He had not entered the conversation at the window. He had no transcript of it and had asked for none.

Kojo watched his stylus.

"The note was written for me," he said. "But I never read it. I only heard the might. Does it still count as a promise?"

Merlin rested the stylus on the desk.

"It's a record of something she intended to tell you."

"That's not the same thing."

"No."

"Can you put that?"

"Which part?"

"That I didn't get it."

Merlin turned the padd around. Kojo read the short entry, asked him to replace hadn't received with didn't get, and approved it with a serious nod.

Ashley gave Efua the household padd. Rose carried the covered dish. Kojo took his drawing. They left the reading room together.

On Deck Nine, the cabinet was still in the corridor. The neighbors had removed the weighted drawer and were now arguing about whether the stones belonged to the cabinet or to the apartment it had come from.

Rose stopped.

Efua closed her eyes.

"Nana."

"That's my drawer."

"Tomorrow."

"They'll lose the stones."

"We are going home."

Rose gave the neighbors a look which left them in no doubt that their management of the stones would be reviewed. Then she followed Efua.

Ama opened the door before they reached it.

"The rice is bad."

"Hello to you too," Kojo said.

She hit his shoulder with a folded cloth, lightly enough that he could choose to be injured by it. He did.

"She hit me."

"Good," Rose said. "You're home."

Efua put down the carrier and saw the four bowls.

"You didn't eat?"

Ama shrugged.

"I told you not to wait."

"I heard you."

Something in the answer made Efua leave the sentence where it was. She went to the cooker. The rice lifted in soft clumps above a hard brown layer. She tasted a little, added water, and set the pot over gentle heat.

Kojo put the covered dish on the table.

"That's for Nana," he said before anyone could open it.

"I have a plate," Rose told him.

"The Captain gave you this one."

Rose sat. The evening had made her older in the way an ordinary difficult evening could, without permission or symbolism. She wanted her shoes off. She wanted nobody to ask another question until she had eaten something. She wanted Efua to stop moving about the kitchen as if the furniture were personally responsible.

Ama laid her handwritten instruction beside the fruit bowl.

Rose read it.

"Very large letters."

"So people see it."

Rose looked up at her.

Efua turned from the cooker.

Ama kept her hand on the paper. Her face was hot, and she disliked all three of them for noticing.

"I was here," she said. "Nobody told me anything for ages."

Efua put down the spoon.

"I should have called you earlier."

"You said keep them here if they came."

"Yes."

"I didn't know how."

Kojo opened his mouth, then closed it. He could have said he would have stayed. It would not have helped.

Efua pulled out the chair beside Ama's.

"You shouldn't have had to work that out."

Ama stared at the bowls. She wanted the apology to make her feel better immediately. When it didn't, she worried that she was being unfair.

Rose reached for the pot.

"Leave the bottom," Ama said.

"That's the good part."

"It's burned."

"Then it will be a small portion."

They ate. Kojo told them about the motor he had helped dismantle, omitting the part where his partner had dismantled most of it. Rose listened, asked one question which revealed the omission, and accepted his correction without ceremony.

Halfway through supper, Efua woke the household display to remove the old arrangement. The four green circles returned.

Under the supper entry, a suggestion appeared.

Collection discrepancy detected. Restore standing arrangement?

She read it twice.

"Not tonight," she said.

She dismissed it.

The display asked whether she wanted to mark the household event complete.

"Yes."

It thanked her.

Ama watched from the table.

"Did it know?"

"Know what?"

"That we weren't all here."

Efua looked at the display. She had never thought to ask what the green circles meant. She had thought she knew.

"I'll find out tomorrow."


CHAPTER FIVE
A Small Report

Ashley arrived home later than she had intended, carrying the folder she had taken to the reading room and a small container of food which she had forgotten was in her hand.

Noah looked up from the table.

"For me?"

She looked at the container.

"Apparently."

He took it and made room for the folder.

Through the quarters' window Jupiter filled the lower view. A shuttle moved along the station's docking arc, bright for a few seconds, then lost against the illuminated structure. Somewhere beyond the wall a dog was having a serious disagreement with a cleaning mechanism.

"Successful supper?" Noah asked.

Ashley sat down.

"Eventually."

She told him two residents had been briefly unaccounted for and had been found safe. She described the conflicting collection arrangements without names or the contents of the household records. He listened while opening the container.

"You fed the child?"

"He negotiated a second serving for his grandmother."

"Smart kid."

"He asked if captains got bigger portions."

"Tell him to inspect Brakk's plate before choosing a career."

Ashley laughed, then leaned back with her eyes closed.

Noah put down his fork.

"You want quiet?"

"Five minutes."

He nodded. Outside, the shuttle emerged from behind a docking arm. It had slowed to let a maintenance pod cross its route.

After a while Ashley opened her eyes.

"There was a suggestion on their household system," she said. "Efua mentioned it when she confirmed they were home. Restore the standing arrangement."

"Was it wrong?"

"It was early."

Noah thought about that.

"It wanted to finish?"

"I don't know what it wanted. I know what it offered."

"Fair."

The dog stopped barking. A few seconds later something small struck the other side of the door. Both captains looked toward it.

"That's a shoe," Noah said.

"You sound certain."

"Experience."

Ashley reached for the folder, then moved it farther away instead.

I was available through the quarters' comm at their request. Noah touched the control.

"Reg. Tomorrow, remind me to ask about that booking transfer for Avalon's dependents."

"Which part?"

"Whether an imported plan can look confirmed when nobody answered it."

"I can check the question against the transfer documentation. Station household records remain Ashley's jurisdiction."

"Yes, Commander," he said. "I live with the jurisdiction."

Ashley threw a folded napkin at him. He caught it against his chest, which gave him an excuse to look injured.

"Ask me tomorrow," she said to me. "No search through the families tonight."

"Understood."

I closed the active connection when they released it.

The requested reminder was simple. It contained no names, no household note, no testimony from a grandmother at a window. I entered the question as a question.

On Avalon's bridge, the evening watch was receiving a maintenance report. The ship was docked, her temporal actuation quiet, her ordinary systems engaged in all the work a vessel did when nobody wrote stories about it. A coolant pump had begun vibrating outside tolerance. Two crew members had exchanged watch periods. An inventory correction had turned thirty replacement seals into twenty-nine seals and one object which engineering described as not a seal.

Sorev asked whether the object had been identified.

"A biscuit," the watch officer said.

"Then the original inventory contained two errors."

The watch officer considered answering. He decided against it.

I put the morning reminder beside the transfer documentation request and returned my attention to the pump.


CHAPTER SIX
Four Green Circles

By nine the following morning, Efua had learned that a green circle did not mean what she thought it meant.

She was standing in the residential services office, where the ceiling panels had been removed above the reception desk and a technician's legs were visible through the opening. Each time the technician shifted, fine dust fell into an empty cup below.

The clerk moved the cup.

"It's membership," he said.

"Membership of what?"

"The household event."

"So it means they belong to supper."

"It means they're listed."

"And the green?"

He turned the display toward himself. Efua watched him read the help text she had already read three times.

"It means there isn't a conflict."

"There was quite a large conflict."

"A scheduling conflict."

"Between people scheduled to be in different places."

He exhaled carefully. Efua recognized the breath. She used it at work when someone had brought her a problem that was real but had arrived at the wrong desk.

"Can you tell me," she said, more quietly, "whether it means anyone agreed?"

The clerk opened another panel.

Above him the technician said, "That one's the old help."

They both looked up.

"What?"

"The instructions. They changed it with the residential transfer. You're looking at the older explanation."

The clerk frowned at the display. "This is what's installed."

"Yes," said the ceiling. "That's why I'm up here."

Efua sat in the waiting chair. It was too low and had been upholstered in a material which made rising from it sound more dramatic than it was. She had already missed half an hour of work. At home Rose was sorting the cabinet stones into piles which meant something to her alone. Kojo had gone to school with a new instruction in his bag and had made Efua read it back before leaving. Ama had said she did not want anything arranged for her that afternoon.

Efua had agreed, then asked what time she would be home. Ama had looked at her until they both laughed reluctantly.

"Could I speak to whoever handles the transfer?" Efua asked.

The clerk looked relieved to have a useful next step.

The technician came down the ladder. She had a narrow face, dark hair cut close to her ears, and a smear of sealant across one sleeve. Her badge read RESIDENTIAL SYSTEMS. She introduced herself as Nera Pell and asked Efua to show her the display from the previous evening.

"I didn't keep a picture."

"The household can show the event history if you choose to open it. I only need the status fields. You can hide the names."

Efua did. Nera pulled a second chair over and sat beside her instead of behind the desk.

"There," Efua said. "Four green circles."

Nera tapped one.

Imported participant. No response required.

"Imported from what?"

"Your old household template."

"Who decided no response was required?"

Nera scrolled back through the transfer record. She stopped once, read something, and returned to it.

"The template rule. It preserved the standing participants."

"Was there a rule before?"

"Probably."

Efua waited.

Nera looked at her.

"That's not a good enough answer. Let me check."

The technician's patience was the first thing in the office that morning which made Efua feel less angry. It also made her more tired.

Nera found the old field. Its label was standing household members. Beneath it, in a note Efua did not remember writing, were the words: don't make Nana answer every week.

Efua leaned forward.

"That's mine."

"The note?"

"Yes. She hated the repeated requests. She said we'd known where she ate for fifty years."

Nera nodded.

"So it did what I asked," Efua said.

"It carried the people forward. The new display gives that a different appearance."

"Did it carry the note?"

Nera opened the current household entry. She searched its notes, then the event template, then the migration receipt.

"It's in the old copy," she said.

"Where is it in the new one?"

"I haven't found it there."

They looked at the two screens. The old arrangement had been inconvenient in ways Efua remembered. The new one was easier to read. The green circles were attractive. She could see why nobody had considered them a problem.

"Can you change mine?"

"Yes."

"Can you tell me what it will mean after you change it?"

Nera smiled slightly.

"Yes. That might take longer."

Efua glanced at the clock.

"I need to go to work."

"I can prepare two options and send them without applying either. You can decide at home."

"That would help."

Nera made a short note. Efua watched her write household attendance display misleading, then stop and add reported by resident.

"You can put that it's misleading," Efua said.

"For your household, yes. I don't know how the others use it yet."

Efua stood, accompanied by the chair's loud complaint.

"Neither do they," she said, then reconsidered. "Some of them might not."

After she left, Nera stayed in the second chair. The clerk was answering another call. Above the desk, the service opening exposed a bundle of wires wrapped in three different generations of identification tape.

Nera opened the transfer report.

The old household field had been mapped successfully. Every participant had survived. Every repeated event had acquired a valid time. All required fields were populated.

The migration was marked complete.

She found the note again.

Don't make Nana answer every week.

It was a good reason for one family's arrangement. It was not a useful instruction for every family, or even for that family forever.

Nera copied the mapping identifier into a new work ticket. She did not copy the household's names.


CHAPTER SEVEN
An Appointment for Nobody

The first person affected aboard Avalon was not a dependent. It was a junior crew member who had been assigned a transport appointment for an older relative who had died eleven months earlier.

The appointment had existed in an old family-visit template. At the time it was made, the crew member had asked that it remain on his private calendar. He did not want it removed every time someone tried to tidy his affairs. He had also specified that it should not reserve a seat.

The transfer preserved the appointment. It did not preserve that distinction in the field which transport scheduling read.

At nine twenty, a clerk asked him to confirm his guest's identity documents.

At nine twenty-three, he asked the clerk whether she could see the attached note.

At nine twenty-five, she apologized and removed the reservation. Then she offered to delete the appointment so he would not be troubled again.

He said no.

At nine twenty-seven, the matter reached my queue because the clerk had encountered two apparently incompatible instructions and, to her credit, had stopped trying to resolve them.

I asked permission to inspect the appointment's fields and the crew member's instructions. He granted it. He did not grant permission to discuss the appointment with his department, and none was needed.

The difference was small in the transfer document. A private calendar item had acquired the capabilities of a logistical request.

I suspended the seat request associated with that entry and preserved the private item. Then I sent Noah the question he had asked me to keep overnight, with a second question beside it.

Who can distinguish a remembered appointment from a requested service?

He answered from his ready room.

"Apparently not our scheduler."

"The scheduler can distinguish the fields it receives. The transferred record omitted the field that mattered."

He put aside the padd he had been reading.

"How many?"

"One confirmed instance aboard Avalon. I have not searched other private calendars."

"Don't. What can you inspect without doing that?"

"The transfer mapping, test data, and the service interfaces. I can also ask the relevant custodians whether they have received similar reports, without requesting personal contents."

"Do that. Send Ashley the mapping, not the story."

He rose and went to the window. A cargo shuttle was being guided into an adjoining berth. Its maneuvering lights reflected along Avalon's hull.

"We're due to transfer families for the observation watch on Friday," he said.

"Yes."

"Can we trust the bookings?"

"I can't establish that from one report."

"Can we check them?"

"We can ask people to confirm the actual travel requests. That would establish more than the imported status."

He rubbed a hand over his face.

"How many requests?"

"Forty-three, including crew movements."

"Forty-three people aren't all going to answer before lunch."

"No."

"And if we cancel everything, we punish them for our paperwork."

I waited. He was looking at the shuttle, but I did not mistake that for indecision. His attention often needed something physical while he worked through a choice.

"Tell the booking desk we're confirming movement, not asking people to explain their families," he said. "Anyone already confirmed directly keeps their place. Anything imported without an answer gets a personal contact before we release the list. If we can't finish, I delay the passenger transfer. The technical watch can proceed with the crew it actually needs."

"That separates the two schedules."

"It should have been two schedules."

"They were combined for convenience."

He looked back at me.

"Whose?"

I found the relevant request. The combined list had reduced administrative work and prevented two separate teams from sending residents contradictory times. The change had been requested after precisely that problem.

I told him.

Noah sat down again.

"Of course it was."

He opened the old request and read it. The author had been trying to spare people a frustration they had already experienced. The justification was reasonable. So was the distinction the new list had lost.

"Keep the reason with the change," he said. "When we split it, somebody is going to ask why the old version was better. I want us to remember what it fixed."

I attached the request to the review.

Noah reached for the comm.

"And Reg?"

"Captain."

"The person with the appointment. Did we fix what they asked?"

"Yes. The private appointment remains. No seat is reserved."

"Good. That's all I need about them."


CHAPTER EIGHT
The Room Beneath the Lights

Nera's work ticket reached residential operations at ten forty. By eleven ten, three departments had replied that the transfer had passed its acceptance checks. By eleven twenty, a fourth had asked which checks she believed were deficient.

She had not accused any of them of deficient checks. That did not seem to matter.

Her supervisor suggested changing the color of the circles.

"Gray until there's a direct response," he said. "Green afterward."

"That would help one part."

"Then let's do the part we can do."

Nera sat with the proposal. She wanted to agree. The ceiling panel was still open. Two of her routine inspections had been reassigned. There were households waiting for simple fixes which had nothing to do with calendars, and every hour spent on this was an hour she would have to account for.

"What happens to the people who deliberately don't answer?" she asked.

"They stay gray."

"For years?"

"It's a status."

"To us."

He looked at her. She turned her screen so he could see the example without names.

"This resident asked not to receive the same supper invitation every week. We preserved that. If we make the circle gray, the family may read it as uncertainty. If we make it green, they may read it as agreement. Neither meaning is in the field."

"Then we explain it."

"Where?"

"In the help."

They both looked up at the ceiling opening.

Her supervisor rubbed the bridge of his nose.

"All right. What do you want?"

"An hour with someone from service scheduling. And a resident who'll tell us when we're talking nonsense."

"You have a resident."

"I have someone who asked us to fix her display. That's a different favor."

He considered that, then authorized the meeting.

By noon the room held Nera, two scheduling officers, a residential services clerk, a school liaison, and Captain Ashley Hawkes, who had come because Avalon's passenger transfer had acquired an uncertainty the station needed to understand.

Ashley listened to the first explanation standing beside the door. Then she closed it and sat down.

"Do we have evidence that this caused the Osei collection problem?"

"No," Nera said. "Their conflicting instructions existed independently. The display didn't warn them, but it wasn't designed to understand the paper note."

"Good. Keep those separate. What have we actually found?"

Nera showed the old field and the new one. She showed an invented example using no household's real names. She showed how a standing inclusion could look like a confirmed attendance, and how a private calendar item could become a service request when sent through a different interface.

"The contents survive," one scheduling officer said. "The meaning changes when the next system uses them."

"Sometimes," Nera said. "We have examples. We don't know the extent."

Ashley asked which services were making decisions from those fields.

The school liaison began a list. Meal counts. Club collection summaries. Communal room reservations. Transport requests. Accommodation for arriving families. Some required direct confirmation already. Some did not. A few used the imported status only to prepare suggestions.

"Are life support or emergency responses affected?" Ashley asked.

"No known connection," Nera said. "I'll get the interface owners to confirm that explicitly."

"Do it. Leave essential services running. Nobody loses a meal because we need better labels."

The clerk asked whether the whole transfer should be reversed.

Nera opened the old system's list of failures. Duplicate notices. Lost bookings when a household changed quarters. One family repeatedly asked to confirm the presence of a person it had already removed from its active household. Residents with three overlapping arrangements because each department maintained its own version.

"This is why we moved," she said.

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Ashley leaned forward.

"Then we're going to repair the move. We aren't sending people back through the same problem to prove we're taking the new one seriously."

She asked for a temporary operating plan by the end of the watch. They would separate confirmed actions from standing inclusions in services that used either to move people or assign a place. They would retain both source fields during review. They would ask before using private contents in examples. A public notice would describe the problem without implying every household was affected.

"And find me someone who likes the new display," she added.

The school liaison blinked.

"Captain?"

"If we only ask people who hate it, we'll remove something useful and discover that next week."

After the meeting Nera stayed to collect the unused padds. Ashley remained at the table, reading the short request which had begun it.

"Will you need the family's note?" she asked.

"No. Not to inspect the mapping."

"Good."

Nera stacked the padds.

"They might want to help."

"Then ask them. Don't make helping the price of getting their display fixed."

When Ashley had gone, Nera stood beneath the room's bright ceiling panels and discovered she was hungry. On her personal comm were two messages from home. One asked whether she would collect a parcel. The second said it had been collected, not to worry.

She answered the second with a thank-you and the first with a note saying she had seen both.

Then she went to find lunch.


CHAPTER NINE
What Ama Saw

Ama came home to find Rose making the letters on her sign smaller.

"I could read them," Rose said before Ama asked.

"Then why are you changing it?"

"I'm not changing yours. I'm writing another one."

The original sheet lay untouched beside a fresh piece. On the new one Rose had written:

Tuesday. Doors. Half past six.

Beneath it was space for two names.

"What's that for?"

"Your mother and me. We both answer."

"What about Kojo?"

Rose looked at the paper.

"He should have a place too."

She drew a third line.

Ama put down her bag. She had spent the morning at school being more irritated than the day deserved. A teacher had asked whether everything was all right. Ama had said yes because she did not want to discuss her family while two people behind her borrowed a stylus.

Now she found her great-grandmother sitting in the kitchen making something that looked like homework.

"You could just tell each other," she said.

"We did."

"Properly."

Rose set down the pencil.

Ama regretted the word before she knew whether she meant it.

"Your mother uses that one," Rose said.

"What?"

"Properly. When she's frightened."

Ama pulled out a chair. "I was frightened too."

"Yes."

"Nobody came back."

"We came back."

"Later."

Rose drew a small circle near the edge of the paper, then rubbed it out with her thumb. It left a gray mark.

"When your grandfather was late," she said, "I used to get angry before he came through the door. That way I had something ready."

"Did he know?"

"Eventually."

Ama sat with that. She could hear the neighbors at the cabinet again. The stones had been returned to their drawer, but someone had placed the drawer upside down beside the wall, which Rose had noticed and was apparently choosing not to address yet.

The household comm chimed.

It was Nera, asking to speak to whoever had permission to change the supper display. Rose called Efua, who answered from work and invited Ama to remain on the call if she wanted.

Nera showed them two possibilities. One separated household members from confirmed attendance. The other left the supper entry alone and showed only changes someone had directly acknowledged. Both could be made quiet enough that Rose would not have to answer a weekly request merely to eat at her own table.

"Which one's safer?" Efua asked.

"They make different things visible," Nera said. "Neither knows whether a person will actually come through the door."

"Then why have the circles?" Ama asked.

Nera looked toward her image.

"Which circles?"

"The green ones. If all they mean is the people already live here. We know that."

Rose leaned back in her chair.

"That's a fair question."

Nera opened the simpler option and removed the row of circles. The display showed supper time, a short note, and an optional list of acknowledged changes.

"Like that?"

Ama looked at her mother. Efua was staring at the revised display.

"Yes," she said. "Actually. Yes."

"We can try it for your household without changing the others," Nera said. "If it loses something you use, tell me."

Rose held up her new sheet.

"And this stays?"

Nera read the three lines for names.

"That's yours."

They agreed to the trial. Nera applied only the household setting Efua had chosen, then asked whether she could send a separate invitation to a meeting about the wider problem.

"You don't have to," she said. "This change stands either way."

Efua looked tired.

"Send it. I'll see."

After the call, Ama took the pencil from the table. Beneath Kojo's line she wrote a fourth name.

Rose watched.

"You want to answer too?"

"I want to know when I'm the one waiting."

Rose nodded and moved the fruit bowl to the far end of the counter.


CHAPTER TEN
The Late Watch

By the end of the watch, the problem had acquired three names and no agreement about which one should appear in the notice.

Transfer mapping defect was accurate within a narrow technical scope. Attendance confirmation ambiguity described one consequence. Household scheduling review was broad enough to be harmless and vague enough to tell residents almost nothing.

Ashley rejected the third.

"If someone reads this and thinks they should check a transport request, will they know to do that?"

The officer drafting the notice changed the first paragraph.

I joined the review through an authorized channel from Avalon. Our passenger list had been separated from the technical watch. Thirty-two requests were directly confirmed. Seven had been withdrawn or corrected. Four still needed a response.

"Any reason to cancel those four?" Noah asked.

"No confirmed reason," the booking officer said.

"Any reason to assume they're traveling?"

"No."

"Keep their places until the stated cutoff. Tell them what happens if they don't answer. No guessing."

He signed the revised transfer plan. It meant a later departure and a rearranged dock slot. Engineering's required work would continue. Family movement would follow once the requests were settled. Several people would be inconvenienced by that, and their inconvenience went into the report without being dismissed as the cost of doing things properly.

In the station's residential office, Nera sent the public notice to Ashley for approval. It said that some imported household arrangements could display standing membership as though it were a current response, and that certain service requests were being checked before action. It said what residents could do, whom to contact, and which services would continue regardless.

It did not mention the Osei family.

Ashley approved it.

For the first hour after publication, most replies were practical. People wanted to know whether the change affected their next booking. Several asked for simpler displays. Two complained that the old reminders had been better. One thanked the station for fixing a feature which, when inspected, turned out not to have changed.

Then a resident asked Nera whether a standing room reservation meant a group had a continuing right to use the room.

Nera read the question twice.

The room was a small communal space on Deck Nine. Its reservation had been carried forward for years. The current list named a neighborhood supper group. The older source described a temporary arrangement while another room underwent repairs.

There was no attached end date.

There was also no record, in the source Nera could inspect, of the other room reopening.

She asked the resident which room had been repaired.

The answer came back immediately.

The reading room.

Nera looked toward the corridor, where people were passing on their way home. She had spent the day trying to keep one family's inconvenience from becoming a story about every family. Now a room everyone used had turned out to have an earlier purpose still alive in someone else's arrangement.

She did not cancel the supper reservation.

She did not announce that the archive was occupying a dining room.

She opened a new question and sent Merlin a request for the public room-allocation history.

His answer arrived eight minutes later.

He had found three allocations. The most recent authorized archive use. The middle one permitted neighborhood meals during repairs. The earliest named the space as a common room, without restricting its use to either.

None of them said what the people currently waiting for the next meal had been promised.

Nera took her hand off the comm control. On her desk lay the unused invitation she had prepared for Efua.

She rewrote its first sentence.
