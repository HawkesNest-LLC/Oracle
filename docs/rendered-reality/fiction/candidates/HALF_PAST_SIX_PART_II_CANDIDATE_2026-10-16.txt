CHAPTER ELEVEN
An Invitation Without a Name

The invitation arrived during supper, which Ama considered evidence that nobody at residential services had learned anything.

Her mother left it unopened until they had cleared the table.

This was worse. Now the invitation had time to become important.

Kojo offered to read it first. Nobody accepted. He took a piece of bread from the board and went to put the motor from his club bag on the table, where Efua stopped him with a look.

"It's clean."

"It's shedding things."

"Those are separate."

Rose moved her blue bowl away from the small silver dusting appearing beneath his sleeve.

"Then separate them somewhere else."

He left, offended but chewing.

Efua opened the message. It invited household members to a discussion about how standing arrangements appeared in station services. It said their display change would remain in place whether or not they attended. It said there would be no need to discuss personal records. It also asked whether they wished to contribute a description of any experience which might help the review understand the practical effects.

"That's us," Ama said.

"It's an invitation."

"They're asking about Tuesday."

Efua reread the line. "Yes. They might be."

"They are."

Rose reached for the message. Efua passed her the padd.

"They don't know what happened in this kitchen," Ama said.

"No," Efua answered.

"So what would you tell them?"

Her mother looked up.

"I haven't decided."

"Would you tell them I cried?"

The kitchen became very quiet. Ama had not cried where anyone could see. She had cried into a folded cloth while the pot cooled and then been angry with herself for using the cloth they would need for the dishes.

Efua put down the padd.

"Did you?"

Ama wished the question had not been so gentle.

"I don't want them knowing."

"Then I won't tell them."

"You just found out."

"Yes."

Rose turned the message off and set it facedown.

Ama felt better for half a second. Then she saw her own sign beside the fruit bowl. THE PERSON was still darker than the rest because she had pressed harder over the correction.

"But if nobody tells them," she said, "they'll think it was only a wrong time on a screen."

Efua pulled out a chair.

"I can tell them what happened to me. I won't tell your part for you."

"They'll ask why you were worried."

"I can answer that without describing you."

"And if they ask what I was doing?"

"I'll say that's yours to tell."

Ama sat down opposite her. She was relieved and disappointed. Part of her had wanted her mother to say the decision had already been made, even if that gave her something obvious to resist.

Rose got up to put the kettle on. Neither of them asked her to stay. She remained within hearing anyway, measuring tea with greater precision than the tea required.

"Do I have to go?" Ama asked.

"No."

"Would you like me to?"

Efua considered the question instead of reaching for the easy answer.

"I'd like you to know you could speak if you wanted. I don't need you there to make my account convincing."

Ama traced a mark on the tabletop with one finger.

"I might write something."

"All right."

"That isn't yes."

"I heard might."

Rose laughed once beside the kettle. Ama looked at her, then at her mother, and found she could laugh too without deciding anything else.


CHAPTER TWELVE
The People Who Ate There

Merlin knew the supper room by its booking label and the smell of onions which sometimes reached the archive through the ventilation return. He had not known who cut them.

The woman who came to see him on Thursday carried a pan wrapped in cloth. She put it on the visitor's table and asked where the heat-safe mat had gone.

"I'm not sure we've had one," he said.

"You have. Blue. One corner burned."

He opened the cupboard beneath the table. The mat was behind a stack of unused folders.

"There," she said. "Same room."

Her name was Hessa Vale. She had been organizing the neighborhood supper for nine years. Before that she had attended without organizing it, a distinction she insisted was important because organizing a meal and being allowed to enjoy one were different occupations.

Merlin set the mat down. She unwrapped the pan. It held six pieces of something dense and golden, cut unevenly.

"The corner ones are better," she said. "Unless you dislike the corners, in which case don't tell me."

He thanked her and asked whether the food was for a meeting.

"It's for eating. The meeting's later."

They sat at the visitor's table. Hessa looked around the reading room. Her gaze stopped at the cabinets along the inner wall, then at the broad table where Merlin worked, then at the quiet alcove behind a privacy screen.

"You've made it good," she said.

He was not certain whether to thank her again.

"I read the allocation history," she continued. "The part Nera sent me. The meal group moved because this room needed repairs. We were told we'd come back when it was ready."

"The record I found authorizes the move during repairs. It doesn't state a return date."

"I didn't say there was a date."

Merlin lowered his stylus.

"No. You didn't."

Hessa broke a small piece from one corner. "We thought it would be a few weeks. The smaller room was adequate. You can make a lot of things adequate if you think they're temporary."

She described the tables. They had to be set at an angle so the door would open. One chair could not be occupied until everyone had passed it. The sink was shallow. Carrying hot dishes required moving through the same narrow route people used to reach the replicator.

Merlin had been to two neighborhood meals there. He remembered being offered a chair which everyone else had to get past. He had thought it was the courtesy of placing a guest near the door.

"Why didn't you request another room?"

"We did."

"I haven't found that request."

"It might have been a conversation."

He waited.

"I know," Hessa said. "That doesn't help you."

"It helps me know where the written record stops."

She considered him, then nodded.

"It wasn't bad every week. People had children. People moved. Some stopped coming. You couldn't say which change was the room. After a while the number attending became the reason we didn't need a larger one."

Merlin looked at the quiet alcove. That morning someone had used it to read a message they did not want to open in their quarters. They had thanked him for the place before leaving. He had been pleased to have it to offer.

"The archive is using this room well," Hessa said. "That's what makes it difficult."

He was grateful she said it and wished she hadn't needed to.

"What are you asking for now?"

"Enough space to put the table straight. I don't mind which space. I mind being told the only people who need a chair are the ones still coming."

Merlin wrote that down, then showed her the sentence.

She read it and tapped the word only.

"Keep that one. It's the part that makes me cross."

After she left, he took a corner piece from the pan. It was still faintly warm. He ate standing beside the cupboard, looking at the blue mat which had remained in the room longer than either use had been properly explained.


CHAPTER THIRTEEN
The Account She Wrote

Ama's first version began with the rice.

She wrote it in the hour between homework and the time she normally started pretending she was going to bed. She described the sound of the pot and the four bowls. She wrote that she had been angry at Kojo before he came home because being angry at him felt less frightening than wondering where he was.

She deleted that sentence.

Then she restored it.

The writing was on her personal padd. She had turned off sharing, though it had not been on. She checked again after the first paragraph.

Kojo knocked and came in too quickly for the knock to count.

"Are you using the cutter?"

Ama tilted the padd against her chest.

"For what?"

"The paper one."

"Why?"

"I'm making something."

"Is it for club?"

He considered this. "Eventually."

She told him where it was and made him promise to put it back. He spotted the writing before he left.

"Are you doing homework about us?"

"No."

"Good. They'd mark it wrong."

He went out. Ama looked at the paragraph on her padd and laughed despite herself. Then she wrote his sentence below it, in a separate note, because it belonged to him.

She finished an account of the evening. It was longer than she expected. The green circles appeared in the middle, after the phone call, where she had actually looked at them. On the first pass she had put them at the beginning because she knew the meeting was about the display. Moving them back made the story feel more embarrassing. It also made it more like what had happened.

She sent it to her mother with the subject DON'T SEND THIS.

Efua came to the doorway ten minutes later.

"May I?"

Ama nodded. Her mother sat on the edge of the bed with the padd held flat on her lap.

"I didn't know about the cloth."

Ama looked away.

"You weren't there."

"No."

They sat without talking. A cart passed in the corridor. Somewhere nearby a door kept trying to close against an obstruction, paused, tried again, and was finally rescued by someone speaking to it as if it were a stubborn animal.

"I don't want to send it," Ama said.

"Then we won't."

"But I wrote it."

"You can keep something you wrote."

"I know."

She disliked being given permission for something she already knew was allowed. She needed the permission anyway. The two feelings sat beside each other and refused to become one sensible feeling.

Efua looked at the subject line.

"Do you want me to keep my copy?"

Ama had not thought that far ahead.

"Yes. For now."

"All right."

"Not to show Nana."

"All right."

Ama took her padd back and opened a new document. She wrote three sentences.

I looked at the green circles while I was waiting. I thought they meant somebody knew we were coming. I didn't know they only meant we were listed.

She read them aloud.

"Is that true?" Efua asked.

Ama stared at the second sentence. She had not thought the exact words somebody knew. That was what it had felt like when she remembered it later.

She replaced I thought with They made me feel like.

Her mother said nothing.

Ama read it again. She deleted the second sentence entirely.

"Now it's useless."

"It's shorter."

"You know what I mean."

Efua did know, which was why she waited.

Ama put back a different sentence.

I didn't understand what they meant, but I thought they were good news.

She sat looking at it for a long time.

"I could send that."

"You could."

"They'd know it was me."

"Nera would. If she quoted it without your name, people who knew the evening might still recognize it."

Ama was grateful her mother did not call that anonymous.

She closed the new document.

"No. Not yet."

Efua stood.

"I'll answer the invitation for myself. You can change your mind later. Or keep it the way it is."

At the door she paused.

"The cloth washed clean."

Ama looked up sharply.

Her mother put a hand against the door frame.

"That wasn't the important part. I know. I just thought you might want to know that too."

After she left, Ama reopened the first account. She did not change the frightened part. She changed the date, which she had entered wrong because she had started writing after midnight by the station display and had meant the evening before.

Then she put it away.


CHAPTER FOURTEEN
A Place on the List

The last unanswered passenger request reached the booking desk twenty-three minutes before the cutoff.

It was a refusal.

The resident had arranged a different journey after receiving the revised time. The new journey cost her a day with a relative and an overnight stay she had not expected. She wanted the station to know that the request had been a real request, even though she was withdrawing it now.

The booking officer marked the cancellation and added the resident's explanation to the service review with permission. The available seat went to a person on the waiting list who had been ready since morning.

Noah read the outcome summary in the ready room.

"Did the first passenger get an answer?"

"The desk acknowledged the cancellation."

"About the lost day."

I checked.

"Not yet."

He put down the summary.

"Send it to me with the contact permission. I can apologize for our delay without telling her she ought to be happy the list is right."

The station's revised dock slot was confirmed at sixteen hundred. Avalon would move early the next morning. The temporal actuation exercise had been reduced to a technical watch; the invited families would join a conventional observation period afterward, subject to their confirmed requests. Nobody was being asked to treat a family outing as consent to an experiment.

Brakk wanted two additional hours for the coolant pump inspection. He asked for three because, as he explained to Noah, captains tended to find a use for the spare one.

"Then ask for four," Noah said.

"I did. Yesterday."

Sorev read the work plan and observed that departure time was not a useful measure of readiness unless the ship was ready.

Brakk said he would have that put on a plaque and mount it over the pump.

"Preferably after inspection," Sorev replied.

I passed the engineering request through the ordinary scheduling channel. Noah approved the available dock extension and went to record his message to the passenger.

At the station, Ashley was reviewing the room dispute. The earliest allocation described a common room. The next described temporary meal arrangements. The most recent authorized archive use. Each could be read as reasonable in the circumstances it documented. None described how the smaller supper room now functioned with a cart, a mobility chair, and three people carrying hot pans through the same narrow entrance.

"We've been asking which paper wins," Ashley said.

Nera stood beside the display with Hessa's seating sketch open.

"They don't answer the same question."

"What can we find out before tomorrow's supper?"

"We can measure the routes. Ask who actually needs to attend. Inspect the nearby rooms."

"Do it with the people who use them. No surprise relocation."

Merlin had sent an account of the reading room's current uses. It included appointments which needed quiet, storage which could not be moved casually, and open hours which residents relied upon. He had also identified activities which could be scheduled elsewhere without compromising custody.

At the bottom he had written: I can work from a different desk. The residents may need this room for reasons which do not fit an appointment.

Ashley called him.

"Don't give away the alcove before we've asked the people who use it."

"I wasn't intending to."

"Your note sounds as if you might."

He read it again.

"I can change that."

"You can keep it. Just remember you aren't the only person who gets something from your desk being there."

After the call, Merlin looked across the reading room. An elderly man was asleep over a history he had brought from home. He came every Thursday because his quarters faced an interior corridor and he liked the afternoon light. Nobody had entered that reason on his appointment.

Merlin left him asleep.


CHAPTER FIFTEEN
The Table at an Angle

Ama went to the supper room because Hessa needed someone to hold the far end of the measuring tape.

She told her mother this carefully. She was not attending the review. She was helping measure a room. Efua said she understood, and Ama asked her to explain the difference back.

"You're doing a job there. You aren't offering your account of Tuesday."

"Yes."

"And if somebody asks?"

"I'll tell them."

That was satisfactory. Ama left before either of them could make it into a larger conversation.

The room smelled of soap and the dry warmth of the replicator housing. Hessa had moved the chairs against one wall. The table looked larger without people around it, although the floor had not gained any space.

Nera stood in the doorway with a roll of temporary marking tape. Beside her was a man called Daven who used a mobility chair and had already refused an offer to demonstrate how difficult the entrance was.

"I'll show you how I come to supper," he had said. "You can describe the difficult part yourself."

Ama took the tape from Hessa and crouched by the table's far leg. Nera asked before marking the floor. They measured the clear route, then the route with a chair pulled out, then the space needed for Daven to turn without somebody lifting a table corner.

"We normally move that one," Hessa said.

"Who moves it?" Nera asked.

"Whoever's sitting there."

"And if they've got a hot bowl?"

"We wait."

Nera made a note. Daven watched without comment.

Ama extended the tape to the wall. The end slipped, snapped back, and struck her knuckle.

"Ow."

"Hold the body against the floor," Daven said. "Let the tape move under your thumb."

She tried it. He was right.

The work was satisfying. It involved numbers which stayed where she put them. Nobody asked what the numbers had made her feel.

After twenty minutes they put the chairs back and asked two volunteers to carry empty pans from the serving counter. Daven entered as he normally would. One volunteer turned sideways. Hessa lifted a chair. A pan touched the door frame with a loud hollow sound.

"That's Tuesday," Hessa said.

"Every week?" Ama asked.

"When everyone's here."

"Who doesn't come?"

Hessa started naming people, then stopped.

"Some don't come for reasons which aren't mine to explain. We can ask whether they'd like to."

Ama looked at the table. She had been to meals in this room with Rose. She remembered a woman leaving early with a child asleep against her neck. She had assumed they were tired. Perhaps they had been. Perhaps leaving before the chairs were fully occupied had made it possible to leave at all.

Nera asked whether she could keep Ama's measurements with the inspection. Ama said yes, then asked if that put her on the meeting list.

"Not unless you want it to."

"I don't."

"All right."

The answer came so quickly that Ama searched Nera's face for disappointment. There was none she could see. Nera was trying to peel a piece of tape from her sleeve without pulling a loose thread.

Hessa brought out drinks. Daven accepted water and a chair was offered to Nera, who sat on the floor because the remaining upright chair held the measuring equipment.

Rose arrived with the cabinet stones in a cloth bag.

Ama stared at her.

"What are those for?"

"Hessa asked for weights to hold the cloths when the service vent's running."

"You could have said that before you came carrying rocks."

"I thought I'd let you enjoy asking."

She put the bag down and looked at the strips of tape on the floor.

"Does it fit?"

"Not well," Hessa said.

Rose placed a hand on the nearest table edge. "It never did."

"You could have told someone," Ama said, then heard the sentence and wished she had chosen another.

Rose looked at her. She did not look away.

"I told Hessa. She told someone. Then we all kept having supper."

"Why?"

"Because supper was still on."

Ama held the tape measure in both hands. She had thought a problem either got fixed or stayed a problem. The room had been doing both for years.

Nera finished removing the tape from her sleeve.

"There's another room along the service corridor," she said. "Bigger. No food service, and the access route isn't obvious. We can inspect it tomorrow."

Hessa asked about hand washing, storage, and how hot food would get there. Daven asked whether the route included the narrow door near the lift. Nera wrote down the questions instead of calling the room an answer.

Rose sat in the newly cleared space by the table.

"It's been a very educational hour," she said.

"Only forty minutes," Ama told her.

"I've learned quickly."


CHAPTER SIXTEEN
Permission to Quote

The sentence Ama had not sent appeared in no report.

She checked the resident notice twice to make sure. Nothing about rice. Nothing about waiting in a kitchen. There was no photograph of her sign and no account of anyone crying into a cloth.

She ought to have been satisfied.

Instead she found herself reading the notice with the obscure annoyance of someone who had remained silent and then discovered that the conversation had continued.

Nera's inspection summary described the supper-room route clearly. It had a little plan showing the table at an angle, the door swing, and the space for turning. Ama's measurements were there, credited to resident volunteers without names, as she had agreed. They were useful. They were also not what Tuesday had been like.

She took the padd to the kitchen. Rose was peeling something which had been replicated with an edible skin and which she peeled anyway because she preferred it that way.

"It sounds tidy," Ama said.

"What does?"

Ama showed her.

Rose read the summary while holding the knife away from the screen.

"It's a room measurement."

"I know."

"It should sound tidy. Otherwise they'd have measured it badly."

Ama took the padd back.

"That's not what I meant."

Rose put down the knife.

"Then tell me."

Ama sat on the counter stool. "I said I didn't want them knowing everything."

"Yes."

"But now it looks like nothing happened."

Rose considered the statement for longer than Ama expected.

"It doesn't look like nothing happened to me," she said. "I was there when you came home from measuring. You were pleased."

"I'm talking about before."

"I know. There are more people who remember before than the ones writing this report."

Ama looked down at the screen.

"What if they forget?"

Rose reached for the knife, then left it on the board.

"I can't promise they won't."

Ama had expected one of the reassuring things adults said when they wanted a question finished. Without it, she had to stay with the question herself.

"I wrote it down," she said.

"Then you've got it."

"Mum's got it too."

"Good."

Rose resumed peeling. The skin fell in one long strip until it broke. She put both pieces into the compost receptacle as if they were equally acceptable results.

Ama opened a new message to Nera.

It took her twelve minutes to write four lines.

I don't want to send my account of Tuesday. Please don't ask my mum for it. You can use the measurements I helped with. If you write that residents helped, that's enough for me.

She showed Efua before sending it, because she had named her and wanted her to know. Efua read it without suggesting a warmer ending.

Nera answered after her watch.

Understood. Your account won't be requested through your mother. The measurements stay within the inspection as agreed. Thank you for helping with those.

Ama read the answer three times.

"Is that all?" Kojo asked from the doorway.

"What?"

"You made the noise."

"What noise?"

He imitated her exhale badly enough to be annoying and accurately enough to be worse.

She threw a cushion at him. He caught it, delighted to have become involved in whatever had been happening.

"Yes," she said. "That's all."

She saved Nera's reply beside her private account. The reply did not explain the evening. It was useful for a different reason.


CHAPTER SEVENTEEN
The Person Who Liked It

The resident Ashley had asked them to find arrived at the review with a list of everything he liked about the new system and a separate list of things he feared they were about to remove.

His name was Ilan Sen. He worked rotating service shifts. His household's old calendar had sent repeated notices during his sleeping hours. The new display let him see changes at a glance without waking a voice interface or opening four different messages.

"The circles aren't the problem," he said. "If you make me read a paragraph to find out whether the meal changed, I won't. I'll look later. Later is how I miss it."

Nera asked him what he understood a green circle to mean.

"No change."

"Not confirmed?"

"Why would it mean confirmed?"

Across the table Efua made a small sound. Ilan looked at her.

"That's what I thought," she said. "That people had answered."

"But they live there."

"They don't always eat there."

He opened his mouth, then looked back at his list.

"Right."

Nobody had been foolish. Nera found that fact less comforting than it should have been. Foolishness could be corrected by explaining the thing someone had missed. Here, two reasonable people had used the same clear display to read different clear meanings.

Ashley asked whether the review could preserve both useful arrangements without pretending one household's interpretation was universal.

The scheduling officer showed a revised prototype. It separated the household roster from current responses. A household could choose to display changes quietly. Actual service requests had their own status and could not inherit a confirmed response merely because a name had been carried forward.

"What happens to old reasons?" Efua asked.

The officer showed a note field attached to the arrangement's source history.

"That isn't on the main display," Efua said.

"No."

"Then someone changing it might not read it."

"We can require the person applying a change to inspect the attached reason."

Ilan leaned forward. "Every change? Even if I move supper ten minutes?"

Nera looked at the prototype. It was becoming more correct and harder to use in front of her eyes.

Ashley set down her stylus.

"We're designing an arrangement while sitting still with nothing else happening. They use it while cooking, working, sleeping, and getting children out of doors."

She asked them to stop adding features for the next twenty minutes. Instead, each household representative would walk through one ordinary change. No explanation from the designers unless the person asked.

Efua moved supper. Ilan changed a shift. Hessa tried to invite a new neighbor to one meal without enrolling her in every future meal. Daven withdrew from an event while asking to remain on the group contact list.

Three operations worked. The fourth silently removed the group membership.

Daven pointed at it.

"That. I've done that before. I thought they stopped inviting me."

Hessa stared at him.

"We thought you didn't want to come."

Neither spoke for several seconds. The scheduling officer took his hands away from the controls.

"Can we restore it?" Nera asked Daven.

"Yes."

"The contact membership only?"

"Yes. Don't enroll me in everything to apologize."

Hessa laughed softly, then wiped one eye with her thumb as if it were irritated.

Efua watched them. She had come prepared to talk about Tuesday without giving Ama's account. She had feared that would make her contribution smaller than the problem deserved. Across the table was a different evening, and years of possible evenings, carried in two short sentences.

They did not need Ama to make the room listen.

After the meeting Hessa caught Efua by the door.

"Would she like to come tomorrow? Your girl. To the meal. Not the review."

"I'll ask her."

"Tell her we could use her with the table. If she wants."

Efua smiled tiredly.

"She'll hear the if."


CHAPTER EIGHTEEN
Two Doors

The alternative room had good floor space and a door nobody liked.

It opened onto a service corridor used for cart movements during the early evening. The room itself had been a training space. One wall still carried a diagram of a pump whose current replacement was two generations newer. The tables folded. The lighting was excellent. There was no sink.

Hessa stood in the center and counted places silently.

"Enough?" Nera asked.

"For people."

"For supper?"

"Not yet."

Daven tested the route from the lift while a services worker moved an empty cart through its normal path. They passed, but only after one waited in a recess. Ama marked the delay on the sketch because Nera had asked her to measure what happened, not what ought to happen.

Rose inspected the folding tables and pronounced them acceptable in the cautious voice she used when she expected furniture to prove her wrong later.

Merlin came down from the reading room carrying its weekly use schedule. He had asked the current users which parts depended on that particular room. Most wanted quiet. Some needed secure materials. One needed a place where a companion could wait without overhearing a private consultation. The man who slept over his history had said he could sleep somewhere else, then asked whether somewhere else would have a window.

"I'm not moving him to a cupboard," Merlin told Nera.

"Nobody's asked you to."

"I know. I'm discovering what I was about to offer."

They laid the two schedules side by side on a folding table. The archive room could support occasional meals if records were secured and furniture moved. That would take work each time and interrupt services some residents needed. The training room could support larger meals if the serving route and washing arrangements were addressed. That would require equipment and a safe cart schedule, neither of which existed merely because people wanted them.

Hessa looked from one schedule to the other.

"Tomorrow's meal still needs a room."

"The current reservation stands," Nera said. "Nobody's canceled it."

"Then we're back at the angled table."

Ama looked at the plan she had drawn. The old room's furniture made a shape she could remember with her eyes closed. She had measured the troublesome corner three times. A serving counter stood against the longest wall, although food was now prepared outside the room and brought in. Most of its cupboards held things Hessa had forgotten were there.

"Could that come out?" she asked.

Nera followed her finger.

"The counter?"

Hessa frowned. "We'd have nowhere to put the pans."

"There's the cart."

"In the door."

"Not if the counter's gone."

They returned to the old room. Nera checked the counter's service connections before anyone touched it. Its original heating elements had been disconnected years earlier. The washing point was separate. The unit was bolted to the wall and could be moved by a facilities team, but not by four enthusiastic residents who had just discovered a useful idea.

"Not before tomorrow," Nera said after checking the work queue.

Ama felt the idea collapse.

"It could help later," Efua said.

She had arrived quietly and was standing at the doorway in her work clothes. Ama had not known she was coming.

"You're early."

"I asked to leave."

"For this?"

"To see what you'd been doing."

Ama showed her the drawing. She explained the route, the chair, and the counter. Efua asked questions which made it clear she was listening. None concerned Tuesday.

Hessa called facilities about the counter and booked an inspection. It did not solve the larger room question. It might make the next several meals easier while they worked on it.

From the doorway, Rose watched Ama explain the measurements to her mother. Kojo appeared beside her holding two paper door signs he had made with the borrowed cutter.

"Which one's supper?"

He held them up. One had a bowl on it. The other had a book. Both had large arrows and no words.

Merlin looked at the book sign.

"Is that for me?"

"For people looking for you."

"Thank you."

Kojo turned toward the corridor, trying each arrow against the actual direction of the rooms. Then he frowned.

"If they move, I'll have to make new ones."

"Probably," Ama said.

He looked at the signs again and found the solution himself.

"I'll make the arrows separate."

Hessa took the bowl sign and asked whether she could use it tomorrow. Kojo said yes, provided nobody permanently attached it to anything before he fixed the arrow.

Ama put the measuring tape away. In her pocket her padd held an account she had chosen not to send. On the table lay a plan other people would use. She had made both. Neither needed to stand in for the other.

"Are you coming to the meal?" Hessa asked her.

Ama looked at the narrow room, the inconvenient table, the people waiting for her answer.

"For a bit," she said. "I said I'd call a friend after."

"We'll save you a place."

"Not all evening."

Hessa nodded.

"Tell us when you go."

Ama almost said that was the point of the whole thing. She didn't. Kojo needed someone to hold a sign while he worked out how to fasten an arrow without fastening the sign, and she was the nearest person with two free hands.
