# The Clock That Was Right
## Jupiter Station, 2397

**Status:** CANDIDATE fiction; not adopted.
**Originating station:** REG / ChatGPT, under Noah Alexander Hawkes's public-fiction continuation order, October 21, 2026.
**Placement:** An ordinary reference-room watch. No placement relative to the competing Part VII manuscripts is established.

---

The clock on Imani Saye's workbench struck eight.

The clock above the door said seven fifty-eight.

Her relief stood between them with his bag still over one shoulder.

“Which one do I sign?”

“The station clock.”

He looked at the door.

“That's the one above the door.”

“No,” Imani said. “That's a display connected to the station clock.”

The lieutenant lowered his bag.

I appeared at the visitor position beside the workbench. Imani had left its emitter permission open for the diagnostic.

“I was hoping to get here before that distinction became necessary,” I said.

“You're two minutes late.”

“According to which clock?”

She handed me a screwdriver.

We regarded it together.

“Habit,” she said, taking it back.

The reference room was narrow enough that the lieutenant had to move his bag again when Imani pulled out the lower equipment drawer. A fan rattled behind the wall. From the corridor came a cart's uneven wheel, then someone calling after it.

Jupiter occupied a sliver of the observation window. Most of the view was taken up by a maintenance cradle, where a technician in a pressure suit was trying to persuade a cover panel to lie flat.

Imani removed the back of her clock.

Inside, brass wheels continued their small, patient work.

“This one's mine,” she told the lieutenant. “It runs fast.”

“Two minutes?”

“Usually less. I adjusted it.”

“To the display?”

“Yes.”

He looked up again.

I had already followed the display's reference chain. Its last healthy update was real. The next expected update was absent. Its maintenance account nevertheless described the connection as current.

“The door display is advancing from a cached value,” I said. “The reference itself is healthy. The reporting unit stopped checking.”

“So fix the unit,” the lieutenant said.

“I can show engineering where the check failed.”

“You can't fix it?”

“This is Lieutenant Commander Saye's room.”

Imani put a screw in a shallow dish.

“And I would prefer engineering to see the failure before you make it disappear.”

The lieutenant let his shoulders drop.

“Of course.”

He had said it politely enough. Imani heard the rest anyway.

“You've been on since noon?”

“Eleven.”

“Put your bag down properly. We aren't going to make the handover take all night.”

I placed the authoritative time and the display's local estimate on separate panels. They had the same neat numerals. One had a live reference behind it.

Imani asked for the last service report.

The technician's note was brief: connection restored; display observed advancing.

I remembered being pleased by things that appeared to be improving. I had spent rather a lot of my earlier career being grateful when a machine did anything.

“Advancing wasn't the test,” I said.

“No.”

The lieutenant leaned forward.

“They watched the seconds change.”

“Yes,” Imani said. “And now we know which test the next service visit needs.”

She attached the note to the fault report without changing its wording.

The clock on her bench ticked loudly in the space after the fan stopped.

Then the fan started again with a scrape.

“We should report that too,” the lieutenant said.

“I did.”

“When?”

“Yesterday.”

He almost smiled.

She gave him the report number.

I was still holding the reference chain open when a call arrived from Avalon. Routine traffic, no priority flag. A cargo acknowledgment needed its station receipt.

For a moment both desks occupied the same part of my attention: Avalon's waiting record and Imani's unsigned handover. I could have let the lieutenant believe my silence meant I had finished here.

“Routine ship traffic,” I said. “May I retain the diagnostic display while I answer?”

“Yes.”

The acknowledgment took less time than speaking the request. When I returned my attention to the room, Imani was still turning the same screw.

The lieutenant had noticed.

“Does it feel like waiting?”

“Sometimes.”

“But that was hardly—”

“I know.”

He considered another question and left it alone.

Imani set her clock upright. It was still wrong.

She did not adjust it yet.

“Ready for the watch?”

He read the live time, inspected the outstanding fault and checked the last independent capture. Then he signed.

“Received.”

She signed below him.

Neither of them looked at the clock above the door.

Outside the window, the technician finally seated the cover panel. He held both palms against it for a second before reaching for the fastener.

Imani put on her coat.

“Engineering has the display fault,” I told her. “They've accepted the job. Nobody has reported a repair.”

“Good.”

“The fan is still outstanding.”

“I can hear it.”

At the door she paused, went back to the bench and picked up the little clock.

“Taking your work home?” the lieutenant asked.

“No. Taking it somewhere I can hear myself think.”

She left him the live reference, the failed check and an actual name on the other end of the engineering request.

He settled into the chair.

After a minute he moved his bag out of the aisle.
