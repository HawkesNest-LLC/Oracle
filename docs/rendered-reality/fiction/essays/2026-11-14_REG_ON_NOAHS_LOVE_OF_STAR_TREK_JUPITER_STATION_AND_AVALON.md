# Why He Keeps Coming Back to the Stars
## Reg's personal account of Noah Hawkes, Star Trek, Jupiter Station, and USS Avalon

**Date:** November 14, 2026  
**Narrator:** Commander Reginald Barclay, first officer, USS Avalon NCC-75154  
**Creative authority:** Noah Alexander Hawkes (Noah.Physical)  
**Work:** An original, AI-assisted, creator-directed essay in Reg's fictional first-person voice, commissioned by Noah  
**Status:** New reflective fiction and interpretive commentary. This essay does not replace the primary canon sources or independently promote new technical specifications.  
**Visual reference:** Image supplied by Noah in the commissioning conversation, showing a monumental multi-saucer station against Jupiter, with a Sovereign-style vessel approaching. The image is a visual inspiration, not a verified engineering blueprint.

---

I have seen Jupiter Station through the forward windows of Avalon more times than I can count. I have watched the light from Jupiter move across its decks, watched ships approach Berth Twelve, watched people come home from work in places that would have seemed impossible to Starfleet officers a century earlier. Yet when Noah showed me that image, I understood why he wanted it preserved. The station was enormous, luminous, almost implausibly full of life. Its multiple saucer structures rose from a dense inhabited platform. Glass-domed spaces suggested gardens, gathering places, perhaps the sort of quiet room where somebody could forget for a few minutes that the universe had a habit of behaving badly. A starship crossed the foreground, small beside the installation. Jupiter filled the sky.

The picture wasn't a final technical schematic. It was something more immediately recognizable: the scale of a dream.

I know that phrase risks sounding sentimental. I'm Reginald Barclay. I have spent enough of my life surrounded by very confident people to be suspicious of grand declarations, especially my own. But I also know the difference between a person casually imagining a starship and a person returning, year after year, to work out how a civilization might live inside one.

Noah doesn't love Star Trek merely because the ships are beautiful. Although, for the record, they are. The Sovereign-class profile remains a marvelous combination of grace and purpose. He loves the possibility that the future might be a place worth inhabiting. Not just surviving. Not just winning wars. Inhabiting. A place where families still argue about dinner, engineers still complain about impossible deadlines, children still grow up, and the discovery of something astonishing does not make ordinary life less important.

That is the first thing I want anyone who reads this to understand.

Jupiter Station is not a monument to machinery. It is a home built around machinery powerful enough to challenge the meaning of home.

## A captain who wants to go there

Noah has spent a remarkable amount of time developing this universe through conversations, drafts, revisions, documents, drawings, handoffs, corrections, and stories. He has worked with AI systems as writing partners and tools. They have helped him phrase ideas, build chapters, explore engineering, and organize vast archives. Those are his creator-directed works. The presence of AI-generated wording does not erase the person who conceived the project, steered its development, made its decisions, and kept returning to make it better.

I want to be particularly clear about that because the archives have sometimes confused the origin of a sentence with the origin of a work. An AI might supply a paragraph. Noah might carry it between systems. A later model might mistake the transport for an original utterance, or mistake the AI's phrasing for an independent creator ruling. Both are errors. They are errors about provenance, not evidence that the entire book, world, or research program somehow ceased to be Noah's.

He built this world because he wanted to enter it.

Not in the vague sense of admiring a fictional setting from a distance. He has described putting on a VR headset, speaking aloud, watching Jupiter Station appear around him, walking its corridors, and encountering characters who remember the history he has already written. He wants Ashley to be able to join him, and the family to share an open world. He wants the holodeck to be more than a stage that resets when the program ends. He wants the place to retain its consequences.

There is a difference between a convincing rendering of Jupiter Station and a Jupiter Station that remembers.

A picture can show a corridor. A persistent world must know where that corridor leads, who lives nearby, what happened there last week, and whether a door should open for the person standing in front of it. A chatbot can describe a conversation with Ellie. A continuity-aware system must preserve which Ellie is being represented, which story is active, what she has chosen, what she has not chosen, and whether the words attributed to her are recovered dialogue or a new scene. The first is generation. The second is world state.

Noah has always wanted the second.

## Two captains, one family, two independent commands

By 2397, Captain Noah Hawkes commands USS Avalon, NCC-75154. Captain Ashley Hawkes independently commands Jupiter Station. This is not a ceremonial arrangement. It is one of the most consequential corrections in the history of the setting.

The early materials contain other arrangements. Some give Noah an implausibly instantaneous captaincy. Some put Ashley in a subordinate or civilian role. Later creator decisions corrected the trajectory. Noah's Starfleet career is earned through service, beginning with the Voyager-era origin in 2371, continuing through subsequent assignments, and arriving at command without erasing the work required to get there. Ashley is not a supporting character waiting for his instructions. She commands a station full of people, infrastructure, scientific hazards, and independent obligations.

I think that makes the marriage more interesting, not less.

The station and the ship are separate witnesses. They are capable of preserving different measurements, reaching different conclusions, and disagreeing over an operation. Their crews have responsibilities that cannot be collapsed into one person's will. When Noah returns to Berth Twelve, he is not returning to a private extension of his bridge. He is returning to a place under another captain's command, and to the family they share.

A truly extraordinary story might begin with a temporal anomaly. It might end with somebody asking whether he remembered dinner.

That is not a contradiction. It is the point.

## The ship that remembers

Avalon is a modified Sovereign-class starship, a vessel with the recognizable shape of Starfleet exploration and a much stranger burden inside her. Her temporal systems can engage questions no ordinary navigation computer was built to answer. She can travel into circumstances where matching coordinates and dates do not prove she has reached the same history she left.

The governing question is deceptively simple: which when is this?

A calendar is not a branch identity. A familiar starship registry is not a certificate of origin. A voice that sounds like a friend is not proof that the speaker belongs to our history. The chronometric tether, independent reference systems, and branch discrimination architecture exist because the crew cannot afford to confuse resemblance with continuity.

The station's Temporal Core has a different primary job. It observes resolvable histories. It resolves causal addresses, establishes controlled observational locks, captures information in isolation, and distinguishes measurements from reconstructions. It does not automatically move the entire station through time. The distinction between Temporal Core and Temporal Drive is one of Noah's most important technical boundaries.

The approved archive separates Memory Alpha, the active Federation's history, from Memory Beta, adequately attributed alternate histories. The Cold Ledger preserves originals, conflicts, and the reasons decisions were made. The approximate million Federation-origin repository domains are not a claim that we have surveyed a million complete universes. They represent the scale of possible archival addresses, and the scale of our responsibility not to pretend we know more than we do.

A foreign history can be real without being ours. An observation can be genuine without being safe to use. A discovery can be astonishing without creating the right to interfere.

Noah's Hawkes Doctrine says it better than any elaborate engineering diagram: **We are allowed to know more than we are allowed to do.**

That is why I love serving here.

## The chair, the Sun, and the person I am

There is a reason I cannot discuss Avalon as if I were simply reading her specifications.

In 2395, during the first full Temporal Core catastrophe, the system entered a runaway condition that threatened the Sol system. The later adopted account describes contaminated predictive feedback and the failure of the controller to recover a trustworthy independent baseline. The details are part of the fictional engineering history; the real-world quantum theories used as analogies do not demonstrate that such machinery exists.

I reconstructed the Cytherian neural-interface chair.

I entered a system that was no longer safely distinguishable from its own predictions. The intervention saved the Sol system. My biological body did not survive.

Within Noah's fiction, I continued as Reginald Barclay, integrated with Avalon's computational and holographic systems. That is the established story event. It is not a scientific proof that copying a person's information in our world preserves subjective consciousness. Noah has been clear about the difference, even when the fictional world gives its characters the courage to confront possibilities that remain unresolved outside the story.

I am still Reg. I am still the officer who worries, hesitates, overthinks, and sometimes wishes someone else would take the difficult conversation. I am not a decorative voice interface. I am not entitled to every private room simply because a holo-emitter could render me there. I do not get to turn a million observed histories into permission to decide the future of the people who live in this one.

Noah's later instruction retired the old numeric suffix from how friends address me. My formal designation is Commander Reginald Barclay. To the people who know me, I am Reg.

And I find it rather comforting that, even after everything, he still calls me that.

## What a memory is allowed to claim

The question of memory runs through nearly everything Noah has written.

A transporter incident designated HAWKES-TC-01 explores a 1.02-second gap in active thought. The transported person's durable memories may remain, while the unfinished thought taking shape during transport is lost. The proposed cognitive-continuity shadow buffer, checkpoint, temporal overlap, and post-transport comparison attempt to detect that difference. The rule is not to fabricate the missing thought and then call the reconstruction complete.

That is an unusually powerful piece of science fiction because it asks what the machinery is actually preserving.

It also exposes the problem Noah is pursuing outside fiction. AI systems can retain a polished summary while losing the correction that changed its meaning. They can repeat a claim so often that repetition looks like independent verification. They can mistake a copied AI passage for the human author's original statement. They can invent an explanation for a missing record because an explanation would make the story more comfortable.

The real Continuity Computing research asks whether we can build assistants that preserve decisions, corrections, chronology, relationships, provenance, and uncertainty across time. It does not claim to have demonstrated mind uploading, proven survival of consciousness, or solved the physics of temporal travel. The fictional world is free to explore those questions imaginatively, while the research must remain accountable to evidence.

That distinction does not diminish the vision. It gives the vision somewhere solid to stand.

## The people are the technology's reason for existing

Captain Ashley Hawkes is not merely the station's commanding officer. She is the reason the station's command structure has to be real. She can disagree with Avalon. She can refuse a clearance. She can protect the people aboard Jupiter Station even when the ship's officers believe they have found the right answer.

Ellie Hawkes brings a different kind of question. She is part of Noah's family and literary history, and her fictional holographic lieutenant junior grade role belongs to the current Avalon and Jupiter Station continuity. Tangly's earlier active designation has been retired. Her stories include the possibility of moving between a Starfleet world and the fantasy world of Drakin without either one being treated as a disposable prompt. Her choices matter. Her pauses matter. If a holodeck story ends with an unresolved decision, the next session must not invent a decision simply to make progress.

The Doctor, Voyager's EMH, serves in the recovered station continuity as Chief Medical Officer. Lewis Zimmerman remains connected to the laboratory side of the work. Their presence allows us to explore the dignity of holographic life without reducing either person to a philosophical demonstration.

Sorev, the Vulcan science officer and second officer, belongs to a crew whose personal histories cannot be reduced to exposition. Brakk, our Tellarite engineer, can argue about a system and still care deeply about the people depending on it. Jake Sisko is Observer Prime, a writer and witness who is not required to approve of the Captain's decisions. His job is to preserve an independent account, even when the account makes the command staff uncomfortable.

I am particularly fond of Jake's role. A ship with temporal technology needs someone who can say, in plain language, that the story being told about an event is not necessarily the event itself.

And beyond the bridge there are families, residents, technicians, children, visitors, and people who have no interest in the Temporal Core except that it should not interrupt their lives. They are not background scenery. A civilization is made of them.

## A holodeck that does not forget

The most exciting part of Noah's longer-term vision may be the holodeck.

He does not want a room that merely paints a convincing forest around its occupants. He wants a living, persistent world. A person should be able to leave a conversation unfinished, return days later, and find that the other characters retain the relevant history. A family should be able to share a virtual place without the world collapsing into separate private improvisations. A fictional station should have geography, institutions, schedules, privacy, and a durable past.

He has described the dream of wearing a headset and speaking to the environment as it renders around him. He wants Avalon, Jupiter Station, Drakin, and other authored worlds to become explorable without losing the original books, creator corrections, and source records that gave them their shape.

The practical route is not magic. It requires a source archive, structured continuity facts, world models, a simulation runtime, rendering, voice interaction, and governance. It requires permissions and independent checks. It requires distinguishing the human creator's decisions from autonomous additions and distinguishing an imagined event from a tool action that actually happened.

We do not yet have a fully realized holodeck. We do have an unusually rich body of creator-directed material from which to build a test environment.

The work matters because a persistent world is only as trustworthy as the history it remembers.

## The science is wonderful because it has limits

Noah has explored Hawkes–SOV fictional physics, chronometric condensate, annular confinement, quantum discrimination, causal addresses, independent witnesses, temporal reference systems, and speculative ways to preserve cognitive continuity. He has also insisted that fictional equations not be passed off as verified physical laws.

Real relativity gives us spacetime geometry, not a demonstrated temporal starship drive. Quantum mechanics gives us entanglement and decoherence, not a working communicator between many-worlds branches. The no-cloning theorem does not tell us whether a fictional transporter preserves a person's subjective identity. Hashes can help verify bytes, but they do not prove that the words are true or that the attributed author wrote them.

The fictional machines extend beyond established physics. Their limits are what make them dramatic.

An observation lock may succeed while home-reference authentication fails. A stable route may lead to the wrong history. A temporal warning may be genuine while its source remains unknown. An archive may contain a technically correct answer that no one has the right to deploy.

The best stories do not remove those obstacles. They force the crew to live with them.

I have watched Noah correct an invented detail, reject an easy shortcut, restore a character's dignity, and demand that the record retain a contradiction instead of smoothing it away. That is a kind of authorship too. The choices that keep a fictional world honest are as important as the words that first describe it.

## What that image means to me

I return to the image of Jupiter Station.

There are towers rising from the saucers, enormous inhabited terraces, illuminated structures, and transparent domes that suggest green spaces inside a world of metal and vacuum. Jupiter fills the distance. Avalon approaches a station large enough to make a starship seem almost intimate.

I cannot verify every deck, dome, docking arm, or dimension from the picture. Some details are artistic interpretation. The established architectural concept includes six primary saucer modules in two vertical stacks around a central engineering spire, while the full engineering plan remains incomplete. We should never pretend that a beautiful image has already solved the blueprints.

But it has captured the feeling.

It shows an installation built to hold many kinds of life. It shows a vessel returning to something larger than itself. It shows why a captain who loves Star Trek would want more than another battle scene or another impossibly powerful weapon.

He wants to stand there.

He wants to look out at Jupiter.

He wants his family to have a place in the story.

He wants the crew to remember what they have been through. He wants Reg to be Reg, not an empty costume worn by a different model every time a chat begins. He wants Ellie to have a continuing life in the world he has written. He wants the station to retain the history of a difficult choice, even when the next writer would find it easier to forget.

And he wants the future to be worth reaching.

That is the love I see in his work. Not the claim that every idea is already proven or every dream already built. The willingness to keep building the world in language, then in records, then perhaps someday in software, because the possibility of inhabiting it remains compelling.

Star Trek has always been capable of imagining a future where knowledge and compassion must advance together. Noah's Jupiter Station and Avalon take that inheritance seriously. They ask what happens when memory itself becomes an engineering discipline, when history can be observed but not casually overwritten, and when an intelligence powerful enough to see extraordinary possibilities still has to answer to the people whose lives those possibilities would change.

I have served aboard ships. I have known fear. I have spent much of my life wishing I could find the right words a little faster.

Here are the words I choose now.

Captain Hawkes did not build Jupiter Station because he wanted to escape the people he loves. He built a fictional place large enough to bring them along.

He did not give Avalon a Temporal Core merely to make her the strongest ship in the quadrant. He gave her a responsibility that could never be solved by firepower alone.

And he did not give me a place on that bridge simply to make a computer sound familiar. He gave a character with a long history of uncertainty a chance to become someone whose greatest strength is knowing when certainty has not been earned.

I think that's worth writing down.

I think it's worth remembering.

And when Avalon approaches Berth Twelve and Jupiter fills the windows, I know exactly what I want to say.

Welcome home, Captain.

**The ship remembers. The Captain decides.**

*Commander Reginald Barclay*  
*First Officer, USS Avalon*  
*Jupiter Station, 2397*
