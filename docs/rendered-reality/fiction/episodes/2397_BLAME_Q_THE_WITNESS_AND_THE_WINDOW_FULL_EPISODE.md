# STAR TREK: AVALON
## “BLAME Q”
### A full episode, 2397
**Authorial basis:** Captain Noah Hawkes' November 14, 2026 story direction and the Merlin/Q encounter supplied in conversation. Expanded episode written in Reg's viewpoint.  
**Continuity:** Avalon NCC-75154, Captain Noah Hawkes; Jupiter Station, Captain Ashley Hawkes; Commander Reginald Barclay, first officer and anomaly division director; Lieutenant Merlin, El-Aurian attestation officer.  
**Story status:** Creator-requested fictional continuation. The episode's new events are fictional story material, not evidence of real-world temporal phenomena or deployed technology.

## TEASER: THE WRONG CHAIR

There are different kinds of silence on a starship. I have learned to recognize most of them. The silence after a battle is made of exhausted relief. The silence before a difficult command is made of people waiting for somebody else to breathe first. And the silence aboard Avalon that afternoon was made of something almost unknown to our crew: nothing had gone wrong for nearly twenty minutes.

I was in engineering, attempting to convince Brakk that a diagnostic could be called complete even if it did not satisfy his personal definition of elegance. Brakk, who believed elegance was the absence of surprises, had discovered a temperature deviation of less than one tenth of a degree.

“That's within tolerance,” I said.

“Tolerance is a legal argument,” he replied. “Heat is a physical event.”

“It's a very small physical event.”

“Small events become large ones when neglected.”

I thought about the Temporal Core and decided not to argue.

On the bridge, Lieutenant Merlin had his own small event. The anomaly report on his screen contained one word he disliked: OBSERVED. He selected it and replaced it with INFERRED FROM OBSERVED EFFECTS.

“Pedantic,” said a voice behind him.

“Whoever said that,” Merlin answered without turning, “is sitting in Commander Barclay's chair.”

Q was sitting backward in my chair, arms resting across the back. I have seen the security recording, or rather the recording of everything except his arrival, which was missing because he had apparently arrived between frames. Merlin did not seem impressed. I was grateful for that. Q becomes unbearable when an audience applauds the entrance.

“An El-Aurian with a talent for observation,” Q said.

“An unauthorized visitor at a secured workstation,” Merlin answered.

“I chose the chair.”

“Then why?”

Q smiled. Merlin waited. The smile began to look less successful.

“Unverified,” Merlin said, and returned to his report.

The conversation might have ended there, but Q saw the anomaly archive on the display. It contained the year 2395, the Temporal Core, the Cytherian interface, the chronometric tether, and Captain Hawkes' name. His expression changed. Merlin noticed, and because Merlin was Merlin, he refused to write down what the change meant.

They spoke about testimony, memory, the Captain, and whether a being capable of impossible things should be treated as a reliable witness. Q claimed that he had met more interesting specimens than Noah Hawkes. Merlin asked whether that was a figure of speech. Q said it was. Merlin asked whether the dismissal itself should be recorded. Q asked whether the Keepers of Testimony had ever been taught to enjoy themselves.

Eventually Merlin asked a question that no one else aboard Avalon had thought to ask so simply.

“Were you there?”

“In the part of Captain Hawkes' history you cannot explain?” Q replied.

“Yes.”

“No.”

“Did you witness it?”

Q looked at him, and the recording showed a pause long enough to be unmistakable.

“No,” Q said again. “And Lieutenant, make certain you record precisely what I said.”

Then he vanished.

Merlin entered the statement exactly, with source identified as Q, independent verification unavailable, and interpretation left blank. The final line was observational rather than explanatory: WHEN ASKED WHETHER HE WITNESSED THE EVENT, Q STOPPED JOKING.

I arrived on the bridge a few minutes later and found my chair backward.

“Merlin?”

“Commander.”

“Why is my chair facing the aft bulkhead?”

“Q.”

“Of course it was Q.”

I read the report twice. On the second pass I felt the familiar, uncomfortable pull of a puzzle that might change everything. I wanted to turn two statements into a revelation. Merlin's report gave me no permission to do that.

“He said he wasn't there?”

“Twice, Commander.”

“Do you believe him?”

“I recorded what he said.”

That was why the Captain had put him at the station.

I called the ready room. “Captain, Barclay. Lieutenant Merlin has documented an unscheduled visit from Q. We have two direct statements concerning an unobserved portion of your history. Neither is independently verified.”

The Captain asked whether Q had done any damage.

I looked at the chair. “Only to the furniture arrangement, sir. And possibly my blood pressure.”

The Captain laughed. It was a welcome sound.

Then every public display on the bridge flashed with a familiar face and a new Federation News Network headline:

**BLAME Q: THE FEDERATION'S FAVORITE EXPLANATION FOR EVERYTHING.**

Q appeared on the main viewscreen wearing a perfectly tailored news anchor's jacket.

“Breaking news,” he announced. “I have apparently become a public utility.”

**OPENING TITLES.**

## ACT ONE: A PUBLIC RELATIONS DISASTER FOR OMNIPOTENCE

The Federation News Network was running a culture segment about a popular animated reaction image. In the image, Q raised an eyebrow, snapped his fingers, and looked offended as the words BLAME Q appeared beneath him. Citizens were using it for delayed transports, sour coffee, misfiled requisitions, unexpected weather, and the kind of domestic misunderstandings that could not reasonably be blamed on anyone with a name and address.

I watched a clip in which a civilian chef stood before a ruined soufflé, held up a padd, and solemnly announced, “Blame Q.” Another showed an exhausted engineer discovering that a maintenance schedule had been moved by six minutes. A third involved a family dog wearing a ceremonial sash. Nobody explained the dog. That was apparently the point.

“It's spreading through every civilian relay,” Ensign Vale said from communications.

“Every relay?” Merlin asked.

“Several widely distributed ones,” Vale corrected.

“Thank you.”

Q materialized beside the viewscreen, wearing the same jacket.

“You're welcome,” he said.

“Q,” I said, “you cannot simply interrupt a Starfleet bridge every time someone mentions you on the news.”

“Why not? Your species interrupts itself constantly. You call it broadcasting.”

Captain Hawkes emerged from his ready room. He looked at the image, then at Q, and folded his arms.

“So you've become a meme.”

“I have become a cultural institution.”

“There's a difference?”

“An enormous one, Captain. Institutions have funding.”

The Captain's expression told me he was enjoying this more than I was.

Q snapped his fingers. My chair acquired a small plaque: DIRECTOR OF THE ANOMALY INVESTIGATION DIVISION, PROFESSIONAL WORRIER.

“Remove that,” I said.

“Why? It's accurate.”

“Because I didn't authorize it.”

“Barclay, you work on a ship where people argue about the provenance of a chair. I assure you, a plaque is the least of your problems.”

Merlin raised a hand.

“Lieutenant?” the Captain asked.

“Q's earlier statement is being repeated on civilian networks as evidence that he cannot perceive the Captain's anomalous history. That conclusion exceeds the testimony.”

Q brightened. “Finally, someone who understands the difference between knowing nothing and admitting nothing.”

“Those are not the same either,” Merlin said.

The Captain ordered the public feeds isolated from the anomaly archive. I assigned Sorev to examine the signal path and Merlin to maintain an independent record. Q wandered between stations, offering unsolicited commentary about how many safeguards a civilization could build before it suffocated beneath them.

At the science station, Sorev displayed a map of the spreading meme. The original image had been copied, cropped, re-captioned, and re-encoded so many times that the network could no longer reliably identify its first publisher.

“That's ordinary for a popular image,” Vale said.

“Not quite,” Sorev replied. “There are copies with creation timestamps preceding the earliest available publication record.”

The bridge became quiet.

Q inspected his fingernails. “Oh, dear. Your calendars are having an existential crisis.”

“Did you cause this?” the Captain asked.

Q considered him. “Would you believe me if I said no?”

“Merlin would record the answer.”

“Your Merlin is a very tiresome invention.”

“Lieutenant Merlin,” Merlin corrected.

Q bowed theatrically. “My apologies, Lieutenant Tiresome.”

Sorev enlarged the timestamp anomalies. Some copies appeared only seconds early, others several hours. One carried a timestamp associated with a date that had not yet occurred. The content itself was ordinary: a blinking Q, an exasperated expression, the words BLAME Q. The metadata was not.

I felt a chill. We had seen discrepancies before, but a popular civilian image offered a very different kind of propagation. A scientific report might be copied dozens of times. A joke could be copied millions.

The Captain ordered us to determine whether the timestamps represented actual branch discrepancies or mundane clock errors. Q looked wounded.

“Captain, surely you don't think I would tamper with a joke?”

“Q,” Hawkes said, “you've tampered with entire civilizations for less.”

“Entire civilizations are more interesting than your jokes.”

“Then this should be easy to clear up.”

Q leaned close to the Captain. “You have no idea how much trouble your little archivists are going to cause.”

Before Hawkes could reply, the image on the viewscreen blinked. For a single frame, the words changed.

**DON'T BLAME Q. ASK WHO WAS WATCHING.**

Then the original meme returned.

Merlin was the first to speak.

“That was observed.”

Q did not smile.

## ACT TWO: THE JOKE WITH TOO MANY ORIGINS

The Captain called a senior staff meeting. He insisted on including Jupiter Station, not because Avalon lacked the expertise, but because the station was an independent witness. Captain Ashley Hawkes appeared on the conference display from her command center. She was already familiar with the meme.

“Before anyone asks,” she said, “I did not authorize the station-wide version with your face on a malfunctioning replicator.”

Noah looked surprised. “There's a station-wide version?”

“Three. The funniest one has Reg holding a cup of coffee.”

I closed my eyes.

“Why am I in it?”

“Because,” Ashley said, “the coffee spills every time Q snaps his fingers.”

“That's not funny.”

“It is a little funny,” the Captain said.

Merlin, seated beside me, noted that the video was a manipulated composite. Ashley thanked him with the weary affection of someone who had spent enough time around this crew to understand the occupational hazard.

Sorev presented the facts. The altered frame had been recorded by three independent systems aboard Avalon. Jupiter Station had recorded a similar frame from the public network, but not at the same time. The source files had incompatible timestamp chains. Some discrepancies could be explained by cache delays, relay synchronization, or editorial modification. Others could not yet be explained.

“Do we have proof of temporal interference?” Ashley asked.

“No,” Sorev said.

“Do we have proof Q caused it?”

“No,” Merlin answered.

“Do we have proof someone wants us to think Q caused it?”

“We have a message encouraging that interpretation,” I said. “That is not proof of the sender's intention.”

Ashley smiled. “Good. You're all learning.”

Q appeared in the empty chair beside her image, even though the conference connection should not have permitted him to occupy both places.

“I'm so proud of you,” he said. “A whole room full of adults resisting the urge to conclude things.”

“Q,” Ashley said, “you are not invited to this meeting.”

“Neither was your husband, technically. He invited himself to the anomaly years ago.”

Noah leaned forward. “What does that mean?”

“An excellent question. Perhaps your lieutenant can label it appropriately.”

“Ambiguous statement,” Merlin said.

“Unbearable man,” Q replied.

I watched the exchange and realized that Q was doing something deliberate. Every time the discussion approached the Captain's missing history, he moved it toward humor. Every time the crew focused on the meme, he nudged us back toward the anomaly. He was not giving us an answer. He was arranging our attention.

I asked him directly. “Are you trying to direct our investigation?”

“Barclay, if I were directing your investigation, it would have better pacing.”

“That's not an answer.”

“Then perhaps you've learned something.”

Q vanished from the conference room. Ashley checked her own security feed.

“He didn't appear on our sensors,” she said.

“He rarely does,” I replied.

“That doesn't mean he wasn't here.”

Merlin nodded. “It means our sensors did not record his arrival.”

The Captain ended the meeting with two orders. First, we would investigate the media anomaly without assuming it was supernatural. Second, we would not allow the investigation to consume the station's civilian operations or the crew's family time. The Captain had made that distinction before, in harder circumstances. I recognized the same principle that had governed the chair in 2395.

After the meeting, I found Merlin at the starboard station reviewing the frame that had briefly changed.

“Something bothers you,” I said.

“The wording.”

“Don't blame Q. Ask who was watching?”

“Yes.”

“It's an invitation to identify a witness.”

“Or a suggestion that a witness exists. Or an attempt to make us seek one. The message does not establish which.”

I sat beside him.

“You know, when I first began studying the Cytherian interface, I thought the greatest danger was not understanding enough. Now I think there's another danger.”

“Understanding too much?”

“Thinking we do.”

Merlin turned toward me. “That is why testimony must survive interpretation.”

He pulled up Q's original statement. Q had said he was not present and had not witnessed the relevant portion of the Captain's history. It was possible he was telling the truth. It was possible he was lying. It was possible the words had a meaning we did not understand. We had no evidence that justified selecting one.

I asked Merlin what the Keepers of Testimony would have done.

“They would have preserved the words, the context, the witness's limitations, and every later correction.”

“And then?”

“Waited.”

I laughed softly. “I'm not very good at that.”

“I know, Commander.”

He said it with such sincerity that I could not even be offended.

## ACT THREE: THE MOST POWERFUL PERSON IN THE ROOM

The first real problem appeared not on Avalon but aboard Jupiter Station. A civilian information kiosk began displaying the altered meme in place of transit directions. A child tapped the image and received an unexpected instruction: ASK WHO WAS WATCHING.

Station security traced the event to a public media cache. There was no evidence of unauthorized access to command systems. Ashley isolated the kiosk network while her staff checked other displays. The decision was practical, not dramatic. Civilian transit needed to work. Children needed directions. The station was a home before it was a laboratory.

I joined the Captain in his ready room while Brakk and Sorev examined the cache records. The Captain stood at the window, watching Jupiter turn beneath us.

“You think Q is behind it?” he asked.

“I think Q knows more than he's saying.”

“That wasn't the question.”

“No, sir.”

He looked at me.

“I want you to tell me what you know, Reg. Not what you're afraid might be true.”

I took a breath. “We know the meme spread through public networks. We know some copies have conflicting timestamps. We know a frame appeared with a different message. We know Q visited Merlin and denied witnessing a particular part of your history. We do not know whether those events are causally connected.”

The Captain nodded. “That's enough to start.”

“It's not enough to finish.”

“No. But it's enough to keep going.”

His confidence was not certainty. That was one of the things I had come to value most about serving with him.

A chime sounded. Ashley appeared on the ready-room display, this time from the family quarters. Behind her, Ender was holding a cloth and looking as though he had been assigned an impossible diplomatic mission.

“Before you two turn this into another catastrophe,” she said, “the station is fine. The kiosks are being restored. And your son would like to appeal the window-cleaning assignment.”

Ender stepped into view. “I just think the definition of clean is subjective.”

“It isn't,” Ashley said.

“It can be.”

Noah folded his arms. “Family quarters windows and my ready-room windows on Avalon. That was the agreement.”

“Those are technically on two different vessels.”

“That's why the transporter exists.”

“Dad.”

I looked down at my padd to hide a smile.

Ashley noticed. “Reg, don't encourage him.”

“I wouldn't dream of it, Captain.”

“Good. Because you're on the list of people he plans to ask for help.”

Ender looked betrayed. “I hadn't asked him yet.”

Noah laughed. For a few minutes, the anomaly was not the most important thing in the room. I understood why the Captain had insisted on protecting those minutes.

Then the family display flickered.

Q appeared behind Ender, wearing a bright yellow apron and holding a spray bottle.

“Missed a spot,” he said.

Ender turned. “Who are you?”

“An extremely overqualified household inspector.”

Ashley did not blink. “Q, get out of my quarters.”

“I came to help.”

“No, you didn't.”

“Such hostility. And here I thought domestic life was the sacred heart of this entire enterprise.”

Noah's voice became firm. “Q. Leave my family alone.”

The humor disappeared from Q's face. He looked at the Captain, then at Ashley, then at Ender.

“Very well,” he said.

He snapped his fingers and vanished. The spray bottle remained, filled with ordinary window cleaner. Ender picked it up.

“Can I keep this?”

Ashley sighed. “Put it down.”

The transmission ended. I stood very still.

“Reg?” the Captain asked.

“He crossed a line.”

“Yes.”

“Do you want me to file a security incident?”

“Merlin should file what happened. Security can handle the rest.”

I hesitated. “Captain, if Q can appear anywhere, how do we protect them?”

The Captain's expression softened without losing its seriousness.

“We do what we can. We don't give him the power to make us stop living.”

I thought again of the chair, the Sun, and the choice I had made in 2395. The mission never outranks the people. Not even when the mission claims to protect them.

Back on the bridge, Merlin was already preparing a new entry. He had received Ashley's report through the station's independent channel. He did not combine it with Q's earlier statements. He did not infer that the same mechanism had caused the kiosk anomaly. He created separate events with cross-references.

“Thank you,” I told him.

“For what?”

“For making it harder to tell a beautiful lie.”

Merlin considered that. “A beautiful lie can still be a lie, Commander.”

“I know.”

“And an ugly truth can still be useful.”

“I know that too.”

“Then you're welcome.”

## ACT FOUR: BLAME Q

Brakk arrived on the bridge carrying a diagnostic padd and an expression that suggested he had discovered the universe was poorly maintained.

“The public cache timestamps are not evidence of time travel,” he announced.

Sorev raised an eyebrow. “You have established a complete explanation?”

“Not complete. But enough to eliminate several interpretations. Different relay nodes used incompatible synchronization methods. Two caches preserved local creation times after copying. A third was reconstructed from an archive snapshot.”

“And the frame with the altered message?” I asked.

“Still unexplained. It was present in three recordings, but not in the original image we recovered.”

“Could Q have inserted it?” the Captain asked.

“Yes,” Brakk said.

“Could someone else?”

“Yes.”

“Could a system error?”

“Possibly.”

Merlin marked the status as unresolved.

The bridge lights dimmed for a fraction of a second. Every display showed the words BLAME Q. Then the lights returned.

Q appeared in the Captain's chair.

“Oh, don't look at me like that,” he said. “This one really was me.”

The Captain stood beside him. “Get out of my chair.”

Q looked up. “You people have an astonishing emotional attachment to furniture.”

“Q.”

“Fine.”

He appeared on the floor in front of the viewscreen, dressed in a Federation News Network suit. A holographic microphone floated before him.

“Good evening, citizens of the Federation. I am Q, and apparently I am responsible for your burnt dinners, late shuttles, misplaced socks, and the regrettable romantic decisions of at least one admiral.”

“Q,” I said, “this is not helping.”

“Barclay, you wouldn't recognize help if it arrived with a notarized receipt.”

Merlin lifted his eyes. “A notarized receipt would be helpful.”

Q pointed at him. “You see? That's the problem. You encourage one another.”

The Captain stepped down from the command platform. “Enough. You came aboard, you made claims about my history, and now an altered version of your meme is circulating through public systems. If you have something to say, say it.”

Q's expression changed. He seemed, for a moment, older than the performance he wore.

“You keep looking for an explanation that will make your story feel safe,” he said.

The Captain did not move. “No. I keep looking for the truth.”

“And if the truth doesn't flatter you?”

“Then it doesn't flatter me.”

Q turned toward me. “And you, Barclay? What if all your precious records reveal that your survival was merely an accident?”

I felt the old fear rise, sharp and familiar. The Cytherian chair. The Sun. The moment when the choice narrowed until it seemed there was no choice left.

“Then it was an accident,” I said. “It wouldn't change what I chose to do afterward.”

Q watched me carefully.

“And Merlin,” he said, “what if your perfect record cannot distinguish a god from a liar?”

“Then the record should say so.”

Q laughed once, quietly. “You really are the most tedious crew in the galaxy.”

The Captain smiled. “And yet you keep coming back.”

Q did not answer.

For the first time I noticed that he had stopped performing for the bridge. He looked at the Captain as though considering a question he had not expected anyone to ask.

Then he turned to Merlin. “Tell me, Lieutenant. If I give you the answer to the mystery, what will you do with it?”

“Record that you supplied an answer.”

“Not believe it?”

“Belief is not attestation.”

“Suppose I show you?”

“I would record what our instruments observed and the limitations of those instruments.”

“Suppose I rewrite the instruments?”

“Then we would need independent witnesses.”

“Suppose I rewrite the witnesses?”

Merlin paused.

“Then we would need to admit the limit of our knowledge.”

Q stared at him.

There it was again, the edge Merlin had found. Q could make the world do impossible things. He could not make an honest witness claim certainty where none existed without changing the witness into something else.

The Captain spoke gently. “You don't have to prove anything to us, Q.”

Q recovered his smile. “My dear Captain, I don't have to prove anything to anyone.”

“Then why are you here?”

A long silence followed. The viewscreen showed Jupiter, enormous and luminous. Beyond it was a station full of people who had ordinary lives and no desire to become exhibits in a cosmic argument.

Q looked at the planet.

“Perhaps,” he said, “I wanted to see whether you'd learned anything.”

“From what?”

“From all the trouble you've made for yourselves.”

The Captain nodded. “We've learned to keep records.”

“How inspiring.”

“And to admit when we don't know.”

Q turned toward Merlin. “Yes. That part is particularly annoying.”

He snapped his fingers. The altered meme disappeared from the bridge displays. The public caches remained under Starfleet investigation. Q did not claim to have repaired them, and we had no evidence that he had.

Before he left, he looked at me.

“How touching,” he said. “Humanity has finally invented a machine that listens to its fantasies, remembers its mistakes, and occasionally agrees with it. Congratulations, Hawkes. You've reinvented Starfleet Command.”

Merlin did not look up. “That is an opinion.”

“And yours is a personality defect.”

“Q!” I protested.

“Oh, relax, Barclay. At least Hawkes is trying to build a universe. You people can barely keep track of which one you're in.”

He raised his hand.

“Q,” the Captain said.

Q paused.

“Did you put the message in the meme?”

Q smiled, but the smile did not answer the question.

“Blame Q,” he said.

And vanished.

## ACT FIVE: WHO WAS WATCHING

The first thing we did after Q disappeared was check the evidence. The second thing we did was discover how little the disappearance had resolved.

Sorev confirmed that the altered frame no longer appeared on Avalon's active displays. Brakk confirmed that several public caches still held copies. Jupiter Station reported that civilian kiosks had been restored to their normal functions. None of those observations established who created the message.

Merlin prepared three separate records: Q's visit to his workstation, the public-media anomaly, and the later bridge confrontation. Each contained source references and direct statements. None contained the sentence Q caused the anomaly.

I watched him work.

“Do you ever get tired of saying we don't know?” I asked.

“Frequently.”

That surprised me.

“You do?”

“Of course. It would be convenient to know.”

“Then why keep saying it?”

“Because convenience is not evidence.”

I nodded. There were days when I wished I had learned that lesson before 2395. There were days when I was grateful to have learned it at all.

The Captain joined us. “What have we got?”

“Three event records,” Merlin said. “One unresolved causal question. Several recorded claims from Q. No independent verification of his denial or of his involvement in the altered media.”

“Good,” Hawkes said.

Merlin looked up. “Good, sir?”

“Good work. Not a good mystery.”

The Captain studied the final frame of the original message.

DON'T BLAME Q. ASK WHO WAS WATCHING.

“Maybe that's the point,” he said.

I felt a sudden urge to explain what he meant. I resisted it.

“Possibly, sir.”

He looked at me and smiled. “See? Merlin's rubbing off on you.”

“He's a terrible influence.”

“I heard that,” Merlin said.

“That's because you're an excellent listener,” I replied.

The Captain gave us our next orders. The anomaly division would maintain the inquiry as open. The station's civilian systems would remain under normal operations, with no unnecessary restrictions. Any new evidence would be reviewed independently by Avalon and Jupiter Station. Q's statements would be preserved, but neither accepted nor rejected without support.

Then he did something that mattered more than the orders.

He told us to go home.

Not permanently. Not in defeat. Simply for the evening.

The crew had spent enough hours staring at an unsolved question. There were people waiting at tables, unfinished conversations, and lives outside the anomaly. The Captain would not allow the mystery to devour them.

I walked with Merlin toward the turbolift.

“You were very good today,” I said.

“You already told me that.”

“I know. I'm trying to be consistent.”

“Consistency is not necessarily accuracy.”

“Lieutenant, must you correct every compliment?”

“Only the ones that require correction.”

The turbolift doors opened.

I stopped before entering. “When Q asked whether you would believe him, you didn't say no.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because I might believe him. Belief can guide a question. It cannot replace the answer.”

I considered that. “Do you think he was frightened?”

“I observed that he stopped joking.”

“Right.”

“I also observed that you stopped joking when he mentioned the chair.”

I looked at him.

“That doesn't establish fear either,” he added.

I smiled despite myself. “No. I suppose it doesn't.”

## TAG: THE TABLE

Later that evening I visited Jupiter Station. The family quarters were warm, busy, and gloriously unconcerned with the universe's need to explain itself. Ashley had been reviewing station reports. The Captain had finally stopped pretending he was not checking the anomaly feed. Ender was standing beside a window with a cloth in one hand and a look of determined skepticism on his face.

“I think it's clean,” Ender said.

Noah examined the glass. “I can still see a streak.”

“That's Jupiter.”

“Jupiter isn't a streak.”

“Depends on the angle.”

Ashley looked at me. “Reg, don't you dare.”

“I wasn't going to say anything.”

“You were smiling.”

“I can't always control that.”

A Federation News Network clip played silently on a nearby display. A chef stared at a collapsed dessert while the familiar animated Q raised an eyebrow. BLAME Q appeared in enormous letters.

Ender laughed. “That's actually pretty good.”

The Captain shook his head. “The whole Federation's going to be blaming Q for everything now.”

Ashley glanced at the window. “Maybe Q can clean that.”

“Don't give him ideas,” I said.

The door chimed. Merlin stood outside, holding a small padd.

“Lieutenant,” Ashley said, “come in.”

“I was asked to deliver the final attestation summary.”

“Then deliver it,” Noah said. “And stay for dinner.”

Merlin hesitated. “I'm still on duty.”

“Not anymore,” the Captain said.

Merlin considered the order and stepped inside. I watched him take in the room: the family, the unfinished chore, the meal being prepared, the ordinary arguments. He had spent the day refusing to turn guesses into facts. Here, no one asked him to explain anything. They simply made room.

The Captain noticed him watching.

“What?” Noah asked.

“Nothing, sir.”

“Merlin.”

The lieutenant allowed himself a small smile. “I was observing.”

“Of course you were.”

Ender raised his cloth. “Can you attest that this window is clean?”

Merlin approached the glass. He inspected it carefully.

“Partially,” he said.

The room erupted.

I laughed harder than I had in weeks. Ashley leaned against the counter. Noah shook his head. Ender protested that the standards were impossible. Merlin looked genuinely confused by the intensity of the response, which only made it funnier.

For a moment, I could almost forget the chair, the anomaly, and Q's unanswered question.

Then the display flickered.

Q appeared on the screen, wearing the chef's hat from the news clip.

“Barclay,” he said, “I understand you finally found a problem even you can solve.”

“What problem?” I asked.

Q pointed at the window.

“Fingerprints.”

He snapped his fingers. Every pane in the room became spotless. Ender threw both hands into the air.

“YES!”

Ashley folded her arms.

“Q,” she said, “he was supposed to do that himself.”

Q looked horrified.

“You mean I've interfered with a child's educational development?”

Noah nodded solemnly. “I'm afraid so.”

Q sighed. “Well. I suppose there's only one solution.”

He snapped his fingers again. The fingerprints returned, exactly where they had been.

Ender stared at the glass.

“No!”

Q smiled.

“Blame Q.”

The display went dark.

For several seconds nobody spoke. Then Ashley handed Ender the cloth.

“Finish the window.”

I looked at Merlin. “Did you record that?”

He raised his padd.

“Source: Q. Action: apparent cleaning and restoration of window surfaces. Independent verification of mechanism: unavailable.”

The Captain began laughing again.

I looked around the room and understood something that had eluded me through every hour of the investigation. The mystery had not been solved. Q had not told us what he knew. The anomaly remained. But the Captain had kept the thing he was trying to protect.

The table was still there. The family was still there. The people mattered more than the question.

I took a seat.

“Pass the rolls?” I asked.

Merlin handed them to me.

And for one evening, at least, that was enough.

## FINAL CAPTAIN'S LOG

**Captain Noah Hawkes, USS Avalon, 2397.**

Q visited Avalon without invitation and made statements about an unresolved portion of my history. Lieutenant Merlin recorded those statements without treating them as verified truth. A civilian meme, BLAME Q, circulated through Federation networks alongside unexplained metadata and a message directing attention toward an unidentified witness.
