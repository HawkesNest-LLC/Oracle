# STAR TREK: AVALON
## ONE UNACCOUNTED SECOND
### The First Temporal Refit | 2395

**By Noah Hawkes**

*Developed with AI drafting assistance under the author's creative direction.*

*The ship had brought them home from disasters. Now Starfleet wanted to teach her how to measure the spaces between moments.*

### PROLOGUE: THE PEOPLE WHO KEEP THE LIGHTS ON

At 0314 shipboard time, the USS *Avalon* smelled faintly of hot insulation, fresh coffee, and the cleaning compound Engineering used on an access junction beneath Deck Nine.

Crewman Yara Pell had been working there for fifty-three minutes.

The deck panel refused to sit flush.

She tried once more, heard its fastening catch, and decided she would never tell another person she had considered a transfer to the prestigious *Enterprise*. On the *Enterprise*, she imagined, some junior crewman might receive praise for repairing an experimental warp interface. Here she had spent nearly an hour persuading an access panel to lie flat.

"Bridge to Engineering. Please confirm auxiliary environmental grid."

Pell tapped her communicator. The little chirp echoed down the maintenance trunk. "Grid confirmed, sir. And if it loses pressure again, it's because somebody insists on routing the new experimental conduits through an access hatch designed by a sadist."

"Your report will be noted," came the reply.

"Please note the sadist."

Outside the hull, dozens of tugs moved around the ship. The *Avalon* had entered orbital drydock above Earth two days earlier. At the far end of the facility, technicians were installing a chronometric-isolation assembly designed to keep the vessel's clocks, sensors, and navigation references from disagreeing under extreme temporal interference.

The ship's captain was supposed to be asleep.

He wasn't.

### I. THE CAPTAIN'S GLASS

Captain Noah Hawkes stood in the observation lounge with a replicated Coke Zero with vanilla and looked down the length of his ship.

Eight years.

That was the working figure in Starfleet's service record: eight years since he had taken Avalon's center seat. It did not include the years he had served aboard her before that, or his three-year tour on the Nova-class *Bozeman-A*, or the time he had spent in Admiral Owen Paris's office reading the reports of captains he had never met.

No tally captured what it meant to see a crewman's uniform sleeve caught under a repair clamp and know whose sleeve it was before he turned around.

The *Avalon* was not one of those ships that existed only as a silhouette in a Starfleet technical database. People had argued on her decks, married while on leave, learned how to mourn, made career-ending mistakes, and been forgiven for lesser ones. Her chief engineer knew that a particular conduit sang whenever the impulse reactors passed through a specific load. Her science officer was trying to teach her daughter to play an ancient stringed instrument over subspace. The night-shift security officer always took his breakfast five minutes after relief, even when nobody was there to watch.

All of them were aboard a ship being opened to experimental equipment.

The doors whispered apart.

"You're late," said Chief Engineer Maren Voss.

"For what?"

"The six-hour argument we've been having."

Hawkes raised his glass. "Then I owe you six hours."

"Not yet. I have a list."

She held out a PADD. The modifications would have been impressive even to someone who didn't understand them. Twelve independent synchronization pathways. Shielded chronometric sensors. An experimental field regulator. A recorder designed to remain outside the temporal-control software's authority.

"You're worried," Hawkes said.

"I'm furious that the people who built this assume they can predict every way it might fail."

"You're worried."

"Yes."

He handed the PADD back.

"Good. So am I."

### II. A VISITOR FROM THE DEPARTMENT

The Department of Temporal Investigations arrived at 0830, represented by an investigator named Commander Sera Kest.

She wore a regulation uniform without a single concession to the novelty of the assignment. Her hair was tied back. She carried a slim paper notebook, which immediately interested Hawkes.

"Captain," she said.

"Commander. Welcome aboard."

"I've read your submission."

"Then you know our concerns."

"I know your *stated* concerns. Those are not always the same."

Hawkes gestured toward the ship beyond the window. "My chief engineer believes the containment field is insufficiently characterized."

"Your chief engineer may be correct."

"And you?"

"I believe Starfleet has an unusually poor understanding of the relationship between experimental clocks and experimental explanations."

He grinned. "We'll get along."

"Possibly. I don't particularly enjoy getting along."

Kest explained that the new regulator did not promise time travel. Its stated purpose was narrower: to prevent the ship's clocks and critical records from being silently normalized when it encountered chronometric interference during exploration.

"That sounds like an excellent goal," Hawkes said.

"It does. Which makes it easier to stop asking what else the device might do."

She opened her notebook.

"One question before the readiness review. If your temporal recorder produces a result that contradicts the ship's computer, which one will you believe?"

"Neither automatically."

Kest looked up.

"Then you may be the first captain I've interviewed this month who understands the problem."

### III. THE BRIDGE AT REST

The bridge had been rearranged for the refit, but it still felt like home.

At the conn, Lieutenant Rian Solis was checking attitude-control thrusters against the station's docking restraints. Beside him, Operations Officer Nessa Vale was arguing with a diagnostic readout that had been convinced, for the last half hour, that Deck Four had more atmospheric pressure than Deck Three.

Hawkes crossed to the center chair.

"Good morning."

"Morning, Captain," came two voices, slightly out of sync.

"Not a good sign," he observed.

Vale looked up. "We're having synchronization problems."

"Then this is excellent timing for a temporal experiment."

A smile traveled around the bridge. It disappeared when Chief Voss opened the Engineering channel.

"Captain, the chronometric array has been powered for diagnostics. Final approval pending."

"Hold there, Chief."

Kest stood behind the command chair, watching how the bridge worked when nobody was performing for an inspector.

Hawkes tapped his communicator.

"All hands, this is the captain. In two hours we'll begin a controlled test of the new temporal-isolation equipment. This is not a time-travel experiment, and it's not an excuse for any department to improvise. If you observe something you can't explain, report what you actually observed. No guesses dressed up as readings. No corrections made simply because a result looks inconvenient. Hawkes out."

Solis turned slightly.

"Sir? Is that speech intended to reassure us?"

"Did it work?"

"Not particularly."

"Good. Stay alert."

### IV. WHAT THE ARCHIVE DOESN'T SAY

The evening before the test, Kest asked to see Hawkes's original sector reports from his Paris assignment.

"Why?" he asked.

"Because I want to know whether you've always been like this."

"Like what?"

"Annoyingly unwilling to accept a tidy conclusion."

He brought her the records.

One concerned a missing shuttle. Another, the unexplained loss of an unmanned probe. A third, the testimony of an engineer who believed a sensor had briefly reported an impossible location.

Kest read in silence.

"You recommended reopening all three."

"I recommended follow-up investigation. I didn't have the authority to reopen anything."

"Two of these turned out to be instrumentation errors."

"And one?"

"A clerical classification issue."

Hawkes leaned against the table. "Doesn't sound particularly heroic."

"No," Kest said. "But I wasn't expecting heroism. You recorded reasons you might be wrong."

He thought of Captain Ellison, his predecessor aboard Avalon, who had once told him that a crew remembers what you do, not what you intended.

"An officer who only preserves his correct predictions isn't preserving a career," Hawkes said. "He's writing an advertisement."

Kest closed the file.

"That," she said, "I intend to put in my report."

### V. THE FIRST TEST

By 1400 the next day, the *Avalon* rested inside an isolated drydock instrumentation envelope.

The regulator came online with a sound almost too low to hear.

Voss's voice arrived from Engineering.

"Primary containment stable. Independent recorder active. Navigation and warp controls isolated from the field. Shipyard observers acknowledge readiness."

Hawkes looked to Kest.

"Any objection?"

"Several. None that would prevent a carefully bounded first trial."

"Bridge to Engineering. Increase to five percent."

"Five percent."

The light on the main display thickened around a diagram of the engineering compartment.

"Chronometric differential?"

"Within modeled tolerance," Vale said.

"External reference?"

"Aligned."

"Ten percent."

A low vibration ran through the chair.

Solis looked over his shoulder. "Sir, the docking thrusters report a momentary corrective impulse. Thrusters did not fire."

"Log it."

"Fifteen percent."

The independent clock appeared on the auxiliary monitor: ordinary ticking marks on a strip of physical recording medium.

At first nothing happened.

Then the bridge's shipyard-link display went black.

Vale leaned forward. "External timing reference lost."

"Engineering?" Hawkes asked.

Voss answered immediately. "Local field nominal."

A second monitor went dark.

"Captain," Solis said, "the station reports our vessel still secured, but I no longer have an external positional checksum."

Hawkes stared at the silent display.

He had spent years imagining the future of this ship, and not one imagined future depended on the success of a single laboratory experiment.

"End the test," he ordered.

Renner, the Daystrom project scientist, broke onto the Engineering circuit.

"Captain, the internal sensors show no excursion beyond the field boundary."

"That isn't what I asked, Doctor."

"Request permission to maintain the field for diagnostic capture."

"Denied. Chief, shut it down."

"Aye, Captain."

The ring assembly went dark.

The screens returned in sequence.

Nobody applauded.

Kest stepped closer to the independent recorder monitor.

"Captain. Don't let them synchronize the clocks."

"Operations, freeze all correction processes. Engineering, preserve the raw instrument images. And transmit an unaltered copy to the investigator's external recorder."

"Aye, sir," Vale said.

Thirty seconds later she looked up again.

"Sir. Internal elapsed time, four minutes seventeen seconds."

"External digital?"

"The same."

"Independent physical recorder?"

Vale's voice changed.

"Four minutes eighteen seconds."

One second.

A discrepancy small enough to mock, and large enough to make an entire room afraid of laughing.

Renner spoke quietly. "It could be the mechanical recorder."

"Yes," Hawkes said. "It could."

The captain waited for someone to offer a second explanation.

Voss supplied one. "Or the synchronization link that shut itself down."

"Yes."

Kest said, "Or an interaction we're not yet measuring."

"Also possible."

Hawkes settled into the center chair.

"Then we have three hypotheses and one observation. We are not going to promote any of them into a conclusion."

### VI. THE REPORT THAT WOULD NOT CLOSE

The hearing began before the ship had completed its first reassembled systems check.

Renner presented the simplest explanation: a recorder whose calibration had drifted under the field's electromagnetic load. He had evidence, and Hawkes listened carefully. He also had assumptions, and Kest noted each one.

Voss presented a competing hypothesis involving a failed external data link.

An independent engineering team proposed a third: the regulator might briefly have separated the ship's internal timing frame from an unsynchronized observer, without moving any part of the hull.

No one had demonstrated that third possibility.

Hawkes said so.

Starfleet Command wanted a recommendation. The shipyard wanted its berth back. Half the scientists wanted another test, and the other half wanted the first test invalidated so nobody would have to explain it.

Hawkes wrote:

**Finding:** A one-second discrepancy occurred between independent reference systems during a bounded temporal-isolation experiment.

**Cause:** Undetermined.

**Current evidence:** Insufficient to establish temporal displacement, recorder failure, or physical time discontinuity.

**Order:** Suspend higher-intensity trials. Preserve original outputs and calibration conditions. Require independently witnessed reproduction attempts.

**Command responsibility:** Captain Noah Hawkes, USS Avalon.

Kest read the order and tapped the last line.

"You could have put this on the engineering officer."

"The engineers didn't order the experiment."

"You did."

"Yes."

She signed her concurrence.

### VII. AN OLD ADMIRAL

Admiral Owen Paris did not appear on the bridge viewscreen.

He called the captain privately, and for a few seconds looked less like an admiral than a tired father who had seen too many reports promising that the next generation of machinery would save everybody trouble.

"Noah. I hear you stopped a billion-credit project over one second."

"A one-second discrepancy, Admiral."

"That wording hasn't changed in years."

"Neither has your habit of counting the credits."

Paris almost smiled.

"I've read the investigators' recommendation. You're asking to keep this system aboard under sealed test conditions, rather than uninstall it."

"Yes. We might learn more by preserving the configuration than by dismantling it."

"Are you certain?"

"No."

That answer seemed to relieve the older man.

"Good. Then tell me who can stop you if you become certain for the wrong reason."

"Chief Voss. My first officer. The Department of Temporal Investigations. Starfleet Command. The crew, through established safety procedures."

"And if you ignore them?"

"Relieve me."

Paris nodded.

"You've grown into the chair."

Hawkes looked away for a moment.

"I had good examples."

"Some of them were very irritating."

"Yes, sir."

"Keep the recorder. No further live tests without independent approval. And Hawkes?"

"Admiral?"

"Don't fall in love with the mystery."

"I'll try not to."

"Try harder."

The connection closed.

### VIII. MAKE IT SO

At 2230, the bridge had resumed its ordinary rhythms.

Vale was off duty. Solis had left an apology on a junior officer's console after an unnecessary argument about docking limits. Chief Voss was eating something cold at her engineering station and pretending not to be hungry.

Hawkes came onto the bridge carrying his familiar drink.

"Status," he said.

The night officer stood.

"All decks report nominal. Station traffic is clear. The independent recorder has been secured under dual custody. The field array remains isolated."

"Any message from Temporal Investigations?"

"Commander Kest says she'll return tomorrow."

"She doesn't like us?"

"She said your procedures were acceptable, sir."

Hawkes considered.

"From her that's practically affection."

He settled in the center chair.

Solis had stayed late to oversee the docking systems.

"Captain, once the yard releases us, our outbound course is already filed."

"Where?"

"A small survey assignment along the corridor. Nothing particularly exciting."

Hawkes smiled. "Perfect."

The helmsman hesitated.

"Sir. I heard about the extra second."

"So did half of Starfleet by now."

"What do you think it means?"

Hawkes looked at the stars on the viewscreen.

"I don't know, Lieutenant."

Solis waited.

"That's the answer you want your captain to give you?"

"When it's true."

The stars remained still while docking control finished its checks.

"Release confirmed, Captain," Solis finally said. "Course laid in."

Hawkes raised the glass slightly.

"Engage."

The *Avalon* slipped free of the station.

For the first time in days, her engines carried her under her own power. The familiar sound rose through the deck, and Hawkes felt the unremarkable comfort of a ship doing exactly what a ship was built to do.

### EPILOGUE: 2396, THE UNDELIVERED REQUEST

Eleven months later, Commander Kest reopened the file.

The recorder had survived two independent laboratory examinations. One hypothesis had become less likely. None had been proved.

Across her desk lay a classified routing memorandum bearing the insignia of Starfleet Command and a location in the Sol system.

**JUPITER STATION: TEMPORAL EVIDENCE CUSTODY REVIEW**

Kest read the destination twice.

The memorandum did not say Avalon was being reassigned. It did not mention Captain Hawkes.

It requested something simpler and, in her experience, more dangerous.

A system that could retain contradictory records without overwriting either one.

She took out the paper notebook she had used aboard Avalon and wrote a single line.

*Ask the captain whether he still has the original second.*

Then she closed the file.
