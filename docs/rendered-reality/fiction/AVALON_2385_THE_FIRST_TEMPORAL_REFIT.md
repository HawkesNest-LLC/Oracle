# STAR TREK: AVALON
## An Unaccounted Second
### Book One, Chapter: The First Temporal Refit
**Mars Orbital Shipyards, 2385**

### I. BERTH TWELVE

Mars occupied nearly the whole observation window, a rust-red planet ringed in bright shipyard traffic. Utility craft traced deliberate paths between gantries. Welding flashes glittered across the distant hull of a half-built cruiser. Below, the terminator advanced across Valles Marineris.

Commander Noah Hawkes stood with a cold glass of replicated Coke Zero with vanilla and watched the USS *Avalon* hang motionless in Berth Twelve.

Motionless was, technically, the wrong word. The platform orbited Mars at thousands of kilometers per hour. The starship rested in an articulated cradle. Supply drones revolved around her. Nothing in the Sol system was truly standing still.

He had learned that distinction from Admiral Owen Paris.

He had learned to care about it aboard Voyager.

And three years aboard the Nova-class *Bozeman-A* had taught him that the view from a science vessel's cramped wardroom could be worth the discomfort of getting there.

Now, from the window, Avalon looked like a ship in the middle of being taken apart by people who loved her and were trying very hard not to kill her.

Half her dorsal engineering access panels had been lifted away. A ribbed containment frame embraced the engineering hull. Amber hazard buoys marked out a rectangle of space around the vessel. She was not yet the ship Hawkes hoped to command. She was a ship entrusted to the woman who presently commanded her, Captain Mara Ellison, and to a crew whose careers would be shaped by what happened in this yard.

"You're staring again," said Lieutenant Commander T'Vel.

Hawkes did not turn. "I'm overseeing."

"From an observation window."

"An excellent location to observe."

T'Vel presented a PADD. The Vulcan carried diagnostic reports the way some people carried unpleasant family news: without embellishment.

"The Daystrom team has completed the mounting of the primary temporal-field regulator. Chief Voss has refused their request to connect it to the navigation backbone."

"Voss refuses many things."

"Her refusal is usually accompanied by evidence."

"Then we'll get along. Has the captain signed off on the test conditions?"

"Captain Ellison directed that you chair the readiness review. She will attend the field activation remotely from the bridge."

He looked at Avalon. "Good."

"Admiral Paris also sent a message."

"Personal or official?"

"He marked it both."

T'Vel activated the recording.

Owen Paris appeared in miniature, his expression halfway between fatigue and amusement.

"Commander Hawkes. Your latest memorandum includes eighteen precautions for an experimental system nobody has activated yet. The department calls it exhaustive. I suspect they mean exhausting." He glanced aside at an unseen report. "The additional independent recorder is approved. Do not let the engineers sell you a perfectly elegant explanation before they've checked whether it is true. And Noah, keep me informed. Owen Paris out."

Hawkes let the silence settle.

"Does that constitute encouragement?" T'Vel asked.

"From him? Practically a standing ovation."

"You are assigning an emotional interpretation to an informational message."

"Three years on the Bozeman-A would have done you good."

"I have reviewed her records. Your assertion remains unproven."

Hawkes smiled. "Put that in the log."

"I already have."

### II. THE SHIP BEFORE THE CAPTAIN

Captain Mara Ellison met them at the transfer airlock.

She was a compact woman with silver at her temples and the patient, sharpened gaze of an officer who had spent enough years in command to distrust miracles and enough years among scientists to know she could not avoid them.

"Commander."

"Captain."

"Tell me why the temporal array needs your analog clock."

"Because our computer can only tell us what our computer believes it measured."

Ellison studied him. "The engineering division is threatening to name the clock after you."

"As long as it runs."

She gestured toward the ship. "My ship is going to come back from this refit in working order."

"That's the plan."

"It had better be more than the plan."

There was affection in the severity. Hawkes had served as her first officer since 2382, after leaving the *Bozeman-A*. She trusted him with the crew and with the ship, although she had not been shy about telling him that a flawless report was no substitute for a frightened ensign receiving an understandable order.

Ellison lowered her voice. "A ship doesn't remember what a commander intended. A crew remembers what a commander did."

Hawkes nodded.

"Your readiness review is yours. You have authority to call a stop. You do not have authority to turn this into a private crusade."

"Understood."

"Good. Now go disappoint some physicists."

### III. A SHIP WITH A PAST

The lift carried them through a quarter deck whose newly installed holographic projectors were being tested by a crewman in an engineering tunic.

A coastal sunrise shimmered through an open doorway, turning a small cabin into an improbable beach.

Hawkes paused.

"I remember when those projectors were a luxury on the Bozeman-A," he said.

T'Vel regarded the holographic surf. "A technically interesting solution to a psychological problem."

"It's called wanting somewhere else to look after ten hours on duty."

"That is a less precise description."

"Most of the good ones are."

He remembered the little Nova-class ship with startling clarity. Her narrow passages. The rattle of a serving tray when the impulse drive made an ugly adjustment. The first evening he had activated a holographic snowfall in his quarters because he missed the simple ability to stand in weather. The way a ship's crew could stop being strangers and become the people who knew you at your least polished.

He had reported aboard the *Bozeman-A* as a lieutenant junior grade after his year in Admiral Paris's sector-records office. The work under Paris had involved reading every captain's report and every log submitted from a specified sector, documenting what had happened, what remained uncertain, and what Starfleet should investigate next. On the *Bozeman-A*, he learned that each log was written by a person who sometimes had to choose between competing risks in less than a minute.

A paper career became a lived one.

There had been brilliant scientific work aboard that ship. There had also been a minor catastrophe involving a malfunctioning hydroponic tray, two escaped experimental pollinators, and the ship's entire mess hall. The captain had made Hawkes write the incident report without using the phrase "unforeseeable consequences."

It was, he now thought, an excellent lesson.

### IV. THE READINESS REVIEW

Chief Engineer Maren Voss stood beneath the regulator's nested rings, arms folded, as Doctor Elias Renner explained why the equipment was safer than conventional warp-core auxiliaries.

"The field is localized," Renner said. "The probability of a chronometric propagation event outside the containment envelope is less than one in seventeen million."

"That's an impressive number," Hawkes said.

"Thank you."

"It wasn't a compliment."

Voss almost smiled.

Renner composed himself. "Commander, we have run over eleven thousand simulations. The temporal field is designed to introduce a bounded, reversible offset between two synchronized references."

"And the external reference?"

"Shipyard timekeeping."

"Which receives its correction signal through Avalon's communications system."

"The corrections are minuscule."

"Then disconnect them."

Renner blinked. "Excuse me?"

"Disconnect the correction feed during the experiment. Isolate navigation. Record the shipyard reference through a separate instrument whose output does not pass through our experimental computer. Preserve a sealed record of every initial condition."

"You are asking for an elaborate test of the test."

"Yes."

Hawkes walked around the rings, watching their surfaces catch the light.

"I spent part of my early career reading captain's logs under Admiral Paris. After an accident, the reports are full of phrases like 'within expected tolerances' and 'no credible failure mode.' They are often accurate descriptions of what people believed at the time."

Renner crossed his arms. "And what would you have us believe?"

"Nothing we cannot demonstrate."

"The system cannot make an entire vessel vanish."

"Excellent. Then an independent clock will be bored."

Voss said, "I can isolate the navigation backbone in forty minutes."

"Do it."

Renner frowned. "You are delaying a fleet-priority project."

Hawkes met his eyes. "I'm protecting it from a result nobody will be able to interpret."

### V. THE FIRST ACTIVATION

The isolation took fifty-three minutes.

Renner spent twelve of those minutes arguing that forty would have sufficed.

T'Vel's report later noted this detail with impeccable fairness.

From the bridge, Ellison authorized the test.

"Commander Hawkes, Engineering is yours. If it fails, make sure my ship survives."

"Yes, Captain."

"Then proceed."

The regulator awakened with a soft resonance. Its luminous rings came alive one at a time, surrounding a chamber no larger than a torpedo casing.

Voss called out each reading.

"Containment stable. Reference lock confirmed. Field intensity two percent."

"Chronometric offset?" Hawkes asked.

"Point zero zero three microseconds."

"Outside reference?"

"Aligned."

Hawkes watched the independent analog recorder through a small camera feed. An unremarkable needle crawled over a strip of physical recording medium.

"Five percent."

The tone deepened.

"Ten."

The air seemed to hold its breath. A stray reflection moved across a bulkhead as if a lamp had shifted position, although nothing else had changed.

"Fifteen."

The shipyard monitor went dark.

A second display blanked.

T'Vel looked up. "External synchronization channel has ceased responding."

"Engineering telemetry?" Hawkes asked.

"Normal," Voss said, already sounding angry about it.

Renner leaned toward the main screen. "The field remains completely stable."

"Abort," Hawkes ordered.

"Commander, we are within nominal parameters."

"Abort the test."

Voss closed the isolation circuit. The rings dimmed, then went dark.

Nothing exploded. The ship stayed exactly where it belonged. Air continued moving through the vents. From the bridge, Captain Ellison requested status in a voice so calm that it made the engineering crew straighten.

"Avalon intact," Hawkes reported. "Experimental field shut down. Investigating external reference interruption."

He turned to T'Vel.

"Compare the recordings."

She studied the readouts.

"Internal elapsed time: four minutes, seventeen seconds."

"Shipyard digital reference?"

"Four minutes, seventeen seconds."

"And the isolated recorder?"

T'Vel hesitated, and Hawkes knew something had gone wrong.

"Four minutes, eighteen seconds."

"Repeat."

"The analog strip records an additional second."

Renner was already shaking his head. "A calibration error."

"Possible," Hawkes said.

"It must be a calibration error. Our containment was stable."

"We have one set of instruments that agrees with itself and another that doesn't. That is all we know."

Voss replayed the strip. The trace moved forward in a line of evenly spaced ticks, except where an extra interval appeared as ordinary as all the others.

One second.

The most unremarkable unit of time in the universe, until someone could not explain where it had gone.

### VI. THE REPORT

At 1900, the shipyard commander's office informed Hawkes that the refit schedule was slipping.

At 1920, the Daystrom delegation submitted a preliminary interpretation declaring the analog discrepancy non-significant.

At 1935, Chief Voss brought him the raw data packet and told him she wouldn't sign the Daystrom conclusion.

At 2000, Captain Ellison summoned him to her ready room.

"You're stopping the experiment," she said.

"I'm recommending a halt pending independent review."

"Which will stop it."

"Yes, Captain."

She gestured for him to sit. "Tell me the difference between caution and fear, Commander."

Hawkes considered.

"Fear wants certainty before it acts. Caution wants to know which risks it is actually taking."

"And what do you want?"

"An experiment whose outcome we can trust."

Ellison looked at the data packet. "Will you stand by the recommendation if Admiral Paris says you're wasting the Fleet's resources?"

"Yes."

"If Daystrom says your analysis is incompetent?"

"Yes."

"If they're right?"

Hawkes paused.

"Then I want the report to show that they were right."

That pleased her more than a declaration of confidence would have.

"Good," she said. "Because one day you may sit in that chair, and then you'll have to distinguish the advice you like from the advice you need."

She signed the suspension.

"Send your report."

### VII. ONE UNACCOUNTED SECOND

The report began with a plain title.

**USS AVALON: FIRST TEMPORAL REFIT — EXPERIMENT 001**

**Finding:** Divergence between internal and independent chronological references.

**Observed discrepancy:** One second.

**Cause:** Undetermined.

**Recommendation:** Suspend progressive field testing pending an independent engineering and scientific review.

**Evidence preservation:** Raw instrument data, calibration history, sealed analog recording, initial conditions, and command communications retained without normalization or automated correction.

**Assessment:** No claim of temporal displacement is presently justified.

Hawkes read the last sentence twice.

*The absence of an explanation is not evidence that nothing happened.*

He added it under command commentary.

Then he transmitted the report.

The reply from Admiral Paris arrived twenty-two minutes later.

"Commander Hawkes."

"Admiral."

"I've read your recommendation."

"Yes, sir."

"You have halted the Fleet's most expensive temporal refit over a discrepancy lasting one second."

"An unaccounted second."

Paris leaned back. "Do you believe your ship traveled through time?"

"No, sir."

"Do you believe she didn't?"

"We don't have enough evidence to make either statement."

"Then what are you asking me to authorize?"

"An investigation that can tell the difference."

Paris studied him for a long moment.

"The suspension stands. A separate team will examine the recorder and the reference pathways."

"Thank you, Admiral."

"Don't thank me until they finish. If the analog device was faulty, we're going to have a very expensive discussion."

"I'll be there."

"And, Noah?"

"Sir?"

"When you're a captain, someone is going to bring you a report that threatens everything you want to accomplish. You'll be tempted to call it an anomaly and move on."

Hawkes waited.

"Don't."

The transmission ended.

Hawkes remained in the ready room for another minute. His half-finished drink had gone warm.

"Computer," he said, "Coke Zero with vanilla. Cold."

A fresh glass appeared. He took a sip and considered the sealed report on his desk.

For the first time that day, he allowed himself a thought that had nothing to do with the experiment.

He wanted a ship of his own.

Not because he imagined he could always be right. That would have been an absurd qualification for command.

He wanted the responsibility to hold a course when certainty failed, and to answer for the lives that followed.

He looked at the stars beyond the viewport.

"Make it so," he said quietly.

### VIII. THE NEXT MORNING

By morning, Renner had three competing explanations.

One blamed the recorder motor. One blamed an unmodeled relay latency. The third suggested the external signal had briefly traveled through a chronometric gradient.

All were possible. None had yet survived an independent test.

Hawkes asked that all three remain in the working record with their evidence and contradictions attached.

A young engineering ensign looked troubled.

"Sir, doesn't keeping every theory make the record confusing?"

"It makes the uncertainty visible."

"Wouldn't it be easier to choose the most likely explanation?"

"Eventually. When we have a reason."

The ensign considered this.

"Yes, Commander."

He turned to leave, then stopped.

"Sir? Is this why the ship was chosen for a temporal refit? Because of Avalon?"

Hawkes glanced up. "What about her?"

"The name. Avalon. Somewhere outside ordinary history."

"That's a legend, Ensign. Ships don't become extraordinary because we give them beautiful names."

"What makes them extraordinary, then?"

Hawkes looked through the viewport at the workers moving over the hull.

"The people who bring them home."

Outside, a repair craft drifted toward the dorsal access hatch. Inside, a new experiment waited for permission to begin.

The chronometer advanced by one second.

This time every clock agreed.

**END OF CHAPTER**
