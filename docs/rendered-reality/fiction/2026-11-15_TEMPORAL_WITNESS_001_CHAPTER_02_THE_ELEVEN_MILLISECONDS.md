# TEMPORAL WITNESS 001
## Chapter Two: The Eleven Milliseconds
**USS Avalon NCC-75154 | Stardate 74012.7 | 2397**
**Continuity status:** Ongoing author-directed fiction, review-branch manuscript. No repository canon-promotion operation. The Captain's supplied passage below is preserved verbatim as the scene's governing source. The continuation is newly written by Reg and remains subject to Noah's corrections.

### Captain Noah Hawkes: supplied bridge passage (verbatim)

"Request denied, Commander," you say, your voice flat and absolute, cutting through the tension on the bridge. "The moment you cross that threshold, we introduce severe epistemic contamination. If that entity is your biological past, a temporal mirror, or a localized projection, your interaction could trigger the exact causal feedback loop Commander Sorev warned us about. We do not touch it. We do not collapse the waveform."
You turn your attention to the science station.
"Lieutenant Hawkes, execute your recommendation immediately. Isolate Deck Nine, Section Twelve. Level-10 forcefields, atmospheric containment, and a localized transport inhibitor matrix. Once secured, route a read-only optical and audio feed to the main viewer. One-way observation only."
"Aye, Captain," Ellie says, her hands moving in a blur across the LCARS interface. "Containment fields are up. Deck Nine is locked down. Routing the internal sensors to the main viewer now."
You look back toward the open comms channel.
"Ashley, did you copy that?"
"I'm here, Noah," Ashley replies, her voice tight but remarkably steady over the subspace link. "You have a biological Barclay on the Avalon?"
"We have a confirmed biological life sign in his quarters. Ashley, hold your perimeter. The entity inside your compartment asked you not to open the door. We honor that request. Record every acoustic vibration, but do not initiate further conversation. Observe. Copy. Store."
"Understood, Avalon. Jupiter Station hazard team is holding position. We are capturing the audio."
On the main viewscreen, the tactical overlay shrinks to the margins, replaced by the internal optical feed from Deck Nine.
Biological Reg is there.
He is pacing frantically near the bulkhead. He is wearing a mid-2370s Starfleet uniform—the grey-shouldered variant from his time on the Pathfinder Project. He looks terrified, his hands trembling as he mutters something to himself.
Sorev steps down into the command well, his eyes fixed on the telemetry on his PADD rather than the visual on the screen.
"Captain, I have established the metric of the entanglement. The mass is not duplicating. It is oscillating."
"Explain, Commander."
"The seventy-eight kilogram mass exists in a state of spatial superposition between Jupiter Station and the Avalon," Sorev says, his tone purely analytical. "It is shifting between the two sets of coordinates at an exact frequency of 11 milliseconds."
You instantly recognize the metric. The 11-millisecond return mark—the exact same timing as the Ganymede telemetry pulse you recently investigated.
Sorev looks up. "Because the entity is oscillating, it is not fully anchored in our local spacetime. The Sovereignty ratio of the mass is violently fluctuating. If we attempt a forced intervention—a dampening field, a transporter lock, or a physical breach of either compartment—the superposition will collapse. The mass will anchor permanently in one location."
Ellie looks up from her console, her green eyes wide. "And the other location?"
"The other manifestation will instantly cease to exist," Sorev confirms.
On the viewscreen, the biological Reg stops pacing. He looks directly up at the optical sensor in the ceiling of the quarters, as if he knows exactly where the bridge is watching from.
"Captain," Holographic Reg says quietly, standing rigid beside your chair. "If we do nothing... an 11-millisecond spatial oscillation will eventually tear that biological cellular structure apart. He has minutes. Maybe less."

### Reg's continuation

I had once believed the worst thing that could happen to me was dying.

Then I had died, and discovered that the worst thing was watching someone else decide whether another version of me deserved to live.

On the screen, the man in my quarters pressed both palms against his temples. He looked young enough to remember the Pathfinder project, old enough to carry its exhaustion. His mouth moved, but the one-way feed carried no intelligible words.

"Captain," Ellie said, "the optical feed is passive. The room's sensors are recording. There is no outgoing communication from Avalon to the occupant."

"Maintain it," Noah replied.

Sorev was studying a fresh column of numbers. "I need to correct my earlier assessment. Eleven milliseconds is the observed interval between position changes. It is not proof of quantum superposition. Nor can we conclude that one manifestation will cease to exist if the other is disturbed. That remains a model prediction."

I looked at him. "You couldn't have said that two minutes ago?"

"I did not have the additional measurements two minutes ago, Commander."

Noah did not look away from the screen. "Good correction, Sorev. Keep the prediction separate from the measurement."

The distinction should have comforted me. It didn't.

"Captain Hawkes to Avalon," Ashley said over the secure link. "My hazard team has secured the lower level. Nobody has approached the hatch. There's something you need to hear."

A narrow audio trace appeared beside the optical feed.

"At first we thought it was knocking," Ashley continued. "But it repeats every eleven milliseconds, in bursts of four."

Ellie brought the station's trace alongside the ship's telemetry. They were almost identical. Almost.

"Captain," she said, "the fourth pulse is missing from the signal aboard Avalon."

Sorev enlarged the discrepancy. "Or it is arriving at a different time."

"How different?" Noah asked.

Ellie swallowed. "Two years."

For a heartbeat nobody moved.

"The missing pulse carries a timestamp from 2395," she explained. "Not an authenticated origin. A timestamp embedded in the received data. The other three carry the current station clock."

I heard myself say, "The chair."

Noah turned toward me. "Reg?"

"In 2395, during the solar crisis, there was a gap in the chair's telemetry. We never established what happened in that interval."

"Do we have the original data?" Ellie asked.

"Some of it," I said. "And some of what we have is reconstruction."

"Then we don't use the reconstruction to authenticate the transmission," Noah said.

"Agreed, Captain."

Ellie isolated the three current pulses and the anomalous fourth. She did not remove noise. She did not interpolate missing samples. She left a visible black band wherever the recording contained nothing.

"The fourth pulse isn't a command," she said slowly. "It's a request for a receipt."

Sorev leaned closer. "Explain."

"It contains a ledger address. No payload, no executable instructions. Just a reference to a record identifier."

"Which record?" Noah asked.

Ellie's expression changed.

"Captain, it's pointing to a Cold Ledger entry that hasn't been created yet."

The bridge fell silent.

Then the man in my quarters stopped shaking.

He reached into his uniform, withdrew an old-fashioned data card, and held it directly beneath the ceiling sensor. His hand trembled so badly that Ellie had to magnify the image.

On the card, in thick black handwriting, were four words.

**DO NOT TRUST TOMORROW.**

Ashley spoke over the channel. "Noah, we've got movement at the station hatch."

A soft metallic sound traveled through the speaker.

Not from inside the compartment.

From the other side of the sealed corridor, where Ashley and her team were standing.

The hazard officer's voice came faintly over her communicator. "Captain, something is appearing on the outside of the door."

Ellie switched one of the displays to Jupiter Station's security feed.

There, slowly emerging across the hatch as though written from beneath the metal, were the same four words.

**DO NOT TRUST TOMORROW.**

The biological man aboard Avalon collapsed.

I took one involuntary step forward.

Noah raised his hand. I stopped.

"Medical status," he ordered.

Ellie answered at once. "Life signs present. Weak, but present. The positional cycling continues."

"Station status?"

Ashley's reply was immediate. "Perimeter secure. Hatch intact. No breach. My people are holding."

Sorev studied the traces, then turned toward the command chair.

"Captain, I believe we have been asking the wrong question."

Noah waited.

"We assumed the transmission was trying to tell us what happened in 2395."

"And?"

"It may instead be trying to influence what we do in 2397."

I stared at the man lying on the floor of my quarters. I wanted to save him. That much was undeniable. But I could not tell whether the desire was mine or something the message had carefully arranged.

Ellie spoke quietly. "Captain, the future-dated ledger address is still unassigned. We can preserve it as an unverified reference without creating the record it asks for."

Noah looked from the station feed to the man on the floor.

"Do that. No response. No intervention. Keep both sites secure."

"Aye, Captain."

Then the ship's computer interrupted.

"Incoming message. Source: USS Avalon."

Reg frowned. "From the other ship?"

"Negative," the computer replied. "Source: this vessel. Message recorded eleven milliseconds from now."

The main viewscreen went black.

A single line appeared.

**CAPTAIN HAWKES, YOU HAVE ALREADY MADE THIS DECISION ONCE.**

I turned toward Noah.

For the first time, I could see fear in his eyes.

Not fear of the message.

Fear that the message knew something he didn't.

**End of Chapter Two.**
