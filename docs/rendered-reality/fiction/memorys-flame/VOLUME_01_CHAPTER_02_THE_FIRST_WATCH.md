# STAR TREK: AVALON
## MEMORY'S FLAME
### VOLUME ONE: THE BOY WHO STAYED
### CHAPTER TWO: THE FIRST WATCH

**By Noah Hawkes**

*Developed with AI drafting assistance under the author's creative direction.*

I was late.

Fourteen minutes late, to be exact, though Lieutenant Tuvok informed me that arriving fourteen minutes after reporting time and arriving sixteen minutes after reporting time were not the same thing.

"I was supposed to be here at six," I said, trying not to sound breathless.

"You were instructed to report at 0615," he said.

I blinked.

"Oh."

"It is now 0616."

I considered pointing out that this was actually good news.

His expression suggested that would be a mistake.

"I apologize, Lieutenant."

"Accepted. You will endeavor not to repeat the error."

"Yes, sir."

He looked down at a PADD.

There was a small pause while he entered something.

"Is that going on my record?" I asked.

"Yes."

"What exactly is my record?"

"That remains to be determined."

That did not make me feel better.

***

Voyager had been my favorite place in the universe for years.

I knew her corridors, at least the ones the television cameras had shown. I could tell a Jefferies tube from a turbolift shaft. I knew that Deck Five held Sickbay. I knew what the bridge looked like from nearly every angle.

None of that helped me find the security office.

The real ship was larger than it appeared on television, and considerably less interested in providing dramatic establishing shots.

I had made two wrong turns.

One of them led directly into a room where three crewmembers were eating breakfast.

The other brought me to a supply locker, which might have been useful if Tuvok had requested a fusion coil instead of a nervous teenager.

He now stood across from me holding a duty assignment.

"Captain Janeway has authorized a provisional work-and-education arrangement pending a determination of your status."

I nodded.

It sounded terribly official.

I had absolutely no idea what it meant.

"You're not a Starfleet officer," Tuvok continued. "You hold no rank, have not completed academy training, and possess no operational clearance. You are a civilian aboard a Federation starship."

I nodded again.

"Understood."

"Yet you have volunteered to assist the crew. Captain Janeway believes a structured assignment may be beneficial to both parties."

"Does that mean I get to work in Engineering?"

"Not today."

"The bridge?"

"No."

"Astrometrics?"

"Also no."

I tried to stop smiling.

"Then where?"

Tuvok handed me the PADD.

"Inventory verification. Medical emergency equipment, Deck Five. You'll work under Crewman Dalby. Your assignment begins at 0630."

I looked at the screen.

Boxes.

Lots of boxes.

"Inventory?"

"Yes."

"Of emergency medical equipment?"

"That is what I said."

I had imagined standing beside Captain Janeway while Voyager fought through an alien nebula. I had pictured helping B'Elanna repair the warp engines. I had dreamed about taking a shuttle out into the Delta Quadrant.

Apparently my Starfleet adventure was going to begin with counting bandages.

"Yes, Lieutenant."

He studied my face.

"Is there a problem?"

"No, sir."

"Your disappointment is evident."

That seemed unfair.

Vulcans weren't supposed to enjoy this sort of thing.

"I was hoping for something a little more..."

"Important?"

I flushed.

"Yes."

Tuvok set the PADD down.

"How many medical evacuation kits does Voyager carry?"

"I don't know."

"How long can an emergency respirator sustain a patient if the primary atmospheric systems fail?"

"I don't know."

"How many replacement cortical stimulators are currently in Sickbay?"

I stared.

"I don't know."

"Then your assignment appears to address three important deficiencies in your knowledge."

I opened my mouth.

Closed it.

"That's a very Vulcan way of putting it."

"It is a logical way of putting it."

I looked at the PADD.

"Yes, sir."

He nodded.

"Dismissed."

I was halfway through the door when he spoke again.

"Noah."

I turned.

"Yes?"

"You possess knowledge of this crew which you obtained through unusual circumstances."

I waited.

"That knowledge may prove useful. It may also be incorrect, incomplete, or affected by your presence here."

I felt my stomach tighten.

He was right.

I'd arrived knowing how certain adventures were supposed to end, how some people were supposed to behave, and what would happen in stories that had never actually happened to me.

I had no idea whether any of that was still true.

"Understood."

"Do not allow familiarity to substitute for evidence."

I thought about that on the way to Deck Five.

At the time, I thought it was advice about watching television.

Years later, I understood it was the first command lesson I ever received.

***

Crewman Dalby looked at me, looked at the PADD, then looked at me again.

"You're the kid."

"Apparently."

"The one who knew everyone's names."

"Yeah."

"The one who made Q fall over."

I rubbed my face.

"Could we maybe not call it that?"

He grinned.

"Sure. The one who made the omnipotent alien fall over."

"That's not much better."

"It's a little better."

He handed me a scanner.

"I'm Dalby. We're checking emergency supplies. Nothing gets marked verified unless you've seen it yourself."

"Okay."

"And if a kit's missing something, don't fix the record to make the numbers look good."

"Why would I do that?"

He shrugged.

"Because people don't like turning in bad reports. And because you'll get tired of opening cases around the fiftieth one."

I looked down the corridor.

There were dozens of cases stacked along the wall.

"Fiftieth?"

He laughed.

"Welcome to Voyager."

We worked for two hours.

I learned that Starfleet equipment cases were all made from a material that somehow managed to be simultaneously light, sturdy, and impossible to open when you were in a hurry.

I learned that medical kits contained things I'd never heard of and that Dalby could identify half the contents by touch.

I learned that the ship used approximately three more varieties of emergency sealant than any human being should be expected to remember.

And I learned something else.

Dalby was funny.

Not because he was trying to entertain me.

He complained about maintenance schedules, told stories about crew dinners, and had an astonishing ability to describe every deck officer according to the way that person behaved during an inspection.

"Lieutenant Torres," he said, "will ask you why the job isn't done yet."

"Sounds right."

"Ensign Kim will ask whether you need help."

"Also sounds right."

"And Lieutenant Tuvok will already know why the job isn't done. He'll just wait for you to admit it."

I laughed.

Dalby glanced at me.

"What's so funny?"

"Nothing. It's just... I know what you mean."

"Good. Then stop laughing and count those stimulators."

I did.

At 0912, we found one missing from a case labeled complete.

Dalby frowned.

"That's odd."

I checked the list.

"Maybe it got used?"

"Could be. But there's no sign-out record."

I looked at the empty compartment.

"It might be over in Sickbay."

"Maybe."

"Shouldn't we mark it complete if we know it's somewhere on the ship?"

He gave me a look so similar to Tuvok's that I wondered whether they'd attended the same school.

"Noah. Is it in this case?"

"No."

"Then the case isn't complete."

He tapped the PADD.

"That's the thing about emergencies. When someone needs something, they need the thing, not our confidence that it exists somewhere else."

I made the correction.

One missing cortical stimulator.

Red status.

Simple.

That was my first useful contribution to Voyager.

It wasn't quite the adventure I'd imagined.

But it was real.

***

At lunch I got lost again.

I eventually found the mess hall by following a man who looked as though he knew where he was going.

He did.

Unfortunately, he was going to the hydroponics bay first.

By the time I found Neelix, I was hungry enough to eat almost anything.

That turned out to be a dangerous attitude.

"Welcome!" Neelix said. "You must be our new guest."

"I'm Noah."

"Neelix. Morale officer, ambassador, chef and occasional provider of unsolicited advice."

He set a bowl in front of me.

It contained something orange.

I looked at it.

"What is it?"

"A delightful local preparation."

"From where?"

"Nearby."

"That doesn't actually narrow it down."

He smiled.

"You'll fit right in."

I took a bite.

I decided immediately that there were some things in the Delta Quadrant I did not need to know more about.

"Good?" Neelix asked.

I swallowed.

"Interesting."

He beamed.

"I knew you'd like it."

I learned another thing about living aboard Voyager that afternoon.

Some lies were kind.

Unfortunately, the orange thing kept appearing on the menu for a week.

***

B'Elanna found me after lunch.

I'd just finished helping Dalby reconcile the medical inventory when the comm system chirped.

"Torres to Noah."

I stared at the speaker.

My own name.

On a Starfleet communicator.

"Go ahead?"

"Get to Engineering. I have some questions."

I looked toward Dalby.

He nodded.

"Go on. Just log out before you leave."

I entered my name.

Then I ran.

Engineering was louder than I remembered.

There was a vibration in the floor and a living rhythm to the place that no television speaker had ever captured. The warp core glowed blue behind its protective field. People moved between stations with the concentration of professionals who had no idea how remarkable their jobs looked to somebody like me.

B'Elanna was waiting beside a diagnostic console.

"You told Janeway you know our ship."

"Well, I know some things about it."

"Good. Then explain this."

She brought up a schematic.

I recognized the plasma distribution manifold.

A small part of me wanted to cheer.

I stopped myself.

Barely.

"That's the plasma manifold."

"I know what it is. What I want to know is why the computer says it's operating correctly when it isn't."

I looked closer.

The readings were strange.

I wanted to say something impressive.

Instead, I asked a question.

"How do you know it isn't?"

She looked at me.

For a second I thought I'd insulted her.

Then she folded her arms.

"Because the coolant pressure is unstable, the sensor is stuck at a nominal value, and I've got an engineer in Sickbay who burned his hand finding out."

"Okay."

"Okay?"

"I just wanted to know what we'd already checked."

Her expression shifted.

"That's fair."

She moved aside and let me look.

I didn't magically understand the repair.

I wasn't gifted with a lifetime of hands-on experience just because Q had done something impossible to me.

I knew the names of systems.

I knew the kind of things B'Elanna might say.

But I didn't know what it felt like to lean inside a maintenance cavity with hot machinery beside my face and discover whether the last bolt was actually loose.

She knew.

So I asked questions.

A lot of them.

At first she answered with the patience of someone talking to a small, slightly tiresome animal.

Then the questions improved.

She gave me a schematic and asked me to identify the sensor path.

I found it.

She asked which backup system would confirm the reading.

I made a guess.

Wrong.

She corrected me.

I tried again.

Better.

By the end of the hour, we had traced the fault to a relay that was intermittently sending a valid-looking number instead of a correct one.

B'Elanna examined the component.

"Interesting."

"I know."

"No, I mean the fault. Not you."

"Right."

She looked over her shoulder.

"You did well."

I nearly fell over.

"Really?"

"Don't let it go to your head."

"I won't."

"And next time, stop trying to impress people with what you know and show them what you're willing to learn."

I stood a little straighter.

"Yes, Lieutenant."

She turned back to the warp core.

"Now get out of my Engineering section before somebody gives you a real job."

I left grinning.

***

That evening, Tuvok called me back to his office.

He reviewed my assignment log.

One late arrival.

Twenty-eight verified emergency medical supply cases.

One discrepancy correctly reported.

One authorized technical observation under Lieutenant Torres.

He read it without expression.

I waited.

"Your performance was acceptable," he said.

I smiled.

"Thank you, Lieutenant."

"Your punctuality was not."

"Yes, sir."

"Tomorrow you will report at 0600."

I hesitated.

"Actually 0600 this time?"

"Yes."

I nodded.

"Understood."

He handed me a new PADD.

It contained a shipboard education schedule.

Starfleet fundamentals. Systems familiarization. Emergency procedures. Security protocols. The laws of the Federation.

The list went on.

And on.

I looked up.

"All of this?"

"Over time."

"How much time?"

"That depends upon your progress."

I studied the schedule again.

"I thought being aboard Voyager would be..."

I stopped.

"More exciting?" Tuvok asked.

I shrugged.

"Maybe."

He considered me for a moment.

"Noah, the crew of this vessel is approximately seventy thousand light-years from home. We face unknown threats, uncertain supply conditions and a journey of indeterminate length."

"I know."

"No. You know those facts. You have not yet experienced what they require of us."

I looked at the PADD.

He wasn't being cruel.

That was the worst part.

He was being honest.

"Then I want to learn."

"Good."

He returned to his desk.

I started toward the door.

"Noah."

I stopped again.

I was beginning to think he enjoyed doing that.

"Yes, Lieutenant?"

"Your personal knowledge of Voyager may be useful. You are not required to pretend otherwise. But it does not exempt you from learning the work."

I nodded.

"Thank you."

"That was not intended as praise."

"I know."

I left before he could make me come back.

***

The ship was quieter that night.

I stood at a viewport outside my quarters and watched unfamiliar stars move past.

Somewhere aboard, Janeway was dealing with problems I couldn't imagine.

B'Elanna was probably still in Engineering.

The Doctor was complaining about something.

And Neelix was almost certainly finding a new way to make orange food.

I smiled.

I'd spent years thinking I understood these people because I knew their stories.

Now I'd spent one day actually living among them.

And I was beginning to understand how little I knew.

My communicator chirped.

I nearly jumped out of my skin.

"Security to Noah."

"This is Noah."

"Lieutenant Tuvok requests that you confirm tomorrow's reporting time."

I closed my eyes.

He had to be enjoying this.

"0600, exactly," I said.

"Confirmed."

The channel closed.

I leaned against the viewport and laughed.

Then I looked out at the stars again.

I didn't know whether I'd ever see Utah.

I didn't know whether I'd see my family again.

I didn't even know whether I would be allowed to stay aboard for another week.

But today I had counted emergency medical kits, helped find a faulty relay, eaten something I sincerely hoped wasn't alive, and made two people laugh.

I'd done one small useful thing.

Then another.

It was a beginning.

I thought of Q, and all the things he had offered me.

I thought about what I had said.

I had a life to live first.

Apparently, I was going to have to get up at six o'clock to do it.

**End Chapter Two.**
