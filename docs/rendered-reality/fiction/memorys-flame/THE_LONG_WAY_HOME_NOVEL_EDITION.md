# STAR TREK: AVALON
## MEMORY’S FLAME
### THE LONG WAY HOME

**By Noah Alexander Hawkes**  
**Novel-style episode · Revised edition · October 13, 2026**

---

## Chapter One: The Wrong Number

**Jupiter Station · Stardate 74000.1 · 2397**

The first person to call us from another history wanted to complain about a refrigerator.

I was in Avalon’s ready room, trying to read a temporal-navigation review while the station’s docking crew performed a procedure that sounded like someone dragging a cupboard through the hull. Jupiter occupied half the window. Beyond it, the station’s residential rings caught the sun.

Home was over there. Work was under my feet. On most mornings, the distance between them was reassuringly short.

Reg sat opposite me. He had suspended the predictive displays he usually kept somewhere behind his eyes, and for the past ten minutes we had managed a conversation without either of us mentioning the end of the universe.

Then my communicator chirped.

“Ellie to Captain Hawkes. We have an unusual civilian call.”

“Put it through.”

“I think you should come to the bridge first.”

Reg looked at me.

“The last time someone said that,” I told him, “there were three suns.”

“Two and a disputed reflection, Captain.”

“Not better.”

We reached the bridge together. At the AI station, Ellie had one finger hovering over a playback control. Sorev stood behind her, perfectly straight, wearing the expression he used when something had offended mathematics.

“Let’s hear it,” I said.

A woman’s voice filled the bridge.

“Hello? Habitat Seven, service line. Leda Marr. We’ve been trying to reach maintenance for twenty minutes. The refrigeration alarm won’t stop, and the laboratory doors have locked. If this is another automated menu, I’m going to put the speaker in the recycler.”

Somewhere on the recording, a child asked whether they could open the freezer.

“No, you cannot,” Leda said. “Not unless you’d like to eat everything in it right now.”

Ellie stopped the playback.

“Ganymede,” she said.

“We have Ganymede maintenance on speed dial?”

“We have this Ganymede maintenance address in historical records. It was retired decades ago.”

Sorev put the carrier analysis on the viewer.

“The transmission’s chroniton content indicates temporal displacement. Its local date field reads 2329.”

I looked toward Reg.

“Old recording?”

“Her carrier is responding to present receiver conditions. Someone is calling now, Captain. Their now is sixty-eight years behind ours.”

The voice had sounded tired, annoyed, ordinary. That made the date on the screen harder to absorb.

“And the branch?”

Reg examined the trace longer this time.

“It doesn’t match our historical reference.”

Another Federation. Another Ganymede. A woman trying to keep a child away from a freezer, somehow reaching a starship that did not belong to her century or her history.

“Did we answer?”

“No voice response,” Ellie said. “I stopped it as soon as the temporal alert appeared.”

“Preserve the whole thing. Raw carrier, routing information, everything.”

Reg was already preparing the station comparison.

Ashley answered from Jupiter Operations. She was standing beneath a traffic display filled with approach vectors. An officer beside her pointed to a blinking cargo manifest; she acknowledged it with two fingers while listening to us.

“We captured the call independently,” she said. “My isolated receiver has it too. No change in our home reference.”

“Can you identify the crossing?”

“A narrow connection near Ganymede. We’re still resolving it.”

I glanced around the bridge. Sorev waited. Reg watched the carrier. Ellie had removed the playback from the live communications queue.

“We need to find out how something from another history got into ours.”

Ashley nodded.

“I’ll hold your reference. You investigate the crossing. Nobody assumes a distress call gives us ownership of the situation at the other end.”

“Agreed.”

I took my chair.

“Prepare Avalon for temporal departure. Passive investigation. Cloak before entry. No contact without my order.”

Reg stood beside me.

“Destination: Ganymede, 2329. Return reference: this station, this history, 2397.”

“Hold the tether, Reg. I don’t want to get lost out there.”

For a moment, his humor left him.

“Neither do I, Captain.”

## Chapter Two: The Line Home

**USS Avalon · Stardate 74000.2 · Departure year 2397 / destination year 2329**

Brakk came to the bridge because he distrusted reassuring lights.

He stood beside the helm, holding a PADD with the return circuit displayed on it. Every indicator was green. He looked personally offended.

“Explain it again,” I said. “Without making my eyes bleed.”

Reg took the PADD.

“The drive can put us in a year. The tether tells us which history we came from. Jupiter keeps answering with a reference only our station can authenticate.”

“So we follow the signal back.”

“With continuous checks. We do not allow local conditions to redefine what home means.”

Brakk jabbed at one junction.

“And this circuit has reserve power. If the cloak, sensors, and tether all want the same energy, the tether eats first.”

Sorev reviewed the departure conditions.

“Matching clocks are necessary but insufficient. Two histories can both contain the year 2397.”

“Like two houses with the same street number,” Ellie said.

“Precisely,” Reg replied. “I would prefer not to arrive at the wrong one with the captain’s laundry.”

The explanation helped. The jokes helped more. They meant my officers had enough room in their minds to be themselves.

Reg did not always have that room.

In 2395, Avalon’s first full temporal-core activation had begun confusing possible events with events that had actually happened. The cascade threatened Sol. Reginald Barclay had rebuilt the Cytherian interface chair and entered the holodeck with a reference the runaway core could not rewrite.

He saved us.

His body did not survive.

The man standing beside my chair did. He carried his history, his anxieties, his brilliance, and a new ability to perceive more histories than any biological mind should have to endure.

Sometimes I wondered whether the ordinary shipboard jokes were how he found his footing among them.

“Counselor when we return,” I told him quietly.

“Captain?”

“You heard me.”

He looked toward the blue home-reference line.

“Yes, sir.”

Ashley’s final departure authorization came through the relay.

“Jupiter reference holds. Receiving epoch reserved. Avalon controls the maneuver.”

“Execute.”

The ship’s hum deepened until I felt it in my teeth. Jupiter elongated across the viewer, then thinned to a golden thread.

The blue line remained.

When the planet returned, the navigation beacons had changed. The station was absent. Ganymede’s settlements occupied different locations on the ice.

“Local year 2329,” Sorev said. “Foreign branch signature consistent with the received transmission.”

“And our arrival relative to the call?”

“Before its embedded local timestamp. We have time to observe the disturbance that carried it.”

It was one of the things I disliked about temporal investigations. At home, a call had already arrived. Here, the equipment failure that sent it was still ahead of us. The received date was an address, not an instruction to arrive after everything was over.

“Cloak?”

“Stable,” Brakk answered. “Which I intend to keep true.”

Ganymede turned below us. Above its inhabited limb hung a ship assembled around an opening in space.

Three hull sections revolved slowly around a bright aperture. Pale filaments held them in alignment. A beam reached down toward a cluster of habitat lights.

“That,” Ellie said, “is not maintenance.”

Reg watched the ship.

“No. But I suspect it explains why nobody is answering their telephone.”

## Chapter Three: An Enterprise in the Dark

**Ganymede · Stardate 74000.3 · 2329**

Our first scan gave us a shape, then rooms, then people.

The forward hull contained a bridge, habitation spaces, and a medical compartment crowded with moving life signs. The other sections carried most of the machinery surrounding the aperture. Each had its own impulse engines.

“At least eighty-three aboard,” Sorev said. “Internal shielding prevents a complete count.”

“Federation?”

“The reactor harmonics have Starfleet ancestry,” Reg replied. “That is stronger evidence than the emblem painted on the hull.”

“How advanced?”

Brakk answered from Engineering.

“They’ve made three ships behave like moving components inside one engine. Our yard would spend a year arguing about the docking tolerances.”

The pale beam below them interrupted Ganymede’s traffic. Routine messages continued. Laboratory reports and agricultural transfers vanished from the queue or returned under changed routing codes.

Reg isolated a fragment.

“Sample transport request. Diverted.”

“They’re suppressing a discovery?”

“Possibly. We need more than one message.”

Another call came from Habitat Seven. We listened without answering.

Leda Marr was now trying to reach the habitat administrator. Her irritation had given way to fear. A child was crying in the background, and someone else kept telling her the door would open when the alarm reset.

“Environmental readings?” I asked.

“Stable,” Sorev said. “At present, the communication failure exceeds the physical emergency.”

It was a careful answer. Doors could stay closed while people panicked behind them.

Ellie looked up.

“Starfleet dispatch. A vessel has been diverted to Ganymede.”

She put the name on the viewer.

USS Enterprise. NCC-1701-B.

I stood before I meant to.

“Enterprise-B?”

Reg smiled at my expression.

“Yes, Captain. Refitted Excelsior class.”

“In 2329?”

“Compatible with several of the station’s reference histories. One places her disappearance during this year. Her presence alone does not tell us which history we’re visiting.”

“Then let’s not become the reason she disappears.”

“An excellent mission amendment.”

The warp flare came from beyond the moon. Enterprise emerged with her shields down and her long hull gleaming in reflected sunlight. I knew the shape from a thousand pictures. Seeing it move was something else.

She approached the perimeter and stopped.

“Unidentified vessel near Ganymede, this is Captain Thomas Johnson of the Federation starship Enterprise. Identify yourself and explain your interference with civilian communications.”

The strange ship kept rotating.

Then a voice answered.

“Captain Ilyan Rusk. Federation temporal intervention vessel Asterion. Withdraw. Your presence compromises a containment operation.”

Johnson did not withdraw.

“Transmit your authorization.”

“It originates beyond your historical jurisdiction.”

“Your ship is presently inside my jurisdiction.”

Brakk made a pleased sound over the bridge feed.

“I like him.”

I leaned back into my chair.

“Nobody transmits anything. Let Enterprise handle her own backyard.”

Reg sat beside me.

“Aye, Captain.”

On the viewer, the beautiful old ship held steady against machinery from a future its crew had never imagined.

## Chapter Four: Two Tenders

Rusk sent Enterprise a file and a warning.

“Thirty-eight years from now, a derivative of the organism being studied in that laboratory enters Federation atmospheric reclamation systems. Eleven inhabited worlds become uninhabitable. We are preventing its discovery.”

Johnson took several seconds to answer.

“Your evidence will be examined. Restore the habitat’s communications.”

“We cannot permit the sample transfer.”

“Then speak to the people preparing it.”

“We know what they will say.”

Reg’s fingers tightened against his knee.

“No,” he whispered.

Johnson opened a direct channel to the habitat. An administrator began explaining that containment had already been initiated. Asterion’s beam brightened, and his voice disappeared.

Enterprise launched an optical communications buoy.

It drifted beneath the saucer, found the habitat with its laser, and began establishing a line the subspace jammer could not swallow.

Asterion turned one hull section.

The buoy vanished.

There was no explosion. No debris. Just stars where Federation equipment had been.

“Localized displacement,” Sorev said.

“Destination?”

“Unresolved.”

Johnson raised shields.

“Return that buoy, Captain Rusk.”

“It was unmanned.”

“That was not my question.”

Then the maintenance tender detached from Ganymede’s docking arm.

At first it looked like an ordinary departure. Its engines flickered, cut out, and fired again at the wrong angle.

“Tender Four to control. We’ve lost approach guidance. Request manual clearance.”

The reply vanished inside the suppression field.

The tender drifted toward the aperture.

I reached for the armrest controls.

Reg saw me.

“Enterprise has detected it.”

Johnson’s voice followed almost immediately.

“Tender Four, cut your engines. We have you.”

Enterprise accelerated. A tractor beam caught the little craft and began drawing it away.

The aperture pulsed.

The beam split.

Two tenders appeared in nearly the same place.

Both cried for help.

One voice arrived half a second before the other. They said the same words until one pilot screamed, and the other asked what had happened to the ship beside him.

Our bridge fell silent.

“Which one is real?” an officer at Operations asked.

“Both,” Reg said.

Sorev isolated their tracks.

“Identical up to separation. Different afterward.”

Asterion transmitted.

“Release the trailing vessel. Its persistence destabilizes the field.”

Johnson ordered a second tractor lock.

“Both tenders, shut down your reactors. Maintain emergency batteries. We are bringing you aboard separately.”

“One of those vessels did not exist before your intervention.”

“Neither did this emergency.”

Enterprise held both beams. Her engines flared as the aperture tried to drag the tenders back.

For a terrible few seconds, the old ship was doing work our modern systems could barely explain. Her crew did not need an explanation before deciding whether those people were worth rescuing.

The first tender cleared the field.

Then the second.

Enterprise’s shuttle-bay doors opened.

I realized I had been standing again.

“There you go,” I said softly.

Reg watched the last craft disappear into the bay.

“Yes, Captain.”

When Asterion finally withdrew the suppression beam, the habitat channels flooded with accumulated messages. Docking requests, environmental reports, an irritated botanist, a lunch order nobody had canceled.

Administrator Venn’s voice returned.

“The sample transfer was stopped twenty minutes ago. Your first pulse tripped our laboratory containment system. Nothing has left.”

Rusk answered after a pause.

“That is inconsistent with the recorded sequence.”

Reg looked at the beam, then the habitat.

“He’s still trying to stop the event in his file,” he said. “He isn’t listening to the event in front of him.”

## Chapter Five: The Wrong Future

**USS Avalon · Stardate 74000.4 · 2329**

Enterprise began working with Asterion to reduce the aperture’s output.

The two ships made an unlikely pair: an Excelsior providing an external damping field while the future vessel adjusted three separate hulls around a shrinking opening.

Our science displays filled with phase changes.

“Temporal wake crosses our position,” Ellie warned.

“Move above its plane,” I ordered. “Impulse only. Keep us cloaked.”

Avalon climbed.

The viewer doubled.

For half a second, I saw Jupiter through the deck.

“What was that?”

“Phase excursion,” Brakk barked. “Holding the home reference. Cloak compensation recovering.”

The deck became solid again. My stomach took longer.

“Tether?”

“Authenticated,” Ellie said.

Sorev turned toward me.

“Asterion has detected us. They are hailing.”

Reg closed most of the branch display, leaving one blue line.

“They have a contact. Not our home coordinates.”

We received without answering.

“Unknown vessel,” Rusk said, “we have recorded your phase recovery. You are maintaining an external temporal reference. Do not disconnect it.”

I looked at Reg.

“He recognized what saved us,” he said. “That doesn’t mean he can read it.”

Then Rusk asked for help.

His engineer released diagnostic packets over the same narrow carrier. Commander Nera Vale’s voice was brisk, strained, and much more specific than her captain’s.

“Original receiver returns included. No acknowledgment required. Our departure-reference failure is in the second block.”

Sorev opened it in quarantine.

Three section clocks. One central integrator. A separate beacon receiver.

“They have an external reference,” Reg said.

“Then why are they lost?”

“Their computer rejected it.”

The failure timestamp preceded the laboratory containment. It coincided with the displacement of Enterprise’s buoy.

The buoy remained displaced. We had no authenticated destination or recovery report.

Johnson kept its last measured position in the incident record.

Then he asked Rusk whether his return coordinates were valid.

“They are valid,” Rusk said.

Vale’s voice came from farther away.

“Captain. The coordinates are valid. The destination isn’t there.”

The phrase stayed with me.

I had spent the mission worrying about ours.

“Could we speak privately?” I asked.

Reg described a narrow reply along their existing beam, separated from the tether. He warned that Enterprise could notice scattered energy, and that Rusk could disclose anything we said.

I opened the channel anyway.

Rusk appeared on a bridge with two dark consoles and one open maintenance panel. He looked younger than his voice sounded. Vale stood behind him, holding a diagnostic probe.

“This is Captain Noah Hawkes of Avalon. State your condition and intentions.”

He reported overheating machinery, injured crew, and declining reserves.

“Your departure year,” I said. “Your Federation. Your reference station.”

“2461. United Federation of Planets. Meridian Anchorage, Jovian system.”

“And the beacon?”

He hesitated.

Vale answered.

“It says 2494.”

“Thirty-three years,” Reg said.

Rusk looked toward a console.

“My daughter is in its personnel register. Same name. Same birth date. She’s forty.”

“How old when you left?”

“Seven.”

He played the recording.

“Dad. Stop adjusting the corridor. We’ve kept the anchorage transmitting. Whatever year it is for you, we’re still here.”

Nobody on Avalon spoke.

Rusk stopped the playback.

“I don’t know whether that’s my daughter.”

I thought of Ashley beyond a blue line on a screen. Of Ender leaving a model unfinished because he expected me to be home to look at it.

“Hold position,” I said. “We’ll examine what you’ve received.”

Reg looked at me before he nodded.

It was the first promise I should have made more carefully.

## Chapter Six: The Captain’s Table

The conference room gave the problem enough space to become worse.

Reg put Meridian’s rejected packets above the table. Thirty-three years of maintenance, personnel transfers, oscillator replacements, and reference-array repairs.

“Their receiver has been collecting these,” he said. “The integrator quarantined them because the date was wrong.”

“So nobody read them?”

Vale’s image occupied the wall monitor.

“We examined the failure reports. Not the complete maintenance sequence.”

Brakk arrived with a mug and sat heavily.

“Your computer wrote ‘bad signal’ on the box. Everybody kept reading the label.”

Vale looked tired enough to appreciate the criticism.

“Yes.”

Sorev selected two overlapping entries.

“These crews maintained custody across a shift transfer. Their records disagree on a calibration offset and preserve the correction.”

“Meaning?” I asked.

“A history has been recorded here. It is not merely one clean file asserting that thirty-three years elapsed.”

Reg enlarged an oscillator replacement.

“This change gives us a recognizable difference between possible stations. It narrows the search.”

“Find it.”

He opened an observational display from Avalon’s core. Stations flashed through it too quickly for the rest of us to resolve. Reg slowed them, isolated a few, then compared each against the packet sequence.

One candidate faded. Another did not have the replacement array.

A third remained.

Meridian Anchorage, 2494.

Rusk stood on the monitor.

“That’s our spindle.”

“The old part,” Vale said. “Those rings are new.”

Reg held up a hand.

“A candidate. Configuration matches several records. We need an independent examination before treating it as your destination.”

“Can you see my daughter?” Rusk asked.

“I haven’t searched for her.”

“Why not?”

“Because finding someone who looks right could persuade us before the navigation evidence does.”

Rusk sat down.

I understood the answer. I also disliked how much it cost him to hear it.

We muted the foreign channel and discussed our authority.

Sorev spoke first.

“We have not established that this century is ancestral to their departure branch. Nor that their predicament directly threatens our protected history.”

“Their distress call reached us.”

“Leda Marr’s call reached us. That is the crossing we came to investigate.”

It was an important distinction, and an irritating one.

Reg folded his hands.

“We can review evidence. Opening a route for them would be a separate decision.”

Brakk looked at the image of Asterion on his PADD.

“I’d prefer they stop spending power on a corridor that doesn’t go where they expect. Their medical bus is struggling.”

“And Enterprise?”

“Johnson is keeping the system safe,” Sorev said. “He has local responsibility.”

Three courses emerged. Continue the investigation. Leave the crisis with Enterprise. Withdraw home.

I leaned toward the second.

Every second Avalon sat there, a ship from our century was adding something to a history that had not asked for us. We had already made contact. Reg was already searching their future.

“We could be changing this timeline just by being here.”

“Yes,” Sorev said.

“And we might be leaving a father stranded while his daughter keeps calling.”

Nobody answered immediately.

Reg eventually looked at me.

“She’s already waited thirty-three years, if the beacon is genuine. We should be careful about deciding which of those years we’re entitled to erase.”

“I’m not trying to erase her life.”

“I know.”

He sounded as though he did know, and was frightened of something close enough to resemble it.

Ashley received our jurisdiction update. Her answer was brief: no change detected in our protected reference; limit exposure; keep the return corridor clear.

Outside, Enterprise continued her work.

“We’re withdrawing,” I said. “Keep the evidence. Leave the outcome with them.”

Reg nodded.

“Aye, Captain.”

Before the foreign channel reopened, Brakk’s mug rolled slightly across the table with a course adjustment.

I caught it.

“Your dwarf wouldn’t let that happen.”

He looked at me, then took the mug.

“My dwarf owns a properly leveled tavern table.”

Reg blinked.

“I thought he bought a forge.”

“Diversification.”

For a few seconds, the room belonged to our crew again. A holodeck tavern waited at home: rain against the windows, dice on wood, Brakk arguing with a fantasy mayor about apprentice wages.

I wanted to get them back to it.

## Chapter Seven: The Ship That Wouldn’t Leave

**USS Avalon · Stardate 74001.0 · 2329**

Rusk listened to my explanation without interrupting.

I told him our destination assessment was incomplete. That Enterprise remained responsible for local safety. That we had entered to examine a crossover into our history and could not assume authority over his.

“You said you could help,” he replied.

“I said we would examine the evidence. We have. We cannot promise a return voyage.”

“Then finish.”

“Captain, continuing because we began is not enough reason to keep interfering.”

He looked toward Reg.

“You understand the reference system.”

“Portions of it,” Reg said. “Commander Vale understands your machinery.”

“She can’t see what you see.”

I leaned forward.

“Neither of us can give you certainty we haven’t established.”

Rusk glanced at his daughter’s console.

“How can you leave?”

“Because I don’t know what helping you would do to everyone else in this history.”

He looked as though he had physically swallowed the answer.

We closed the channel.

On the bridge, Reg ordered our return vector prepared. Ellie asked Jupiter to reserve the receiving lock. The home line remained blue.

Then Sorev looked up.

“Asterion’s projector is turning toward us.”

The violet light formed before we could clear its path.

Avalon shuddered.

“Cloak compensation in resonance,” Brakk shouted. “Tether or cloak, Captain. Pick one.”

“Tether.”

Reg’s hands moved across Operations.

“Lock stabilization to Jupiter. Disable the cloak’s foreign-phase correction.”

The cloak peeled away from the hull.

Enterprise turned.

Her sensors found us.

Rusk transmitted over the open channel before Johnson finished his hail.

“The vessel is Avalon. Captain Hawkes has temporal-navigation capabilities beyond yours. They have located a possible route home and are refusing to complete their assessment.”

I stared at the viewer.

“Son of a bitch.”

Reg went very still.

“He wanted witnesses.”

Johnson asked whether we were in distress. It was a better question than Rusk deserved.

I answered, identified myself, and told him we had sustained damage from the directed field. He asked whether we were associated with Asterion. I said no.

“Your ship is not in our registry,” he said.

“I cannot fully explain it without giving you information that could affect your history.”

“That explanation is insufficient.”

“I would find it insufficient too.”

He studied me, then ordered a three-way channel.

Rusk admitted the field was intended to expose us.

“You attacked a withdrawing vessel,” Johnson said.

“I needed him to reconsider.”

“You could have asked.”

“He had decided.”

“Yes,” Johnson said. “That happens.”

Vale isolated the projector’s control coupler. Enterprise began a medical transfer when Asterion’s habitation power failed. Johnson moved from accusations to docking clearances in the time it took his officers to turn their chairs.

He could not compete with Rusk’s temporal machinery.

He could command a ship.

We supplied our witnessed attack record and identified the restrictions in Rusk’s mission orders. Observation and sample acquisition had been authorized. Historical suppression had been expressly prohibited.

Johnson asked Rusk to relinquish operational control and accompany his officers for an interview.

“No,” Rusk said.

Then he energized a second array.

A silver arc formed across our return vector.

“Transit exclusion envelope,” he said. “Do not initiate a temporal maneuver.”

Sorev read the gradient.

“He can make departure unsafe.”

“Can we fly beyond it?”

“At impulse. Unless it follows us.”

The arc widened.

It followed.

Rusk opened the private channel.

“I am giving your engineers time to finish.”

“You are holding us here.”

“Yes,” he said at last. “I am.”

Behind him, Vale warned that the envelope was consuming their transit reserves.

“I know,” Rusk answered.

He was spending his way home to prevent us using ours.

For the first time, I understood how far his fear might take him.

## Chapter Eight: Four Minutes

Brakk needed four minutes.

The disruption field had left a phase pattern in the damaged cloak assemblies. Entering transit with it still coupled to our hull could distort the return corridor.

He could retract the modules into onboard containment and purge the trapped chronitons. He could also eject them, which would be faster and leave twenty-fourth-century hardware in 2329.

I refused to leave the equipment behind.

“Four minutes,” he said. “Get me four minutes.”

On the viewer, Enterprise’s medical shuttle approached Asterion. Johnson had stopped charging weapons when Vale explained what phaser fire might do inside the variable gradient.

Rusk held the immediate field geometry. None of us could pretend otherwise.

But he still wanted something.

“Your exclusion field is corrupting the destination comparison,” I told him.

Vale checked her console.

“Sidebands overlap their observation frequencies,” she said.

“Then compensate,” Rusk told Reg.

Reg put two unstable traces on the shared display.

“Your field makes one candidate appear to move between histories. If we assume its distortion and compensate incorrectly, the result could look excellent and point to the wrong station.”

“You have the processing capacity.”

“Processing capacity cannot tell me which assumption you haven’t measured.”

Rusk looked toward Vale.

She did not defend him.

“He’s right, sir.”

“Lower the envelope,” I said. “During repairs, we continue the bounded evidence assessment. No transit promise. No access to our tether.”

“If I lower it, you can leave.”

“Yes.”

I let the word sit there.

Eventually he ordered Vale to ramp it down.

The silver arc faded.

Our return line remained.

At the same moment, two local Starfleet vessels arrived: Resolute, an Excelsior under Captain Anika Voss, and the smaller Miranda-class Kestrel with medical and damage-control teams.

Voss asked for close inspection. Johnson held her at range and maintained our departure volume.

Another captain knew our name. Another sensor array could study our hull.

The clock kept running.

“Ashley,” I said through the authenticated relay, “we need one independent observation of the Meridian candidate.”

She listened to the limits.

“One window. Evidence only. My capture stays isolated from your receiving lock. If it doesn’t resolve before clearance, you bring the question home.”

“Agreed.”

The station’s independent core acquired the candidate.

Meridian appeared. A local transmission referred to the Asterion recovery watch. The replacement reference array matched the maintenance history.

Rusk moved closer to his camera.

“They’re still looking.”

Then Asterion turned toward us.

Vale looked down.

“We are detecting a structured chronometric return near Avalon.”

Reg checked the observation timer.

“They can measure the window, Captain. Recommend closure.”

“Close it.”

Meridian faded.

Ashley confirmed the observation aperture was sealed. The home relay remained authenticated.

We sent Vale the limited findings: configuration matches, independent traffic, discrepancies, incomplete lineage. No route settings. No core schematics.

She began reviewing the evidence outside the integrator.

Rusk said nothing.

Brakk reported the hull-clearance test was beginning.

We were almost ready.

In the maintenance bay beneath the port hull, Brakk was discovering how misleading that phrase could be.

The second emitter assembly had retracted halfway and stopped. Its mounting collar had distorted under the disruption pulse. A junior engineer, Ensign Davi, lay on a maintenance sled beneath it, one arm inside the inspection hatch.

“It’s catching on the starboard guide,” she said.

“I can see that.”

“Then why did you ask?”

“I wanted to hear whether you could.”

She looked out from beneath the housing.

Brakk handed her a different tool.

“That one. Quarter turn. Nothing more.”

The emitter carried enough trapped chroniton activity to make the light around its edges shimmer. The containment cradle was clear on one side, doubled on the other. Davi kept her attention on the physical guide rather than its reflections.

“Chief, if the collar fractures, we lose the assembly.”

“Then don't fracture the collar.”

“I was hoping for a more technical answer.”

“That is the technical answer. The philosophical answer contains swearing.”

She made the quarter turn.

The guide released. The housing dropped a centimeter and the sled jolted beneath her.

“Out,” Brakk said.

“I haven't seated the secondary latch.”

“Out.”

He caught the sled's handle and pulled her clear as the doubled light surged along the containment edge. Something snapped inside the guide. The assembly descended another centimeter and stopped against its mechanical catch.

Davi sat up, breathing hard.

Brakk crouched beside her.

“Hands.”

She showed him both.

“Vision?”

“One of you. Regrettably.”

He nodded and called Medical for an exposure check. Then he looked at the damaged latch.

The quick repair required a person beneath a component that was intermittently occupying more than one phase. The slower repair required shutting off the local compensation and letting the containment cradle carry the load by itself.

He opened the bridge channel.

“Captain, second assembly has a mechanical obstruction. I can fix it safely, but I need another ninety seconds.”

I looked at the growing list of local sensor contacts.

Ninety more seconds of being seen. Ninety seconds of Rusk watching our engines and wondering whether to change his mind.

“Alternative?”

“Put someone under it while the field's live.”

“Take the ninety seconds.”

“Aye.”

Sorev adjusted the departure estimate. Ellie updated Jupiter. Neither argued that the contamination clock made the engineer expendable.

Brakk returned to the housing. Davi stood beside him, waiting to be told to go away.

“You can operate the overhead arm,” he said. “From outside the barrier.”

“I can finish the latch.”

“You can operate the arm.”

She took the controls.

Brakk killed the compensation circuit. The housing's doubled edge became one hard piece of machinery with an unpleasant internal glow. A load indicator climbed across the containment cradle.

## Chapter Twelve: The Blackout

## Chapter Fourteen: The Way Back

## Chapter Fifteen: What the Ship Brought Home

## Epilogue: The Next Roll

I looked around the table at my crew.

“All right,” I said. “What do you do?”
