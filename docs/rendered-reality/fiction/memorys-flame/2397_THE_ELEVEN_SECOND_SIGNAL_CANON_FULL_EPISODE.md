# PART ONE: THE ARRIVAL

## Chapter One: Lights in the Window

The first thing I remember about our return to Jupiter was a little girl waving at a starship.

That isn't the first thing I observed. I can tell you the exact order in which the ship's instruments received our approach authorization, the dimensions of the docking corridor, the moment our impulse engines surrendered control to maneuvering thrusters and the precise timestamp of the chronometric comparison.

But none of those details is how I remember coming home.

I remember a child pressed against an observation window, her palm lifted to the glass, so small against the immense architecture of Jupiter Station that the bridge camera almost missed her.

I remember Captain Hawkes waving back.

And I remember how surprised I was that this mattered to me.

Jupiter occupied nearly the entire forward viewscreen. Its bands of cloud rolled beneath the Avalon in great cream and rust-colored rivers, a hundred storms living their long lives within the turbulence of a planet that had been turning for billions of years. Lightning illuminated the lower clouds, sometimes so far beneath the visible layer that it looked as though another sky were trying to shine through.

The Great Red Spot curved into sunlight along the edge of the planet.

And above it, Jupiter Station appeared.

The upper habitation ring emerged first, silver against the planet's ocher limb. Then the central spine, the enormous curved braces supporting the docking complexes, the tiers of gardens and observation galleries. Miles of windows resolved into neat bands of gold and white. Maintenance launches moved between the outer arms. A passenger shuttle rose along the station's side and became a speck against Jupiter.

The station was not simply a shipyard. It was a home. It had schools, restaurants, workshops, nurses trying to finish shifts, engineers who forgot to eat, people arguing about the price of dinner and children who would never think it remarkable to see Jupiter outside their bedroom windows.

Captain Noah Hawkes stood beside the command rail.

“Well, Number One,” he said. “There she is.”

“Indeed, Captain.”

“That sounded almost emotional, Reg.”

“Almost?”

“I was going to say sentimental, but I didn't want to embarrass you.”

From his starboard console, Ensign Merlin said, “Commander Barclay has been watching the approach for eleven minutes while pretending not to be excited.”

I turned.

“Your observation is technically accurate and entirely unnecessary.”

The Captain laughed. Merlin grinned at his screen. At Science, Lieutenant Sorev continued working without looking up, which was Sorev's manner of informing us that our conduct was beneath comment. Across the bridge, Lieutenant junior grade Ellie Hawkes, her strawberry-blond hair caught by the monitor light, glanced over with a smile that made it clear she was recording the exchange for personal amusement, not for evidentiary purposes.

Merlin had his own duty. His console preserved an account of what the ship observed, what officers actually ordered, and which statements remained claims. He had also developed the habit of recording laughter in the margins of otherwise miserable incidents.

I approved of that habit more than I generally admitted.

The communications officer announced an incoming hail. Captain Ashley Hawkes appeared on the viewscreen, standing at the center of Jupiter Station Operations.

“USS Avalon,” she said, “you're approaching a little faster than expected.”

Noah smiled. “We were under the impression somebody wanted us home.”

“Somebody does. That somebody would also appreciate you not removing one of my docking arms.”

“Good evening, Captain Hawkes.”

“Good evening, Captain Hawkes.”

I could see officers moving behind her. Commander Tessa zh'Renn stood over a station traffic plot. Farther back, Imani Saye was reviewing an arrival register. Nobody had stopped working simply because the station captain was speaking to her husband.

That was important.

Noah commanded Avalon. Ashley commanded Jupiter Station. Their marriage connected them; it did not merge their authority.

“Reg,” Ashley said. “Keeping him out of trouble?”

“Doing everything reasonably possible, Captain.”

“Good. Bring my husband home.”

“Aye, Captain.”

The transmission closed.

“Helm,” Noah said, “take us in.”

We passed beneath the habitation ring. The ship's external cameras picked out individual gardens, restaurants, running tracks, and living rooms. A group of children stood against an observation window and began waving as Avalon crossed their view.

Noah raised one hand.

“Give them a little something, Helm.”

Our running lights brightened in sequence from the saucer to the nacelles. The children jumped and waved harder.

Merlin entered the observation in his log.

“There is no tactical value in that, Ensign,” Sorev remarked.

“No, Lieutenant.”

“Then why preserve it?”

“Because the Captain will ask about it later.”

Sorev considered that.

“A defensible operational prediction.”

The Captain's laugh was still on the bridge when our timeline reference began its final comparison.

Two indicators represented independent chronometric readings: one maintained aboard Avalon, the other supplied through Jupiter Station's separately governed reference network. The third displayed their relationship. For a fraction of a second the two readings drifted apart.

Then they converged.

**TEMPORAL REFERENCE: MATCHED.**  
**BRANCH AUTHENTICATION: CONFIRMED.**  
**JUPITER STATION TETHER: STABLE.**

“Home,” I said.

Noah exhaled. “Good.”

I registered that word too.

Then a warning appeared on my console.

An unidentified signal had entered the temporal authentication channel.

I looked at it once, then twice.

“Captain.”

He heard the change in my voice.

“Report.”

“Someone is transmitting on our private reference frequency.”

“Station?”

“Negative.”

“Source?”

“Sorev?”

The Vulcan leaned toward his display. “Apparent direction is below the station, toward Jupiter. No stable source location.”

The message included an Avalon identification code.

Then a timestamp.

Tomorrow.

A second later, the bridge speakers emitted my voice.

Not an approximation. My voice, with the pauses and inflections the crew knew.

“USS Avalon, this is Commander Barclay. Do not complete your docking approach.”

Noah folded his arms. “Why not?”

“Because Jupiter Station is no longer where you think it is.”

The lights flickered.

Sorev's standard subspace sensors stopped reporting the station's existence, while the optical viewscreen still showed it ahead of us, brilliant and enormous against the clouds.

“Commander,” Sorev said, “optical and gravitational returns continue to confirm the station. The subspace interrogations are arriving in an impossible sequence.”

“Explain.”

“An answer is being received before the corresponding question is sent.”

My chronometric display split.

Two readings, once convergent, now drifted apart. Between them appeared a third trace. It was faint, unstable and entirely unauthorized.

“Helm, all stop,” Noah ordered.

I turned to Tactical. “Defensive shields. Weapons powered but not locked. Don't select a target from contradictory instruments.”

“Captain Ashley is calling,” Communications said.

Noah stepped forward. “Put her through.”

Her voice arrived through static.

“Noah?”

“Ashley, something's wrong with the approach.”

“I know.”

“How?”

“Because I'm standing in Operations looking at the Avalon.”

“We're five kilometers from your berth.”

“No,” she said. “You're not.”

A terrible silence settled over the bridge.

“Your ship docked here twenty minutes ago.”

## Chapter Two: The Other Ship

The docking camera showed her.

The USS Avalon, NCC-75154, secured to berth seventeen.

Our silhouette. Our lights. Our registry.

She even carried the small hull markings Brakk insisted were evidence of the craftsmanship of his engineering team rather than evidence of his refusal to keep the hull clean.

But there was a black fracture along the starboard nacelle. It was a wound I had never seen on our ship, running almost to the pylon.

“Another Avalon,” Noah said.

“Apparently, Captain.”

“Could it be a hologram?”

“Not likely,” Sorev answered. “Station gravimeters register a second vessel's mass.”

“Then we have two of them.”

“Two observed vessels,” I corrected. “That does not yet establish how the second arrived.”

Noah looked at me.

“Number One, I'm beginning to dislike today's distinction between what we see and what we know.”

“I understand, sir.”

He rose from his chair.

“Let's see what's out there. Issue orders to the crew.”

I turned toward the bridge.

“Helm, withdraw five kilometers along our original approach vector. Thrusters only, manual control. Sorev, establish independent optical, thermal, gravimetric and subspace observations. Preserve the disagreements. Do not allow the computer to combine them into a single tidy answer.”

“Acknowledged.”

“Brakk, isolate the temporal core from incoming synchronization requests. Retain our last independently verified reference, but suspend automatic update.”

Brakk's voice came through Engineering. “That's the correction system that stops us wandering into the wrong century.”

“And I'd like to know whether something is using it to drag the wrong century toward us.”

There was a pause.

“Aye, Commander. Isolating.”

I turned to Merlin.

“Original signals. Original time stamps. Separate what the instruments measured from what the computer inferred. If an event claims to have happened tomorrow, record the claim as arriving today.”

“Understood, Commander.”

“And Ellie?”

She looked up from her auxiliary science station.

“I'm here.”

“Watch the AI-continuity interfaces. An unauthorized source may be trying to replace a trusted command identity. Do not let it persuade our software that a valid-looking signature is a valid order.”

Ellie glanced at the Captain.

“Understood. Dad, this is the first homecoming I've ever seen that required an incident-response team.”

“Welcome to the family business, Lieutenant.”

“It's an absurd business.”

“Also true.”

The arrival of an emergency transmission prevented further comment.

Merlin's attestation screen illuminated.

A record from tomorrow had appeared within the local archive, apparently witnessed under his own credentials. It reported evacuation of Jupiter Station's civilian sectors four through nine. Two hundred and seventeen casualties. Station command status unconfirmed.

And a final instruction:

**DO NOT PERMIT THE TWO AVALONS TO OCCUPY THE SAME TEMPORAL REFERENCE.**

“Did you write it?” I asked.

“No, Commander.”

“Did you observe the event?”

“No.”

“Then it's a hostile or unexplained entry claim. Quarantine it.”

Merlin did so immediately.

Noah read the casualty number.

His expression had changed. The children at the window were no longer an abstraction to him. They were somewhere inside the structure a message claimed would be destroyed.

“Reg, we are not going to treat that as an inevitable future.”

“No, sir.”

“Good.”

Sorev raised a hand.

“Captain. We can establish an independent observation.”

“Go on.”

“Two expendable probes, outside the suspected disturbance. Passive sensors and conventional optical reporting, with no temporal-network authentication. They will determine whether both vessels are physically observable from independent positions.”

Noah considered the proposal.

“Launch them.”

I supervised their trajectories. One flew above the station's plane; the other went below. Both recorded Jupiter Station and both Avalons, though their measurements disagreed about the timing of certain events.

Then Probe One acquired a third contact.

The object was dark, elongated, jointed. It passed beneath Jupiter Station's lower engineering complex with no detectable propulsion exhaust.

As the probe turned toward it, the object stopped.

A line of blue light appeared on its surface.

It rotated toward the camera.

“It's looking at the probe,” Merlin said.

“Careful,” Sorev replied. “It has changed orientation toward the instrument.”

I could have kissed him for the qualification. At another time.

The flash destroyed Probe One.

Probe Two remained operational long enough to show a black circular opening in space. Through it appeared stars that belonged to no chart in our local sky.

The opening closed after three seconds.

The Captain studied the recording.

“What was on the other side?”

Sorev replied, “Insufficient information.”

Then the damaged Avalon hailed us.

The face that appeared was Noah Hawkes.

Not our Captain.

Another man, injured and exhausted, whose features matched ours so precisely that even the Captain's unconscious expressions seemed to have been duplicated.

His voice was weak.

“Don't trust the station's time reference.”

“Identify yourself,” our Noah demanded.

The other man looked directly at me.

“Reg. Is that you?”

I stepped toward the screen.

“Who are you?”

“Someone who got home first,” he said.

Then the signal dissolved into static.

## Chapter Three: The Doorway

Brakk's survey shuttle looked like a flying toolbox because it was, in practical terms, a flying toolbox.

He named it Reginald.

I considered submitting a formal objection.

Captain Hawkes informed me that I lacked standing.

The shuttle was unmanned, heavily shielded and fitted with an isolated recorder. It carried an independent optical relay, extra maneuvering thrusters and enough propulsion reserve to evacuate a moderately sized asteroid if the asteroid had been inclined to leave.

The plan was to approach the disturbance without entering it.

The Captain reviewed the flight path.

“No crew aboard.”

“Correct.”

“Nothing that can authenticate an incoming historical record?”

“Correct.”

“Then send it.”

The shuttle drifted toward the coordinates where the opening had appeared. For several minutes it reported nothing but vacuum, particulate matter and Jupiter's overwhelming electromagnetic noise.

Then the background stars began to move.

Not together.

Individually.

A circular darkness widened beneath the station. Beyond it, lines of blue radiance revealed an immense system of rings and suspended platforms. Some were whole; others were broken. At the center stood a luminous chamber with a deep, slow pulse.

Nothing in the image established its scale. Nothing established whether it was a machine, a city, or the architecture of an environment we did not understand.

“It appears constructed,” Sorev said. “That is an observation about geometry, not an identification of its builders.”

“Can we measure distance?” Noah asked.

“Not reliably.”

The structure began to rotate.

An entire inner ring turned toward our probe.

Merlin received a transmission.

It contained an image of Jupiter Station with the upper habitation ring shattered and debris spilling toward the planet. Fires glowed inside the promenade windows.

Noah stepped forward.

“Is that live?”

“No verified source, Captain,” Merlin answered. “No authenticated time. No evidence it is a prediction.”

Another image appeared, this time of our bridge. Captain Hawkes was sitting in his chair, though the real Captain stood at the command rail.

I seized on the discrepancy.

“It's presenting a model, not a perfect observation.”

“Can we prove it?” Noah asked.

“We can test whether it responds to what we do rather than what it expects.”

I ordered three random course changes for the drone, each selected only at the moment of execution.

The remote structure altered its displayed images after every maneuver. The sequence appeared reactive. When the Captain took a deliberate step away from the bridge rail, the other Captain in the transmitted bridge image did not move.

“Then it isn't tomorrow,” Noah said.

“Not demonstrated to be tomorrow,” Sorev replied.

The Captain sighed.

“I'm going to have that engraved on your console, Lieutenant.”

Before anyone could answer, the immense chamber beyond the opening went dark.

Long black appendages emerged and closed around the shuttle.

“Reginald withdrawing,” Helm reported.

The drone's engines flared.

Its outer hull began to buckle.

“Brakk, overload the propulsion system,” I ordered. “We will not allow it to take an active recorder into that structure.”

The shuttle exploded between the mechanical fingers.

The object recoiled.

Then six appendages emerged from the doorway and began moving toward Jupiter Station.

“Battle stations!” Noah shouted.

Avalon's phasers struck the nearest arm. It recoiled, then resumed its course, apparently undamaged.

The opening widened.

Jupiter Station's emergency lights spread across its lower rings.

Ashley appeared on the main viewscreen.

“Noah, my docking sector is taking structural stress. I've sealed berth seventeen and evacuated the lower observation galleries. Several civilians are trapped behind a pressure door, and the damaged Avalon won't answer a security challenge.”

“We're engaging the objects.”

“I can see that. Keep them away from my station. I'll get my people out.”

Noah nodded.

“Aye, Captain.”

I watched her face for a moment after she disconnected. I knew that she was frighteningly competent. I also knew precisely what waited behind those station windows: a life built deliberately, a family, a home that had no intention of becoming a historical casualty just because the universe had developed a taste for paradox.

And then Merlin called from the attestation console.

“Commander, another entry has appeared.”

“Tomorrow again?”

“No, sir.”

He looked toward me.

“Two years ago.”

The date was 2395.

The year of Avalon's first full temporal-core activation.

The year my biological body had died.

The entry was marked with my original integration signature.

**INTEGRATION FAILURE. TEMPORAL REFERENCE LOST. SECONDARY WITNESS DETECTED.**

I stared at it.

“Merlin, preserve the source bytes.”

“Already done.”

“Do not reconcile them with my existing archive.”

“Understood.”

From the damaged Avalon came another transmission. This time the image of Noah Hawkes appeared on a failing bridge, his uniform scorched, blood across his forehead.

“They've been looking for the one thing they couldn't reproduce,” he said.

“What?” our Captain demanded.

“A witness who survived the loss of his original body.”

The message cut off.

Noah turned toward me.

I looked back at him.

“Captain, we don't know whether he actually sent that.”

He nodded.

“Then let's talk to him without letting the temporal core do the talking.”

## Chapter Four: The Other Captain

There was something almost comforting about using light.

No chronometric handshake. No identity transfer. No external system allowed to negotiate what it was permitted to know. A simple optical relay through our surviving probe.

We sent a challenge to the damaged Avalon using its running lights.

The vessel answered.

**WE HEAR YOU.**

We requested a live link.

**THEY ARE LISTENING.**

Our Captain ordered a physical challenge: extinguish the port running lights for five seconds while leaving the starboard lights illuminated.

The damaged ship complied.

It did not prove who was aboard, but it established that someone or something could control its physical lights in response to a fresh instruction.

A video connection followed.

The other Noah Hawkes looked more human than the earlier recording.

Which was to say, considerably worse.

He had a bleeding cut along his forehead. His left arm was wrapped in a makeshift sling. A smoke alarm sounded behind him. He stared at the Captain's face and seemed both relieved and disgusted.

“Jesus Christ,” he whispered.

Noah stepped closer.

“Who are you?”

“Captain Noah Hawkes. USS Avalon.”

“That's my ship.”

“Yeah.” The other man looked around his ruined bridge. “Mine too.”

He described arriving at Jupiter Station and receiving clearance in Ashley's voice. They had docked. They had secured their ship. Then his Barclay had warned him that the reference did not match, and their computer had begun producing commands nobody issued.

We asked whether he had sent the earlier message about a witness who survived death.

“No,” he said.

We asked whether he had told us not to dock.

“No.”

I watched Merlin separate those denials from the earlier transmissions.

The other Captain continued.

“Our computer started telling us things. Things from our childhoods. Things nobody had said aloud. Then it started giving us tomorrow's orders.”

“Did any of them come true?” I asked.

“Some of the things we did afterward looked like the orders it had predicted. I don't know what that proves.”

An excellent answer from a badly injured man.

A violent impact shook his bridge.

Someone shouted that a forward pressure door had failed.

The other Noah turned away, issuing evacuation orders.

“How many aboard?” our Captain demanded.

“Thirty-eight survivors when we docked. We've already lost people. I can't get the escape pods to launch.”

Brakk's voice came from Engineering.

“Commander, the pods have manual releases. If their crew can reach the local fittings, they don't need to trust the computer.”

I told the other Captain.

He nodded and sent his people aft.

Then Jupiter Station called.

Ashley reported that forty-two personnel had occupied the lower docking area at the onset of the emergency. Thirty-four had escaped; eight remained trapped behind an emergency pressure door. The adjoining habitation ring was taking the strain of the entire abandoned docking assembly.

“Your berth is tearing itself away from my station,” she said.

Noah looked toward the damaged Avalon.

“We're trying to get the crew out.”

“I know. I'm evacuating mine.”

“Can you give us time?”

“Until the last eight reach the inner corridor. Then I release berth seventeen.”

The Captain was silent.

She met his eyes.

“I will not lose a civilian ring to preserve an unidentified vessel. Even one carrying your face.”

Noah nodded.

“Understood, Captain.”

We had minutes.

We did not have a solution.

The first pod launched from the damaged Avalon.

Then another.

Within forty seconds six pods were moving through space, carrying twenty-four people. We brought them toward an evacuation corridor protected by Avalon's shields and tractors.

One of the black appendages turned toward the pods. We intercepted it, losing much of our forward shield capacity in the collision.

Pod Four lost maneuvering power and began falling toward Jupiter's atmosphere.

I ordered the port tractors energized.

The field caught the pod just before its trajectory passed beyond recovery.

Four life signs.

Still alive.

Eight more people escaped aboard two additional pods as the other Avalon fought to break free. At the last moment, its battered engines ignited and pushed against the thing holding it.

But not all its people had reached the escape bank.

Ashley announced that her final eight trapped station personnel had been recovered. She ordered the docking spine released.

Explosive charges flashed along berth seventeen.

The massive assembly came free, saving the habitation ring. Pieces of it drifted between the two starships and Jupiter.

The damaged Avalon was pulled toward the doorway.

A final transmission arrived from its captain.

“Reg,” he said, “if you see our Barclay, tell him I never blamed him.”

“For what?” I asked.

The answer never came.

The doorway contracted.

The damaged vessel remained trapped at its edge, damaged beyond any realistic self-rescue.

Noah looked from the disappearing aperture to the life-sign tally.

Five.

Five people still aboard, including a man whose face was his own.

“Rescue them,” he said.

“All five. I don't care which version of the universe they came from.”

“Your orders are clear, Captain,” I answered.

And for the second time in my unusual career, I prepared to go aboard a ship that should not have existed.


# PART TWO: THE RESCUE

## Chapter Five: A Stranger with My Face

Brakk greeted me in Auxiliary Bay Two with an expression I had learned to dread.

He was holding a bracelet.

“No,” I said.

“I haven't told you what it does.”

“Because I know what it does, Chief. It looks insufficiently engineered.”

“It is precisely engineered. That's what makes it small.”

We stood beside the armored boarding craft that would take us to the damaged Avalon. Lieutenant Solis and two other security personnel were securing rescue gear. Chief Mbeki from Medical had arranged portable oxygen, trauma packs and emergency restraints in case we encountered injured crew members who could not walk.

Brakk pointed to a reinforced case inside the shuttle.

“Your holographic projection will use the bracelet. Processing stays aboard our ship. The optical carrier supplies a narrowly limited connection. No network negotiation. No remote identity exchange. One Reginald Barclay.”

“And if the carrier fails?”

“You disappear from their point of view until we restore it. Your active process remains aboard Avalon.”

“If the signal attacks the emitter?”

“It has a physical cutoff. I can break the connection without letting an external system become a command source.”

I watched him for a moment.

“Thank you, Chief.”

He looked uncomfortable.

“Don't thank me. Bring the equipment back.”

I fastened the bracelet.

My projection flickered, and the familiar sensation of being distributed throughout Avalon's sensors retreated. Thousands of ordinary pathways narrowed to a thin, deliberate connection.

For a second I felt small.

Then I remembered something human: being a man inside one body had always felt like that.

Solis approached.

“Commander, the boarding craft is ready.”

“Then let's go.”

Noah's voice came from the bay intercom.

“Reg.”

“Yes, Captain?”

“All five. And yourself.”

“Aye, sir.”

“That's an order.”

“I'd gathered as much.”

The outer hatch closed.

The craft released its clamps and crossed the short distance to the crippled starship.

Up close, the other Avalon was worse than her camera image suggested. Her nacelle housing had been crushed by an immense force. The hull had been cut along a line so straight that it looked less like damage and more like something had attempted to open the ship with a blade. Her illuminated windows revealed emergency fog, overturned furniture, moving shadows.

Our craft attached to a port maintenance hatch.

Solis checked atmosphere.

“Thin. Gravity intermittent. No confirmed intruder near the entry point.”

“Helmets sealed,” I said. “We recover the survivors and leave. No direct connection to their computers.”

The cutter burned a circle through the exterior plate.

The maintenance hatch opened.

The damaged ship smelled of hot metal and leaked coolant. I did not need biological lungs to appreciate how dangerous its atmosphere had become.

We advanced through a corridor whose red alert lights flashed in irregular intervals. A broken family photograph lay beneath a shattered display frame. I saw two children smiling beside a dog, their faces crossed by a fracture in the glass.

I wanted to stop.

I did not.

From behind a pressure door came a voice.

“Starfleet! We're in here!”

Solis raised her rifle.

“Identify yourself.”

“Lieutenant Mara Venn, Environmental Systems! We have wounded!”

The door's mechanical release was bent. I used a cutter to sever the damaged fitting while Solis held the door.

Inside were three people: Venn, an officer with a broken leg and another unconscious after oxygen deprivation.

Mbeki went to work immediately.

Venn looked at me and recoiled.

“You.”

“Commander Barclay. Another Avalon. We're here to get you out.”

“That's impossible.”

“I appreciate the sentiment.”

She studied my face, then the emitter on my wrist.

“You're a projection.”

“Yes.”

“Our REG said he was going to save us.”

“Where is he?”

Her voice broke.

“He's in the computer core corridor. The Captain went to find him.”

“Can you walk?”

“Yes.”

“Then help Mbeki.”

As we prepared the wounded, I asked what had happened after they docked.

“The computer talked to us,” Venn whispered. “Not just alarms. It knew my brother's name. It knew things I never put in any record. Then it started telling us who would die.”

“Did you believe it?”

“At first I thought it was some kind of cruel diagnostic failure. Later, when the pressure doors sealed, some of the warnings looked right.”

She swallowed.

“Dr. Hale was helping the wounded. A door broke in the medical access corridor. We couldn't get him out.”

I looked at her.

“Was he killed?”

She nodded.

“One confirmed fatality, Commander. He was our medical officer.”

“I'm sorry.”

“Please don't tell the Captain any more of us are going to die.”

I met her eyes.

“I won't tell him anything the evidence doesn't justify.”

We moved the three survivors into the boarding craft. Mbeki prepared to return them to our Avalon.

“Commander,” Solis said, “do you intend to continue?”

“Yes. Two people are still forward.”

The security officer's jaw tightened.

“Then I'm coming with you.”

“Understood.”

The shuttle departed with the three rescued personnel. Solis and I crossed into the forward engineering section, where the ship's corridor geometry had begun to change.

That was not a metaphor.

A straight passage bent around a corner that had never existed in Sovereign-class plans. A bulkhead flexed as though it were made of rubber. An open hatch led into a corridor longer than the vessel's exterior dimensions.

“Commander,” Solis said, “I don't like this.”

“An entirely reasonable response.”

We heard phaser fire ahead.

Then a man shouted, “Get back!”

We rounded the turn and found Captain Noah Hawkes.

The other one.

He leaned against a shattered access panel, blood running along his neck. He raised his phaser when he saw us.

“Which Reg?”

“First officer of the Avalon currently outside your ship. Isolated optical projection. Limited operational access.”

His weapon lowered.

“Thank God.”

“We have recovered three survivors. That brings the total evacuated to thirty-five.”

“Good.”

“Your medical officer?”

His face tightened.

“Hale didn't make it.”

“One confirmed fatality,” I said.

He nodded.

“There were thirty-eight alive when we docked. There are thirty-seven now. Don't let them lose count.”

He pointed toward the illuminated access corridor.

“He's inside.”

“Your Barclay?”

“Yes.”

I stepped into the chamber and met myself.

He looked like the man I remembered being.

Human. Tired. Left hand trembling. He wore a uniform from 2395 and stood before a computer panel whose interfaces flashed blue without producing ordinary status readings.

His eyes met mine.

“Reginald.”

“Identify yourself.”

He almost smiled.

“A sensible first question.”

“Who are you?”

“I don't know how to answer that without lying to one of us.”

His candor surprised me.

He looked at my wrist emitter.

“Single projection, remote processor, isolated optical carrier?”

“Yes.”

“I insisted on those safeguards too.”

“Then you know we should leave.”

The other man placed a sealed cylinder in my hand.

“A record from my integration. Not certified. Not yet.”

“Why give it to me?”

“Because I can't trust what I remember anymore. I need somebody who will ask how the record was made.”

A terrible sound came from the corridor.

Metal tore. Air hissed. A black circle opened at the far end, revealing the blue-lit ring structure beyond the doorway.

Solis grabbed the injured Captain's arm.

“Move!”

We ran.

The corridor stretched unnaturally ahead. The other Barclay stumbled, recovered and followed. I used my holographic field to hold up a collapsing structural beam while Solis pulled Noah clear. The strain drove the emitter toward its limits.

“Commander!” Brakk's voice crackled. “Your field integrity is falling!”

“Then I'd better not waste it.”

Solis shoved the Captain through the maintenance hatch. The biological Barclay followed.

When his hand touched the shuttle rail, a medical sensor announced that his body matched archived human records associated with Reginald Barclay.

That was a medical result.

It was not an explanation.

I boarded last, just as the beam behind us collapsed.

The shuttle broke free.

The vessel was gone.

Across all rescue craft, thirty-seven people had survived.

“Repair what?”

“I don't know.”

## Chapter Six: Eleven Seconds

Our recovery bay was full of people who needed help and officers who were desperately trying to provide it.

Mbeki directed the wounded toward Sickbay. Security established a quarantine perimeter. A medical officer brought our other Captain Hawkes onto a transport stretcher. He protested until the pain in his shoulder became impossible to ignore.

Then Captain Noah Hawkes arrived.

He looked at the man wearing his face.

The other Noah looked back.

“Well,” the injured man said.

“Well,” ours answered.

“You look better than I feel.”

“That wouldn't be difficult.”

For a moment they almost laughed.

Then our Captain's gaze moved to the biological Barclay.

Two men with my face occupied the same room. One with a heartbeat. One with a holographic emitter.

Neither of us spoke.

Noah looked toward me.

“Medical quarantine. Both of them. Nobody gets access to the computer core.”

“Aye, Captain.”

The biological Barclay glanced toward the ceiling.

“That might already be too late.”

Every console in the bay illuminated.

The same sentence appeared on every screen:

**THANK YOU FOR BRINGING HIM HOME.**

Then my projection lost contact with the room.

I did not experience the transition as a loss of consciousness. A new environment replaced the old one so smoothly that, for a moment, I believed I had simply turned around.

I stood on Avalon's bridge.

Not the bridge of 2397.

The bridge from 2395.

A Cytherian neural-interface chair occupied the center of the room. Its awkward arms and luminous tubing had been reproduced with terrible accuracy.

A younger Reginald Barclay stood beside it.

Human.

Frightened.

He looked at me.

“Did we save them?”

I felt a pulse at my wrist. I could smell burning insulation. There was a weight beneath my ribs that I had not felt since the day my body died.

I looked down.

My hands were flesh.

A pulse beat beneath my skin.

I knew how convincing a reconstruction could be. I had helped build some of the best holographic simulation systems in Starfleet. The sensation of skin was not proof of biological embodiment. Neither was the smell, or the heartbeat, or the terrifying conviction that I had returned to a moment I could no longer change.

“Where am I?” I asked.

The younger Barclay looked toward Jupiter.

“Don't you remember?”

There was no station outside the viewscreen.

Only the planet and darkness.

“I'm inside a reconstruction,” I said.

He smiled faintly.

“That's the first honest answer anyone has given me.”

The scene dissolved.

I was in Engineering, surrounded by the alarms of the original activation. The temporal core blazed behind transparent shielding.

A countdown appeared above it.

Eleven seconds.

Ten.

Nine.

Then the display stopped.

“Someone took those seconds,” the younger man said.

“Who?”

“The ones beyond the doorway.”

“Evidence?”

He pointed toward a console.

A record appeared, marked as the original integration archive. It requested my attestation that eleven reconstructed seconds represented my own witnessed experience.

I stared at the authorization field.

There it was.

Not an invitation to remember, but a demand to certify.

“You want me to sign this,” I said.

The man nodded.

“It will restore what they took.”

“How do you know that?”

“Because I was there.”

“But I don't know whether you're a record, a generated image or an actual continuation of someone. And neither do you, as far as I can establish.”

His expression hardened.

“Afraid?”

“Yes.”

“Then you should accept it.”

“No.”

The chamber trembled.

A hundred versions of my face appeared in the walls, all looking toward me. Some were old. Some were young. Some wore uniforms I had never seen. One was the biological Barclay from the other ship.

A voice spoke from everywhere.

“RETURN THE WITNESS.”

I stepped toward the console.

“Why?”

“COMPLETE THE RECORD.”

“You have a record. You want authority.”

“THE RECORD MUST BE TRUE.”

“Then show me evidence that it is.”

The figures turned their heads together.

“CHOOSE.”

“No.”

I don't know how long I stood there. I know only that the environment changed every time I refused. It showed me Jupiter Station destroyed. It showed my Captain giving orders he hadn't issued. It showed a child at the observation window growing old, then vanishing. It offered the one thing I wanted: certainty that I had continued after the loss of my biological body.

And it demanded only that I agree.

Then a door opened in the wall.

Captain Hawkes stepped through.

He wore a medical interface visor, and a thin line of light trailed back toward the holosuite where his physical body remained.

He looked around.

“Reg, I've seen better holodeck programs.”

“Captain! You shouldn't be here.”

“Apparently the ship needs a second opinion.”

“Brakk permitted this?”

“He objected. Sorev objected. Merlin wrote down six reasons it was a terrible idea.”

“And?”

“I read them.”

He studied the younger Barclay.

“Who is that?”

“A representation of my biological self. I haven't authenticated it.”

“Good.”

Noah looked toward the console.

“And what's this?”

“An attempt to make me certify a reconstructed interval as my personal experience.”

“Have you?”

“No, sir.”

“Excellent. Let's get out.”

The chamber shook violently.

The many-faced figure appeared. It seemed made of images stacked upon images: starships, rooms, faces, worlds, all flashing too quickly to distinguish.

“THE CAPTAIN CANNOT PROTECT WHAT HE CANNOT REMEMBER.”

Noah looked at it.

“Funny. I've spent my whole career protecting people whose futures I can't predict.”

The figure advanced.

Merlin's voice broke through the distortion.

“Commander! This is the attestation console. Do you read me?”

“Merlin!”

“An external process is attempting to write an authenticated entry into the original archive. It has your credentials.”

“Reject it!”

“Already rejected. There is no authorized originating action from you.”

The figure convulsed.

Merlin continued.

“I can preserve the attempt, Commander. I cannot certify the content.”

“Good man,” Noah said.

Behind the figure, a narrow passage opened toward the real medical holosuite.

“Captain, the exit is open! But the intrusion is attached to the commander's return pathway!”

Noah grabbed my arm.

“Reg, now!”

I felt the reconstructed environment trying to hold me in place. The young Barclay stood near the chair, looking frightened again.

“Did I die?” he asked.

The question had no trap in its tone. That was what made it difficult.

“Your biological body died,” I answered. “Avalon survived. Sol survived. A Reginald Barclay continued in her systems.”

He looked down.

“Is he happy?”

I thought of the Captain waving at children. Of Brakk complaining about my emitter. Of Ellie sharing a joke with Merlin. Of a home whose windows I could finally distinguish from the black of space.

“Often,” I said.

The younger man nodded.

“That's good.”

Then he was gone.

Noah pulled me through the passage.

The chamber dissolved.

Brakk was kneeling above an engineering console. The Captain's abandoned visor lay beside him. Merlin stood by the attestation interface with an expression I would remember for years.

“Number One?”

“Sir.”

“Don't ever make me go into a haunted computer to retrieve my first officer again.”

I took it.

Noah helped me stand.

“Your active process never stopped. The outside reconstruction is quarantined. Your old integration pathways are still isolated.”

“Contained,” Brakk said. “Don't confuse that with gone.”

“An excellent distinction, Chief.”

Merlin stepped forward.

“Commander, the hostile record tried to write itself eleven times.”

“Yes. Once for every second it claimed you had forgotten.”

I considered that.

One thing had become clear.

The entity did not merely want to describe me.

It wanted me to authorize its version of my history.

And it had failed.

For now.

A second object broke apart beneath Avalon's phasers.

A third accelerated toward Transport Seven.

Twenty-nine civilians aboard.

The transport could not outrun it.

“Sorry, Captain,” she whispered. “Lost the helm.”

Noah put a hand on her uninjured shoulder.

“You held this ship together while something tried to eat it. Take the rest of the watch off.”

“We have to break it.”

“Because the copied Avalon follows our maneuvers. We may be able to lure it into the abandoned docking structure.”

“And get out of my firing line.”

The other ship followed, firing as it came. Our aft shield strength fell into the teens. Broken station components passed above and below us as we entered the field.

At my mark, our helmswoman executed an abrupt vertical climb.

The copy followed the route we'd left behind.

Ashley's voice answered.

“Stand by.”

The window narrowed.

“Captain,” I warned, “it's changing course.”

A breath.

Another.

“Jupiter Station to Tug Six. Full emergency thrust.”

And inside the column, a shape I knew too well.

A chair.

Then the ship collapsed into a point of brilliant light and disappeared.

There are times when a crew deserves the mercy of hope.

And there are times when a captain must let them have it.

## Chapter Nine: Merlin's Five Questions

The bridge was unusually quiet when Merlin asked me to sit beside his console.

“Are they independent?”

## Chapter Eleven: A Probe Without a Name

On the bridge, the probe was called Ghost One.

***

The first images from the upper atmosphere were astonishing.

Jupiter was not a smooth ocean of painted bands. It was a living cathedral of motion. Towering clouds rose into sunlight. Dark chasms opened between storm systems. Miles-long filaments of ammonia crystals glittered as they moved through atmospheric currents. Lightning traveled sideways through the clouds, illuminating enormous masses of vapor and color.

The probe sank between two rising formations, its engines firing in short corrections against the wind.

Sorev read the pressure and temperature.

Merlin marked the arrival order of the images.

Ellie watched the incoming carrier for signs that the measurements had been replaced by instructions.

The Captain leaned forward.

“Now that's a sight.”

It was.

Even after everything that had happened, I found myself feeling the old excitement of exploration.

No alternate captains.

No false casualty lists.

No impossible declarations about the survival of consciousness.

Just a machine descending into the weather of a world we had studied for centuries and still barely understood.

Then a band of blue light moved across the clouds.

Sorev adjusted the camera.

“Not consistent with the recorded discharge pattern.”

The blue light appeared again.

Three flashes.

A pause.

Four more.

Merlin straightened.

“Same interval.”

Not a station.

Not a ship.

A ring suspended inside an atmosphere, supported by a field we had not yet identified.

Blue light traveled around its circumference.

A silver ship.

USS Avalon.

No larger than a toy in the image, surrounded by an enormous luminous structure.

A woman with her back to the camera.

She turned.

Captain Ashley Hawkes.

Or her likeness.

Noah rose.

The image raised one hand.

Then vanished.

**WITNESS REQUIRED. RETURN AUTHORITATIVE HISTORY.**

“Commander, the device is translating its own demand into our language.”

Ellie shook her head.

“More importantly, it's trying to make a device with no identity negotiate identity.”

Ghost One reported no authorized response.

There was no such function aboard it.

The phrase repeated.

Nothing happened.

Then, very slowly, the ring began changing shape.

Ghost One's thrusters fired.

The probe turned.

The object followed.

Not because Ghost One had escaped.

Because something else had happened.

Three pulses.

A pause.

Four more.

Black material.

Ceramic contacts.

Three grooves.

Four.

Then the camera went dark.

We lost the signal and could not establish whether the probe had been destroyed, captured or moved beyond communication range.

“What did we get?”

## Chapter Fourteen: The Ocean Without a Shore

Jupiter has no surface to forgive a bad landing.

To remember.

To find out whether there was something about those eleven seconds that would finally tell me what I had become.
