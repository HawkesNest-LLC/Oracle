## Prologue: Two Voices

*Later registry annotation, Ensign Merlin: The first separation time is bounded by the rescue telemetry. The crews remember the same childhoods. They do not remember the same rescue.*

Petra Lindqvist had been telling Theo to stop arguing with the guidance system when the light changed.

It entered through the forward glass without illuminating anything.

The panel under her hand remained dark. The pilot's shoulder remained dark. Beyond them, a white line bent across Jupiter and ran down the window like water looking for a crack.

"Manual," she said.

Theo already had his hand on the release.

The little maintenance tender lurched. A wrench lifted out of its open drawer, rotated once, and struck the ceiling.

Behind Petra, Ruth Calloway said, "Everybody hold something that belongs to the ship."

Jin Park held the notebook against his chest.

"Something attached to the ship," Ruth amended.

The guidance alarm stopped.

For one unreasonable second Petra thought that meant the trouble had passed.

Then Theo screamed.

Another voice on the open channel asked what had happened to the ship beside him. It was Theo's voice too, with the same high break in it, but it was asking a question while the boy beside her was still trying to get air.

A second tender hung beyond the glass.

Its forward running light was out. Petra had put off replacing their forward running light until after lunch.

"Control, Tender Four, emergency recovery requested."

Her own voice answered half a second later.

Same words. Same annoyance beneath the effort to sound calm.

Petra stared at the other cockpit.

She could see a woman staring back.

A tractor beam caught them hard enough to drive her knees against the console. The second tender moved with them, then drifted outward as another beam found its hull.

A voice filled the cockpit.

"Both tenders, shut down your reactors. Maintain emergency batteries. We are bringing you aboard separately."

Starfleet voice. Captain's voice. Used to being obeyed by people who had a great many reasons to do something else.

Petra cut the reactor.

Theo made a small sound.

"Chief."

"I see it."

"No. Chief. I can see me."

"I see him too."

The other tender's pilot had one hand against the window.

Petra forced herself to read their own power indicators. Emergency battery online. Life support sufficient. Tractor load rising. There were ordinary things to do. Thank God there were ordinary things to do.

"Jin, transmitter on battery."

"Done."

"Ruth, check Theo."

"I'm right here," Theo said.

"Then you'll be easy to find."

The aperture behind them brightened.

The tractor load climbed beyond anything the tender's little instruments had been designed to display. Petra smelled insulation and tried not to imagine the fittings tearing out of the hull.

A second voice on the channel said, "They're holding."

Her voice.

She did not answer it.

There would be time to be terrified of that later if this old beautiful starship managed to get them out.

Enterprise filled the forward glass. Her shuttle bay was open. People were moving behind the atmospheric field, yellow sleeves, blue sleeves, a loader backing out of the landing lane.

The first tender crossed the threshold.

Petra's tender crossed second.

The tractor released.

Her left hand found a hot coolant coupling while she was trying to stop the unsecured drawer from striking Theo's head.

Pain brought the world into focus.

She swore.

Somewhere to port, in a cockpit exactly like hers, someone did not.

The landing crew approached cautiously. Not with weapons. With wheel chocks, extinguishers, and a medical kit.

Petra remembered that afterward.

Before anybody knew what they were, someone had decided they might need a fire extinguisher.

Ruth opened the hatch and stepped down. Another Ruth stepped down from the tender beside them.

Neither spoke.

Theo climbed out, looked across the deck, and sat on the boarding step.

The other Theo remained standing.

Jin came last, notebook in hand. Across the gap, another young man held the same notebook.

Petra pressed her burned palm against her uniform and walked between the two little ships.

The other woman met her there.

Same old nick through the left eyebrow. Same impatience with unsecured drawers. Same expression Petra had seen in mirrors when she was trying not to cry in front of junior personnel.

The other Petra looked at the injured hand.

"Oh," she said.

"Coolant coupling."

"I nearly grabbed it."

"I know."

Beyond them, the shuttle-bay doors began to close.

A medical officer stopped at a respectful distance.

"Senior Chief? Both of you. May I?"

"In a minute," Petra said.

Both women said it.

The medical officer waited.

Petra laughed once, an ugly little sound she could not help.

The other woman put both hands on her hips. Thought for a moment. Looked back at the two tenders.

"Well," she said. "One of us is going to have to learn to cook differently."

Petra knew precisely why she had said it.

Give the others something to laugh at. Something small enough to survive the next minute.

She loved her for that and hated that there was someone else who knew how.

"Start with your coffee," she said.

"Our coffee."

"Not anymore."

The other Petra considered this.

Then she nodded.

The doctor approached again.

This time one of them said yes.

## One: The Shuttle Bay

*Receiving entry, recorded later from Enterprise-B's supplied rescue records: two craft recovered; eight people examined. Their informal designations, Port and Starboard, are self-selected. These are not ranks.*

Captain Thomas Johnson arrived carrying a logbook under one arm and somebody else's cup of coffee.

Petra noticed the coffee because it had a child's painted star on the side.

A captain who borrowed cups was easier to look at than a captain who might have to decide which four people could leave his shuttle bay.

He asked the medical officer whether anyone needed to be moved before he asked anybody for an explanation.

"Minor burns, bruising, acute stress," she said. "Some readings I would prefer to discuss privately."

"Then do that."

Johnson looked at the eight of them.

"I'm Captain Johnson. You're aboard Enterprise. Your craft are secure. The aperture is still being managed outside, so for the moment this is the safest room I can offer."

Theo, sitting on a cargo container, said, "Which one of us are you talking to?"

Johnson did not answer quickly.

"All eight."

Nobody said anything for a while.

The shuttle-bay crew had put folding screens around the two tenders and brought benches from somewhere. Someone had found eight blankets. The blankets were different colors, an accident of supply which suddenly seemed an act of extraordinary tact.

Petra took a gray one.

The other Petra took blue.

Ruth and Ruth went into the screened examination area together and emerged separately. One was frowning at a PADD. The other was frowning at her.

"Well?" Petra asked.

"We're going to need more than one chart."

"We have that much," Johnson said.

"Good. That's the beginning of medicine."

He accepted this without appearing to notice it was a test.

Their records were local records. Tender Four, Ganymede maintenance service, crew contracted through Starfleet facilities support. Four people with a work order to inspect a traffic-guidance repeater. They had detached during a communications emergency because the repeater fault had been classified as a navigational hazard.

Jin had objected to the departure.

Both Jins remembered objecting.

Neither took comfort from being right twice.

Their quarters were on Ganymede. Their lives were in 2329. Petra had a half-finished shelf at home, a younger brother on a transport somewhere near Mars, and a divorce finalized eighteen months earlier which she had no intention of discussing with a starship captain.

The other Petra had all of those things.

They had no wife waiting on a station in some future century. At that point none of them knew there was a future starship hiding outside.

They knew the emergency had put a stranger in each of their places and that the stranger remembered earning the place.

Johnson listened to the account twice.

When the second Petra reached the same sentence about the repeater, she stopped.

"You've heard this."

"From her," Johnson said.

"It's the same."

"Until it isn't."

She looked at the cup in his hand.

"Is that yours?"

"No."

"Good. I was hoping you were the kind of man who could admit that."

His mouth moved a little.

"I'll return it before the end of the watch."

The first argument concerned the tenders.

There was only one maintenance authorization, one inventory of tools, one scheduled return inspection. Jin had been keeping a handwritten supplementary log because he liked being able to see what he had crossed out.

Both notebooks contained the same misspelling of regulator.

Both Jins opened them to that page.

"Oh, that's cruel," said Theo.

"It wasn't spelled correctly when you borrowed it either."

Both Jins said that too.

Theo stood up.

"Can we stop?"

The silence afterward was more frightening than the doubled voices.

Ruth put her chart down.

"Stop what?"

"Doing that. Saying everything together. Knowing what everybody's going to say."

"All right," Petra said.

The other Petra inhaled to answer.

Closed her mouth.

On the next bench, the standing Theo sat down.

They began, awkwardly, to take turns.

It did not make them different people all at once. It made them eight people trying not to hurt one another with something none of them had chosen.

The names arrived over dinner.

Enterprise's galley sent covered trays, and the shuttle-bay duty officer tried to distribute them by the tenders' positions.

"Port crew, these four. Starboard crew, over there."

Theo looked toward the other Theo.

"Congratulations. You've been promoted to a direction."

"Could be worse. We could be Fore and Aft."

Ruth pointed a fork at them.

"Nobody starts that."

But Port and Starboard stayed.

Petra, the one with the burned hand, had been sitting under the portside work light when the trays arrived. That made her Port. The other woman accepted Starboard after checking that nobody intended the terms to describe order of manufacture.

"Just where we ate," she said.

"Just where you ate," the duty officer agreed.

Jin wrote it down.

The other Jin waited until he finished, then wrote it differently.

The medical readings changed overnight.

Ruth Port noticed the monitor rechecking before the nurse did. She had spent enough years in infirmaries to recognize an instrument that was trying to be tactful.

"What is it excluding?"

The nurse looked at the display.

"Background correlation noise."

"Stop excluding it."

The anomaly was faint. It occurred in both crews, intermittently, and did not match a familiar radiation-exposure pattern. Nobody was losing mass. Nobody was fading. Their hearts did not beat in synchrony. Their blood oxygen was perfectly ordinary.

But a field measurement attached to the rescue showed a residual relationship between the two groups and the damaged craft.

Enterprise's physician refused to turn that into a prognosis she did not possess.

"You are clinically stable," she told them. "Your exposure needs investigation. Those statements can both be true."

Ruth Starboard nodded.

"Keep that doctor."

Petra slept badly.

At some point a blanket moved beside her and she woke with her hand out, reaching for the person she had been dreaming was there.

It was herself.

Starboard Petra had moved a bench away during the night.

Neither mentioned it in the morning.

Outside the bay, the crisis continued. They heard scraps through duty communications: a missing buoy, injured people aboard the strange ship, arguments between captains.

Johnson did not give them classified information because they had survived something terrible. He did tell them what directly concerned their safety.

Later, when Avalon became visible and the name reached the shuttle bay, Theo went to the observation panel and stood there a long time.

"That is a very large maintenance tender," he said.

For once the other Theo did not have the same joke ready.

Johnson came back after the future ship departed.

Petra watched him enter without the borrowed cup.

"You returned it."

"Eventually."

"Everything all right outside?"

"No. Some things are better."

He sat on a spare container instead of making eight people stand.

"The ship you've heard about has returned to its own time and history. I have no arrangement to put any of you aboard her. I won't suggest otherwise."

Petra felt an absurd disappointment. She had not known she was hoping for anything.

"We're going back to Ganymede?"

"When Medical clears the transfer. The station has been informed that eight people require accommodation and that they are not to remove four names from the request."

"How did that go?"

"There were questions."

"Did you answer them?"

"Several times."

Theo Starboard raised a hand.

"Captain, can they make us choose?"

"Choose what?"

"Which crew goes back to work."

Johnson rested the logbook on his knee.

"They can determine whether the craft are safe. They can review staffing. None of that gives them permission to decide four of you didn't come out of that bay."

It was not the grand promise Theo had wanted.

It was specific enough to hold.

They stayed aboard Enterprise while local facilities prepared quarters and independent medical observation. One room became two. Four ration profiles became eight. The second tender received a temporary inventory suffix.

A security officer asked which crew should retain the old access codes.

Petra Port opened her mouth.

Petra Starboard got there first.

"Neither."

Everybody looked at her.

"Retire them. Issue eight new ones."

The officer considered this, then nodded.

It was the first administrative answer that did not begin by deciding who had been there first.

On the last evening before the transfer, Port and Starboard worked on the same pump assembly.

Port reached for a wrench.

Starboard moved it nearer.

Both stopped.

"That doesn't have to be forbidden," Port said.

"I know."

"I was going to ask for it."

"I know."

They smiled, tiredly.

Then Starboard said, "I'm not fixing your coffee."

"Our coffee?"

"No," she said. "You were right."

They finished the pump without making a rule about whose hands could know what.


## Two: The Offer

*Custody note: Avalon's later visit to the incident branch is a separate proposed mission. No passenger transfer is inserted into the original departure record. Station acceptance and ship transit authorization are separate documents.*

Three weeks passed on Ganymede.

The sky had the correct sun in it. Petra had not expected to need reassurance about that.

The facility gave them adjoining quarters and took them off active duty while the tender hulls were examined. Their pay continued through an emergency authorization which covered eight people and expired in thirty days.

Every morning somebody asked whether the authorization had been extended.

Every afternoon somebody else said it was being reviewed.

The local doctor was kind. The local administrator was kind. Even the security officer whose new access system locked both Jins out of the workshop apologized in person.

Kindness did not make the rooms large enough.

Petra Port tried to visit her old quarters.

She stood outside holding the access token and realized she did not know whether Starboard had already gone in.

The uncertainty was unbearable.

She went back to the temporary room, where Starboard was sitting on a bed with her boots on.

"Did you go?"

"No."

"Why?"

Starboard looked at the token in her own hand.

They put both tokens on the table.

That night they agreed to inventory the quarters together. Afterward neither would enter alone until they had decided what to do.

It was a reasonable arrangement.

Petra hated it more than any unreasonable thing anyone had done to her.

The medical anomaly did not worsen. It did not go away.

A scanner at the worksite occasionally associated one crew member's presence with the other crew's craft. A proximity lock closed on Ruth Starboard while Ruth Port was on the opposite side of the compartment. Both were unharmed. The incident was written up, access was suspended, and somebody called it a software fault until the physician attached the exposure data.

The next day Johnson contacted them.

He had an inquiry from the ship that had helped during the incident. A limited return to this branch was under consideration. Their physician wanted permission to share the relevant medical and field records.

"Only those records?" Ruth asked.

"Only those records."

She read the release twice.

Then she signed.

Each of them signed separately.

The answer took two days.

It contained uncertainty, which Petra had begun to recognize as a kind of professional courtesy.

Avalon's specialists had equipment capable of measuring the residual coupling at greater resolution. A station in their home history had facilities to isolate and study it without using occupied residential systems as test equipment.

The documents did not say those facilities would cure them.

They said the additional instruments might establish what precautions were necessary and whether the coupling could be reduced safely.

There was an offer of temporary accommodation. There was an offer of medical consultation without relocation. There was a request to hear from each crew member privately before anybody booked passage.

Theo Port read the opening paragraph three times.

"Sixty-eight years."

"And another branch," Jin said.

"Yes. I was going to get to the part where the universe is wider than my qualifications."

Ruth Starboard folded her hands.

"We don't vote on this as a crew."

Petra looked up.

Ruth was looking directly at both of her.

"We can travel together. We cannot decide together that eight people leave everything."

Petra wanted to object on the basis of command.

Then she realized that was why Ruth had said it.

The consultations began over a narrow authenticated communications connection, carried through a separately authorized observational lock. Enterprise supplied the local reception equipment and retained copies of its own records. Ganymede's physician sat beside Ruth when she wanted another doctor present.

Noah Hawkes spoke to them first.

He did not offer a place in his century as though he were giving away a spare cabin.

"You have homes where you are," he said. "Even if those homes have become difficult. I need you to understand that accepting help doesn't require accepting relocation."

Petra Starboard asked about returning.

"Possible only through a new cleared transit," Noah said. "Subject to conditions on both sides. I cannot promise you a commuter service between histories."

"Can you promise one return?"

"No. I can promise that any request will be considered under the same safety and rights review as this journey. If that isn't enough, it's a reason to stay."

The honesty hurt.

It also let her keep listening.

Ashley Hawkes appeared in a separate consultation with the station's housing director.

"We can receive you," she said. "That isn't an offer from my husband on my behalf. It's a station commitment I'm making within the accommodation we actually have."

"Together?" Theo asked.

"If you want adjoining quarters initially. Separate quarters are available. There is no requirement to remain a unit."

"What would we do there?"

"Initially? Recover. Learn the systems. Tell us when our arrangements aren't working."

Theo looked embarrassed.

"I meant work."

"I know. You don't have to earn the first week."

When REG joined the next consultation, Petra found herself looking for the signs that he was a projection.

He noticed.

"Yes," he said.

She flushed.

"I didn't ask."

"You were being polite. I can let you finish."

She almost laughed.

"Are you the doctor?"

"No. First officer. The Doctor is a different person and considerably less patient about that confusion."

He explained the measurements in language Ruth could interrogate and Petra could follow. There were several possible interpretations. Their apparatus might distinguish among them. There was no evidence that one crew was draining the other, or that either had to disappear for the remaining people to become safe.

"That last point," he said, "is worth stating before an imaginative administrator improves the problem."

For the first time in three weeks, Petra felt someone had put a hand between her and an approaching door.

The decisions did not arrive in order of rank.

Jin Starboard chose first.

He had a mother on Ganymede and an elder sister who lived two transport connections away. He spent four hours on a call with them. Came back with red eyes. Signed the provisional passage request.

"I want to find out what happened," he said.

Jin Port did not sign that day.

His mother wanted him to stay.

She also wanted the other Jin to stay.

Neither could bear how carefully she avoided saying which voice she missed when one of them left the room.

Theo Port chose after speaking to his parents. Theo Starboard waited until the next morning, which he insisted was unrelated.

Ruth Port had a partner on a survey rotation. Ruth Starboard had the same partner. They spoke on three separate calls and one awful joint call, then stopped trying to settle a relationship across a delayed channel.

"I am not deciding the rest of my life this afternoon," Ruth Starboard told the passage clerk.

"Understood."

"I am deciding to travel."

The clerk amended the form.

Petra spoke to her brother.

He wanted a practical explanation.

She gave him the best one she had.

Then he wanted an impossible explanation, and she had none.

Finally he asked whether he could send a package.

"What kind?"

"The blue bowl. Mum's one. You always said you'd collect it."

There was one bowl.

Petra closed her eyes.

"I'll ask her."

Her brother went quiet.

Then he said, "Both of you."

Port and Starboard packed the bowl together in a padded crate. It would belong to neither exclusively. That was the arrangement they could make, and no one was required to call it easy.

Jin Port signed last.

His reason was private. He gave the departure officer permission to record the decision and not the conversation behind it.

Nobody asked him to supply a better paragraph.

Avalon returned after separate transit review, with a receiving plan, medical isolation equipment, and a fresh local clearance coordinated through Enterprise.

She did not pretend to have been waiting invisibly all along.

Johnson came aboard the transfer shuttle with their records.

The eight of them were belted into seats along the sides. Between them stood a crate containing a bowl, two notebooks, a set of rebuilt pump tools, and more of a century than its dimensions suggested.

"I've provided certified copies of our rescue measurements," Johnson said. "Your local records remain here. Your departure does not withdraw them."

Theo Starboard nodded too quickly.

Johnson looked at him.

"You don't have to become people who were never here."

Theo looked down at the deck.

Petra Port said, "Thank you, Captain."

Starboard let the sentence belong to her.

When the shuttle crossed into Avalon's bay, Brakk was waiting beside a portable field-monitoring frame.

He looked at the crate.

"Anything in there that thinks it's a power source?"

"A bowl," Petra said.

"I've had worse answers."

The Doctor introduced himself without making a joke about seeing double.

Ruth appreciated that so much she told him.

"I assumed you'd heard it," he said.

"We have."

"Then I shall reserve my originality for your treatment."

They crossed into 2397 under a new transit record.

Petra watched the deck rather than the viewer. She had seen enough interesting light to last several lives.

When they arrived, a woman's voice spoke from Jupiter Station.

"Receiving reference authenticated. Avalon, hold at the medical transfer marker. Welcome to Jupiter."

Petra did not call it home.

Not then.

## Three: Eight Lines

*Initial station intake: eight named applicants, eight individual consent records. Service histories share a pre-event source and diverge thereafter. Temporary call signs do not replace legal names.*

The first thing Petra learned about the future was that somebody still had to find the blankets.

A station reception team had prepared eight beds, eight personal lockers, and a meal which arrived forty minutes late because the medical compartment's food-service connection had been disconnected during its last refurbishment.

Brakk sent the missing adapter over from Avalon.

The Doctor looked at the food.

"An excellent introduction to advanced civilization."

Ruth Port lifted the lid.

"Is it warm?"

"Mostly."

"We've had worse governments."

The two crews lived in adjacent medical accommodation for the first week. Isolation meant equipment separation and controlled testing, not silence. They could receive visitors. They could walk in a designated garden. They could object to a proposed test.

One of them did.

The test was rescheduled with a different procedure.

Petra watched that happen and slept better afterward.

The residual coupling proved measurable, stubborn, and narrower than anybody's worst fear. The field signature was associated most strongly with the tenders and certain materials aboard them. Separation from the craft reduced the interference. A series of isolated adjustments reduced it further.

No doctor announced that eight people had been restored to one correct timeline.

They were treated as eight patients.

When the Doctor cleared them for residential quarters, he gave each a copy of the findings.

"I would rather," he told Ruth, "that your next physician did not have to reconstruct my reasoning from an optimistic discharge sentence."

"Marry me," Ruth Port said.

Ruth Starboard looked up.

"Get in line."

He raised one eyebrow.

"I am prescribing rest."

The registry office occupied a room with an inconvenient pillar near the entrance.

Ensign Merlin had arranged his desk so he could see both sides of it.

He was younger-looking than Petra expected and older in ways she could not yet identify. A paper notebook sat beside his console. Its open page contained a shopping list crossed out with great determination.

Petra noticed him close it.

"Personal," he said.

"I didn't read it."

"I believe you."

There were eight chairs. One had an uneven leg.

Theo Port took that one, then immediately exchanged it with an empty chair by the wall.

Merlin waited until everyone sat.

"The first part is tedious," he said. "I would apologize, but you may prefer tedious."

"Today?" Ruth said. "Desperately."

The records appeared on a shared display, eight lines long.

They did not show four names followed by four exceptions.

Petra read hers.

Senior Chief Petra Lindqvist. Historical source: Ganymede incident branch, local 2329. Continuation designation: Port, self-selected. Current residence: Jupiter Station, protected home 2397, provisional resident status pending individual applications.

She stared at the last words.

"Provisional?"

"Your decision about permanent residence is not due today."

"What if I never make it?"

"Then we review your status with you. We don't let an unanswered question expire your existence."

Theo said, "Can we put that on the door?"

"The pillar gets more complaints."

REG entered as Jin Port was comparing his entry with Starboard's.

"Do we both have the same service before the shear?" Jin asked.

"Yes."

"Is that twice the service?"

REG sat down.

"It's two people with a shared service history. It isn't twice the hours worked before the event. It is two separate claims to benefits and standing afterward, subject to the applicable decisions we are documenting."

Jin considered this.

"That sounds expensive."

"Starfleet has survived admirals."

Petra laughed before she could stop herself.

REG let the laughter pass.

"The record forks at the event," he said. "The childhood is shared. The lives are yours."

Merlin did not type the sentence into their legal records as a regulation.

He wrote it in his notebook later, with permission.


Their quarters were on the same residential section at first.

That lasted four days.

Ruth Starboard requested a room farther from the lift because she had spent thirty years sleeping through engine noise and discovered she could not sleep through cheerful children waiting for school.

Ruth Port liked the noise.

Jin Port asked whether requesting separate rooms meant withdrawing from the crew.

The housing officer said it meant requesting separate rooms.

Jin stared at the form for so long that the officer offered him tea.

Theo and Theo took adjoining rooms and immediately began leaving tools in the passage between them.

A neighbor objected.

They apologized in perfect unison.

Then they argued about who should apologize for that.

Petra Port kept the blue bowl in her room for one week. Starboard kept it the next. A schedule that had seemed ridiculous in the transfer shuttle became ordinary through repetition.

They discovered that neither wanted to unpack the crate's other contents.

Work was easier to unpack.

Avalon's engineering staff arranged familiarization sessions before either crew touched a station system. Sixty-eight years of development had changed interfaces, access procedures, and failure modes. It had not abolished bad labeling.

Brakk gave them a panel whose exterior tag identified it as Auxiliary Environmental Distribution Three.

Inside, in handwritten letters on an ancient strip of tape, somebody had written: DO NOT BELIEVE THE TAG.

Theo Port looked almost happy.

"Finally. Something I understand."

Brakk leaned against the bulkhead.

"You're here to inspect. Don't repair anything you haven't been cleared to repair."

"What if it's obviously wrong?"

"Especially then."

Their old tenders remained under monitored storage. The flight clearance would take longer than the medical clearance. In the meantime, station maintenance offered supervised assignments that used their experience without treating unfamiliar technology as a character defect.

Some days they resented the supervision.

Some days they were grateful enough to hide it.

Petra met Sofia in a classroom with a broken display.

Efua Osei had asked Facilities for help. The request had passed through three queues and arrived in Petra's work list as a routine connector inspection.

Sofia was kneeling on the floor, collecting paper triangles while six children advised her about their intended arrangement.

"They were supposed to form a starship," she said.

Petra looked down.

"They could still form a debris field."

The children approved.

Sofia stood.

She had chalk on her sleeve despite the room containing no chalkboard, and an expression suggesting she would explain that only to someone who had earned it.

"Can you fix it?"

"Probably."

"That's more encouraging than the diagnostic."

"What did the diagnostic say?"

"That it was working."

Petra put her tools down.

"It may have a career in administration."

The display took seven minutes.

The paper starship took forty.

When Petra left, she had learned the names of three children, one invented species, and a woman who could say no to all six children without making the room smaller.

She went back the next week to check the connector.

It did not need checking.

Sofia handed her a chair.

"You could just ask about lunch."

"I'm doing a thorough job."

"So I see."

They ate in a public garden where the ceiling admitted enough of Jupiter to make conversation stop occasionally.

Petra told her about Ganymede, leaving out the most difficult parts.

Sofia listened without turning the omissions into questions.

At the end of lunch she asked, "Which Petra should I look for next time?"

Petra's fork stopped halfway to the plate.

"The one who's here."

Sofia took a breath.

"Fair. I meant, if I leave a message."

Petra set the fork down.

"Port. For now."

"Port," Sofia said.

It was a small repair.

Petra went home thinking about it.

She told Starboard three days later.

They were exchanging the blue bowl at the threshold between their rooms.

"I met someone."

Starboard smiled immediately.

Then she stopped smiling.

The change was so quick Petra felt she had witnessed something private in her own face.

"Oh," Starboard said.

"You don't know her."

"I know."

"I need you to know that."

"I do."

They stood holding opposite sides of the crate.

Finally Starboard let go.

"Tell me what you want me to do."

"Nothing. I just didn't want you hearing it from somebody else."

"All right."

After the door closed, Petra stayed beside it a long time.

On the other side of the corridor, Starboard sat down on the unopened end of her bed.

She knew the exact kind of smile Petra would have tried to conceal at lunch.

She had not met the woman.

She could imagine wanting to.

That was the part nobody's forms had mentioned.

The others were finding their own ways to surprise each other.

Theo Starboard enrolled in a preparatory engineering course, discovered a systems discipline he had never encountered, and missed dinner three nights running.

Theo Port accused him of becoming the sort of officer who used curriculum as a verb.

"You could come."

"I have work."

"So do I."

"You have school."

The argument ended there, unresolved and therefore capable of doing damage.

Ruth Port spent evenings corresponding with her partner in the old branch through the approved communications intervals. Ruth Starboard wrote one long letter, sent it, and asked that further messages be addressed separately.

Neither choice improved the other's confidence.

Jin and Jin discovered that matching handwriting could be deeply irritating when someone borrowed a tool and left a note.

They began signing with their temporary directions.

Then they began writing about things other than tools.

Jin Starboard liked the station's gardens. Jin Port found the apparent openness of the upper paths unsettling and preferred the machine shop.

They went to each other's places once.

That was enough for a while.

At Quark's, Tavren made the mistake of trying to place the Petras on a single tab.

"The name is the same," he said.

"So is yours on every receipt," Port replied. "Do we get to combine those?"

Tavren considered his position.

"Separate tabs."

"Thank you."

"Any existing debts?"

"Separate tabs, Tavren."

Jake Sisko sat at a nearby table, looking over a meal he had apparently forgotten to order.

He knew their story. Everybody who needed to know had been briefed in some form, and everybody else had invented something.

Petra Starboard approached him.

"You're the writer."

"One of them."

"Are you writing about us?"

"Not without asking."

"You're sitting where you can hear."

Jake looked at the table, then toward the bar.

"That's fair. I can move."

She had expected an argument about public places.

He gathered his PADD.

"Wait," she said.

He waited.

"Is it strange?"

"What?"

"Not writing something you could."

Jake looked at her for a moment.

"Less strange than explaining why I thought I owned it."

She took that away with her.

He moved anyway.

## Four: The Paragraph

*Registry review, third week after residential clearance: no personhood determination pending. Employment, service equivalency, and assignment decisions remain distinct questions. A proposed assignment is not an accepted assignment.*

Commander Lena Arendt's message arrived during a lesson on current small-craft controls.

Petra Starboard was learning to trust an interface that kept moving the most important information to wherever it believed her eyes were about to go.

"Can I turn that off?"

The instructor looked relieved.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you say so?"

"Some people prefer it."

"Some people eat breakfast standing over the sink."

Port, beside her, said, "Leave my kitchen out of this."

The instructor pretended not to hear.

Merlin called them after the session.

He asked them to come at a time when all eight could attend, then corrected himself.

"If you want a joint discussion. Individual appointments are available."

They chose the joint discussion.

It had become important to be asked.

The message was longer than one paragraph, but one paragraph carried the decision.

Starfleet Personnel proposed recognizing both crews' completed service while assigning one four-person unit to an available support posting beyond the Jovian system. The other unit could continue its current local attachment during evaluation. The proposal listed Starboard as the departing unit because its temporary intake suffix had been entered second.

Theo Port read that part aloud.

"Entered second."

Merlin nodded.

"That is the stated administrative basis."

"Not qualifications?"

"No."

"Not who asked?"

"No request to depart is attached."

Ruth Starboard read the rest of the message.

"There's an appeal procedure."

"There is."

"A reply date."

"Yes."

"And somebody thought sending us sixty-eight years forward wasn't enough travel for one year."

Nobody laughed.

Petra Port had expected to feel protective.

Instead she felt relief so sudden and shameful that she could not look up.

One set of boots in the corridor. One person with her face arriving at Sofia's classroom. A bowl that could stay on a shelf.

The relief lasted less than a second.

It was enough to know it had happened.

Starboard looked at her.

Petra could not tell what she had seen.

Ashley reviewed the proposal in her office, not in a tribunal chamber.

The distinction did not comfort Theo.

He arrived wearing a uniform he had spent too long getting right.

"I don't know what they're allowed to do," he said.

Ashley put her PADD down.

"Assign personnel within the applicable authority. Review qualifications. Make a case for a posting."

"And this?"

"We're going to examine the basis for this."

"Can you stop them?"

"I can state the station's needs, offer funded billets within my authority, and request a joint review. I cannot promise you that no one wearing a uniform will ever receive an assignment they dislike."

Ruth Port nodded.

"Good. That would be a very strange service."

Ashley looked around the room.

"What I can insist upon is that the event which produced your situation isn't used as a substitute for assessing each of you."

Petra Starboard had not spoken.

Ashley waited until she did.

"What if I want to teach?"

"Then I want to hear about it."

"Small craft. I liked the lesson today. I liked helping the others through it."

Port turned toward her.

Starboard kept looking at Ashley.

"I'm good at flying. I may be good at teaching somebody else how not to be frightened of it."

"Those aren't the same skill," Ashley said.

"I know."

"Prepare a proposal with the flight-training officer. A real one. Hours, requirements, supervision, openings."

Something changed in Starboard's face.

Nobody had offered her a place because she was the second version.

She had suggested work.

Someone had asked her to show she could do it.

Arendt agreed to attend a review on station.

Her reply was courteous, specific, and almost aggressively punctual.

"Bring the assignment paragraph," Ashley said in her acknowledgment. "We'll review it together."

Petra Port went to Sofia that evening.

The classroom was empty. Someone had left one paper triangle under a table, and Sofia was trying to reach it with a broom.

"Don't rescue me," she said. "I'm committed."

Petra sat on a desk.

Sofia retrieved the triangle.

Looked at her.

"What happened?"

Petra told her about the proposal.

Then she told her about the relief.

She had intended to conceal that part. It came out while she was explaining something less important, suddenly there between them.

Sofia leaned the broom against the wall.

"You don't have to turn a feeling into a plan."

"I wanted her gone."

"For a moment."

"Is that better?"

"It means you have to decide what you do after the moment."

Petra looked at the dark display she had repaired.

"I don't know how to share this."

"Me?"

The word stopped her.

"I didn't mean it that way."

"Then let's be careful about what you do mean."

Petra's face burned.

Sofia sat beside her.

"I like you. We've had lunch four times and dinner twice. That's real. It doesn't give either of us a claim on another person's decisions."

"I know."

"And I haven't met her."

"No."

"Would meeting her hurt you?"

"Probably."

Sofia nodded slowly.

"Then we should say that honestly before anybody decides being generous is a test you have to pass."

They sat without touching.

It was the most intimate part of the evening.

Port did not ask Sofia to stay away from Starboard.

She did not arrange a meeting either.

The following morning Starboard found her in the garden.

"I applied," she said.

"For the teaching?"

"Assessment first."

"Good."

"Do you mean good?"

Port looked up.

"Yes."

Starboard sat beside her.

After a while Port said, "I was relieved."

"I know."

"I didn't tell you what I was relieved about."

"You didn't have to."

Port shook her head.

"That's exactly the thing I can't stand."

Starboard looked away.

"I guessed," she said. "I don't know. I can be wrong about you."

Port let out a breath she had been holding for weeks.

"Please be wrong about me sometimes."

"I'll do my best."

They sat watching a child chase a floating toy along the lower path.

Starboard said, "I was relieved too."

"About leaving?"

"About the possibility that somebody else would decide. That I could hate them instead of figuring out what I want."

The child caught the toy and immediately dropped it again.

Neither Petra moved to help.

After a while they went to work.


## Five: The Table

*Review entry: Commander Arendt attended under Personnel's delegated assignment authority. Captain Ashley Hawkes represented Jupiter Station. Applicants could attend, designate representation, or submit separate statements. They chose to attend.*

Arendt arrived with a small bag, a creased uniform, and no visible intention of being the villain in anybody's afternoon.

She had taken the first available transport after an inspection at another facility. Her connecting berth had changed twice. She asked where she could put her bag and whether there was time to wash her hands.

Ashley showed her.

That unsettled Petra more than an officious entrance would have done.

At the table, Arendt placed the assignment proposal beside a second document.

"The second is the vacancy list I was using," she said. "I'd like it included."

Merlin accepted both.

There were twelve people in the room. Eight applicants, two captains' representatives in the form of Ashley and REG, a science officer, and the ensign taking the record.

Noah was not there.

Petra had expected him.

Ashley explained before the meeting began.

"Avalon supplied transport and technical support. This review concerns station placements and your service applications. My husband isn't needed to make my decisions valid."

Ruth Starboard settled back in her chair.

Arendt opened with the part of the case Petra had hoped she would not be able to make.

Starfleet had a limited number of supervised transition positions for personnel whose qualifications belonged to different eras. Those positions required instructors, equipment access, medical coordination, and officers who could verify that current operating procedures were understood.

The proposed support posting had all of those things.

It also had four vacancies.

Jupiter Station did not have four matching vacancies at the time of the initial request.

"Keeping an experienced crew together can be beneficial," Arendt said. "You know how to work together. The receiving command requested a unit."

Theo Port leaned forward.

"Then why Starboard?"

Arendt looked at the paragraph.

"That is the weak part of the proposal."

"Why is it in the strong proposal?"

"Because I treated the intake designations as administratively interchangeable. I should have asked before selecting one."

She said it plainly enough that Theo had nowhere to put his prepared anger.

He sat back.

Arendt continued.

"That mistake doesn't remove the staffing problem. We have to qualify you, assign duties we can support, and avoid leaving people in provisional attachments indefinitely. I don't consider an indefinite wait a kindness."

Petra Port found herself agreeing.

She resented that too.

Ellie brought up the field findings.

She did not make them beautiful.

The rescue telemetry bounded a shared pre-separation state. Later records showed measurable divergence. The post-arrival treatment reduced interference sufficiently for the Doctor to clear ordinary residential activity and supervised work. Continued monitoring remained recommended.

"Can you establish which tender was produced from which?" Arendt asked.

"Not from the evidence we have."

"Would it change the employment question?"

"No," Ellie said. "It could change how we describe the event. It wouldn't make one patient's training vanish."

Arendt nodded.

"Agreed."

REG placed the service histories on the display.

"You have a record problem," he said. "It's tractable."

"Show me."

"Shared pre-event source, separately attributed after the event. Unique current identifiers. No shared access credentials. Individual qualification records. Benefits questions referred to the responsible authority instead of hidden inside a name field."

"And their past service?"

"Recorded as shared history, not split into half-credit."

"That will require a ruling beyond my office."

"Then mark it referred. Don't let an unresolved pension question prevent a current training authorization."

Arendt made a note.

It was the first time Petra saw the problem become smaller without any of them being asked to become smaller with it.

Merlin showed the actual station records.

Eight access identifiers. Eight medical releases. Eight accommodation requests, with later individual changes. Training participation separated by person.

He paused at the incident log.

"There was one incorrect tool assignment in the workshop, corrected before issue. Two misdirected personal messages, returned unopened. The name collision contributed to both. We changed the display format with the applicants' agreement."

"So the system has had problems," Arendt said.

"Yes."

"And you aren't claiming otherwise."

"No, Commander. I'm showing what failed and what was changed."

The ensign's ears colored a little.

He kept going.

"The current distinction is being maintained through identifiers and custody, not by relocating four people until nobody has to learn their names."

Arendt looked at him.

"Point taken."

Ashley then put the vacancy list on the shared display.

"This is where the station's proposal differs."

It was not an offer of eight invented permanent jobs.

Two maintenance placements were already funded. One small-craft instruction assessment could be supported within existing training hours. A systems documentation project needed one additional technician. The medical department had requested transitional clinical support subject to current credential review.

Theo Starboard had applied for further study. That changed his near-term placement request.

Ruth Port was considering a survey attachment with an explicit return arrangement. She had not accepted it yet.

Eight people no longer fitted the shape of two identical four-person crews.

They had supplied the evidence for that themselves.

Arendt read the requests.

Then read them again.

"These came after the proposal."

"Most did," Ashley said.

"If I'd had them earlier, I'd have written a different proposal."

"Then let's write it now."

Petra Starboard put her palms on the table.

The burn on Port's hand had healed enough to leave a pink line. Starboard's hand remained unmarked.

She had spent weeks hating how easy that made them to distinguish.

Now she stopped looking at it.

"Commander, I don't object to another posting because it's far away. If it were work I wanted, I might apply."

Arendt turned toward her.

"What do you object to?"

"Being moved with three other people because we ate on the same side of a shuttle bay."

The room stayed still.

"We chose those names because they were ours to choose. They weren't orders. We aren't asking to be one person. We're asking not to be half each."

Arendt looked at the designation on the proposal.

Petra continued before she lost her nerve.

"Give us different work because we want different work, or because the service needs work done and you've considered us properly. Don't start with the direction and call it a crew."

Ruth Starboard put her hand beside Petra's, not on it.

Arendt took off the thin reading lenses she had been wearing.

"I can withdraw the unit selection," she said. "I have authority to approve the transitional assignments Captain Hawkes has offered, subject to the credential conditions we've identified. The benefit questions go to the appropriate board with your shared-history documentation attached."

Theo Port asked, "And Starboard?"

"The word stays in the record where you asked it to stay. It doesn't decide the posting."

Petra Port realized she had been pressing her heel against the floor.

She stopped.

Ashley summarized the proposed decision and asked each person whether it accurately reflected the request they had made.

One person corrected her.

It was Jin Port.

The documentation project was not the position he wanted. He had agreed to observe it while deciding between that and workshop work.

Ashley amended the entry.

Arendt waited.

Nobody accused him of making the solution untidy.

The meeting took another forty minutes.

They discussed dates, current qualifications, medical appointments, supervision, and the appeal route for the benefits referral. Ruth asked an unpleasantly precise question about whether provisional status could interrupt medication access. The Doctor was consulted. A written answer came back.

Petra had thought victory would feel like a door opening.

It felt like being tired enough to trust a chair.

At the end, Arendt asked for the registry model.

"We have another case," she said.

Everyone looked at her.

"Not this event. Different circumstances. A long-range mission with conflicting service records. Your method may help."

Merlin glanced toward the eight of them.

"With their identifying information removed?"

"Of course."

The ensign recorded the request.

Outside, Arendt picked up her bag.

Theo Starboard held the door for her.

She stopped beside him.

"Engineering study?"

"That's the plan."

"Finish the prerequisite mathematics before you decide the faculty is wrong."

He blinked.

"I haven't said they're wrong."

"You have the expression."

After she left, he turned to Port Theo.

"Do I?"

Port Theo looked delighted.

"Exactly the expression."

For once it was pleasant to be recognized.

## Six: A Table for Three

*Residential note: relationship histories require their participants' permission. This entry records only the accommodation changes they authorized. The dinner described here is fiction within the candidate story, not a transcript filed by the communications desk.*

Sofia met Starboard Petra in a lift.

Neither had arranged it.

Sofia recognized the face, then the absence of the small healing mark on the hand holding the rail.

She did not use either observation as an introduction.

"I'm Sofia."

Petra's eyes moved toward her.

"I know."

The lift hummed between decks.

Sofia said, "I don't know whether that's a good beginning."

"It's the beginning we've got."

They both smiled, cautiously.

Starboard Petra got out one stop early.

Then stood in the corridor, annoyed with herself, until the next lift arrived.

She told Port that evening.

Port asked whether they had talked.

"Thirty seconds."

"About what?"

"How badly it was going."

Port laughed.

Then she sat down because laughing had not made it stop hurting.

They did not decide what Sofia should do.

They asked whether she would meet them somewhere none of them lived.

She chose the garden.

The conversation lasted until the lights shifted to evening.

Most of it would have disappointed anyone looking for a neat solution.

Sofia was attracted to Port. She found Starboard funny in a different way once she stopped bracing herself against resemblance. She did not know whether attraction to one person would become affection for another.

Starboard did not want to be invited into a relationship because the doorway happened to fit her face.

Port did not want every moment she had shared with Sofia treated as an advance copy of a future belonging to all three.

They said these things badly.

Sofia asked them to say some again.

At one point Starboard stood up, walked to the edge of the path, and watched a maintenance drone trim a hedge with excessive seriousness.

Nobody followed.

When she came back, Port had moved her bag off the third chair.

That was all.

The next weeks had no grand experiment in them.

Sofia had dinner with Port.

She had coffee with Starboard.

She canceled an evening with both because she was exhausted and wanted to go to bed without managing anybody's feelings.

Port brought her soup and left it outside.

Starboard messaged to ask whether she wanted the soup moved closer to the door.

Sofia laughed hard enough to spill some.

Later, she told them both.

It became a joke they were allowed to share.

The blue bowl moved to Sofia's quarters for a meal and then went back with Starboard because that was its scheduled week.

Sofia thought the schedule was sensible.

Port stared at her.

"What?"

"Nobody's called it sensible before."

"Does it work?"

"Mostly."

"Then you can stop auditioning it for my approval."

There were difficult evenings.

Port heard about a conversation she had not attended and felt excluded with the violence of someone losing a memory that had never been hers.

Starboard caught herself trying to reproduce a joke she knew Port would have made. Sofia did not laugh. They talked about it afterward, and Starboard hated that more than if Sofia had laughed.

Nobody announced that the situation proved people were infinitely adaptable.

Some days they were not.

One evening Port said she needed a week without any meetings involving all three of them.

Sofia said yes.

Starboard went back to her own quarters and discovered that being allowed to be disappointed was different from being asked to stop existing.

By the time they discussed living arrangements, they had stopped using survival as an argument for intimacy.

There were three people at the table.

There were three separate answers.

They chose larger adjoining quarters with an internal door that could be closed. The housing officer made no comment about the arrangement beyond asking who needed access to which room.

Sofia kept her own bedroom.

Port kept hers.

Starboard put the bowl on a shelf in the shared sitting room and said anyone who called it a symbol had to dust it.

Two months after the registry review, they held a small commitment dinner.

It was not the old marriage continuing by administrative accident. They had written their own promises. The legal formalities remained their business, and the guest list was short.

Efua brought food.

Ruth Starboard brought a salad she admitted someone else had made.

Ruth Port joined over an approved recorded message from her survey assignment, complaining that she had finally obtained a quiet berth and missed the lift noise.

The two Theos arrived separately because one had been studying and the other refused to excuse him for it.

Jin Port carried a handwritten card.

Jin Starboard carried a different one.

Noah and Ashley came after duty.

Noah looked at the bowl.

"Nice shelf."

"Don't," Port said.

"I was complimenting the shelf."

Starboard regarded him with suspicion.

Sofia handed him a plate before he could make it worse.

At dinner, Theo Port asked whether the three of them had expected this.

"No," Sofia said.

"At any point?"

"Several. Usually incorrectly."

The laughter eased something in the room.

Sofia looked at the two women beside her.

"I fell in love with two people," she said. "I took a while to stop asking that to be simpler."

Port reached for her hand.

Starboard did not.

She was busy rescuing the bread from a sauce spill, which Sofia loved her for afterward.

Outside the quarters, somebody hurried down the corridor late for something ordinary.

For an hour nobody at the table needed to explain how they had come to be there.


## Seven: Different Work

*Assignment closeout: individual placements accepted. No unit is dissolved by this entry because no new permanent unit was created by the intake names. Both tender hulls retain separate inspection and flight records.*

The first student to shout at Petra Starboard was twice her age.

He had spent thirty years operating cargo loaders and considered the small-craft training interface a deliberate insult.

"It keeps anticipating."

"Turn the assistance off."

"They said it improves response."

"Does it?"

"No."

"Then turn it off."

He did.

The craft stopped lurching ahead of his corrections.

Petra waited through three controlled turns before saying anything else.

Later he asked how long she had been teaching.

"Not long."

"You don't talk like the others."

"Give me time."

He smiled.

She realized she had not made a joke to keep him from being afraid.

She had waited until he could make one himself.

Port came to watch a later session and stayed behind the observation glass. Starboard knew she was there. Neither pretended the other could be invisible.

Afterward Port said, "You let them take longer."

"They need longer."

"I'd have corrected that second turn."

"I know."

Starboard winced.

Port lifted a hand.

"No. That one you know."

They walked toward the lift.

"I liked it," Port said.

"What?"

"Watching you do something I wouldn't."

Starboard looked at her.

Then away.

It was the nearest either had come to saying goodbye without leaving.

The tenders came out of storage after a long sequence of inspections, isolated field tests, and limited operation trials.

Their original equipment inventory had become a history rather than a usable maintenance list.

Brakk refused to authorize a launch until each power circuit had been checked against the hardware actually installed.

Theo Port spent a week tracing cables with an expression of holy outrage.

"What civilization labels a connector compatible when it requires an adapter?"

"The one you're living in," Brakk said.

Theo stayed with the tender program.

Theo Starboard passed the prerequisite mathematics and sent Port a message containing no words, only the result.

Port replied with a photograph of a correctly labeled circuit.

Neither congratulated the other explicitly.

Both kept the messages.

The Jins began exchanging one page every week.

At first the pages were lists of work completed.

Then Jin Starboard described a child in the garden who had asked whether plants could become homesick.

Jin Port wrote back about a repair bay that smelled like Ganymede when the coolant system was open.

The handwriting diverged slowly.

One stopped crossing the sevens.

The other began leaving more space at the margins.

They could have dictated their letters. They could have sent much longer messages.

They liked the limit of a page.

It forced them to choose something the other person had not lived.

Ruth Port returned from the survey attachment with a sunburn the Doctor found professionally unnecessary.

Ruth Starboard met her at the medical entrance.

"Have fun?"

"Sometimes."

"Good."

"Miss me?"

"Sometimes."

They went inside together.

Their partner's correspondence remained separate. One relationship continued at a distance. The other became a friendship with a complicated beginning.

Neither woman considered her outcome a judgment on the other's courage.

They were learning to stop comparing every departure to a verdict.

## Eight: Received

*Final registry annotation: the incident remains open in its unresolved particulars. Closing the local intake tasks does not close the missing buoy's file or certify Rusk's account. The people need not remain provisional because the investigation remains incomplete.*

Merlin asked for permission to close the intake file on a morning when three other files had arrived incomplete.

He found Petra Port at the flight board.

"Nothing about your residence changes," he said. "This is the intake workflow. The completed tasks."

"Do we get a ceremony?"

"I can make the completion tone louder."

"Don't."

He showed her the entry.

She read it carefully, even the parts she had already agreed to.

Their former unit designation remained in the history. Their current assignments appeared individually. The crew photographs taken on Enterprise-B sat beside later portraits, none substituted for the others.

At the bottom was a link to the ongoing incident record.

"Still open?" she asked.

"The buoy remains displaced. We have no authenticated destination or recovery."

"And that keeps us open?"

"No. It keeps that question open."

Petra looked at him.

He looked almost embarrassed by how much the distinction mattered.

"Good," she said.

REG joined them as the first flight authorizations appeared.

He checked the board, then Petra.

"Senior Chief."

"Commander."

"Ready?"

"I've been ready for three days. Engineering was being unreasonable."

"Engineering kept the evidence."

"Engineering kept my ship."

"That too."

Starboard Petra arrived with a student pilot and a training checklist.

The two tender hulls had been cleared for different assignments. Port would make a maintenance run along the external service structures. Starboard would take an instructor-supervised familiarization flight with her first current-system conversion student.

The call signs had been selected by the crews, checked for traffic conflicts, and approved.

Callisto Port.

Callisto Starboard.

Merlin had asked whether they wanted to retain the directions.

Jin Port had answered for himself first.

"Yes."

Then the others answered.

No chorus. No ceremony. Eight replies.

At the launch bay, Theo Port stood under the access hatch holding a last-minute inspection note.

Petra recognized the expression.

"Tell me."

"The notation on the aft coolant coupling says caution, hot."

"Is it?"

"When the system's running."

"So that's accurate."

"Yes."

"Then why are you looking at it like that?"

Theo lifted his hand.

"I thought about changing it."

"To what?"

He looked at the small pale scar on her palm.

"Something more specific."

Petra put her hand on his shoulder.

"Leave it."

He nodded.

Across the bay, Starboard was telling her student where to put a bag so it could not foul the emergency release.

She did not look over.

Petra boarded her tender.

The cockpit had changed. New displays, new safety equipment, an interface configured by her own preference. The old scuff beside the reactor control remained.

Jin Port sat behind her.

Ruth was not aboard. A routine maintenance run did not require a corpsman simply because the first crew had included one.

That empty seat made Petra smile.

They were allowed to stop being the emergency.

Theo settled into the right-hand station.

"Guidance?"

"Reading station traffic."

"Manual available?"

"Available."

"Good."

Outside, the bay crew released the restraints.

For a moment the memory of another bay held her still. Yellow sleeves. Blue sleeves. Someone approaching with an extinguisher before understanding what she was.

She touched the control.

"Jupiter Operations, Callisto Port. Request departure clearance for maintenance assignment seven."

The answer came from the station, clear and ordinary.

"Callisto Port, cleared via outer service lane. Maintain assigned separation. Good morning."

Petra looked at the other tender.

Starboard raised a hand through the glass.

Port raised hers.

Not a test.

Then she turned toward Jupiter.

---

Ashley watched the two departure tracks separate from Operations.

One curved toward the external service structures. The other climbed into the training volume with an instructor aboard who had once believed she could only be a pilot.

Noah stood beside her with his hands around a cup.

"Both out?"

"Both cleared."

"How's the student?"

"Ask his instructor after the flight."

He smiled.

Below them, the station continued its morning. A classroom display came on. Somewhere a delivery missed its preferred window. Someone complained about breakfast. There were people asleep whose work would begin when these crews came home.

Ashley's next call arrived before Noah finished his drink.

She took it.

He waited until she had a free hand, touched it briefly, and went back to his ship.

---

Merlin entered the departures separately.

He had learned enough not to make eight people into an elegant sentence before giving them their names.

He recorded the actual clearances, assignments, crews aboard, and receiving authorities. The empty medical seat did not become an omission. The student did not inherit anybody's old history.

Then he opened the narrative annotation Petra had authorized.

His notebook lay beside the console.

He used the console first.

The entry belonged in a record that others could retrieve.

He wrote the final three sentences, read them once, and left them alone.

*Both versions transmitted.*

*Both versions received.*

*Neither struck from the record.*

**End.**
