# STAR TREK: JUPITER STATION
## MEMORY'S FLAME
### The Room That Remembers

**By Noah Hawkes**

*Developed with AI drafting assistance under the author's creative direction.*

**USS Avalon, NCC-75154 | Jupiter Station | 2397**
**Narration: Captain Noah A. Hawkes**
**Status:** New AI-assisted fiction at Noah Hawkes's direction. Narrative developments are not automatically promoted to the series canon.

The strange thing about commanding a timeship is that everybody assumes you can be home for dinner.

I've tried explaining this to Ashley.

Temporal navigation and getting home on time are different branches of science. One involves chronometric references, field regulators, a shipboard temporal core, and engineers who become very nervous whenever two clocks disagree.

The other involves my wife.

I know which one frightens me more.

It was late when I got back to my ready room aboard Avalon. Jupiter filled half the window. The station's illuminated docking pylons looked almost delicate against the planet, though I knew there were thousands of people going about their lives inside.

Our family was over there.

The children had their own things going on. Ashley had a station to command. A station with schools, research facilities, restaurants, families, visiting admirals, and enough classified technology to make a Starfleet security officer develop a nervous twitch.

And here I was, trying to drink a Coke Zero with vanilla before another person interrupted me.

I took a sip.

Warm.

Disgusting.

"Computer, recycle. Cold replacement."

The replicator chirped.

Behind me, someone cleared his throat.

"Reg, you know there's a door."

"I used it, Captain."

"How long have you been standing there?"

"Since before the unfortunate beverage experiment."

I laughed and sat down.

REG looked troubled.

That wasn't unusual. Reginald Barclay had always been capable of imagining seventeen things that might go wrong before most people had thought of one. Most of those worries had turned out to be unfounded. Enough had not.

"What's wrong?"

"I found a discrepancy."

"How big?"

"Forty-three seconds."

I put the glass down.

"Reg, the last time we found one unexplained second, we nearly spent the rest of our careers arguing with Temporal Investigations."

"This is not that kind of discrepancy."

"Good."

"It concerns you."

"Less good."

A personnel file appeared over my desk.

There was my photograph.

My name.

An official-looking Starfleet heading.

And a record stating that I had taken command of Avalon in 2379 and subsequently commanded Jupiter Station.

I stared at it.

Then I started laughing.

"That's impressive. Apparently I was promoted before I finished becoming an officer."

"The document came from a recovered historical compendium," REG said.

"Did someone verify it?"

"Not adequately."

"And why am I seeing it?"

"Because a reconciliation service considered the record internally consistent."

There it was.

I remembered Admiral Owen Paris handing me a captain's report decades earlier. That captain had managed to describe the loss of a shuttlecraft as a scheduling inconvenience. The admiral made me read the entire file until I could tell the difference between what the report said and what actually happened.

Apparently the lesson was still useful.

"Reg, a neat document isn't proof."

"I know."

"No. You knew that yesterday. Why did this one get through?"

He looked at me with an expression I remembered from the original Reg Barclay, back when Pathfinder was trying to reach Voyager.

"I trusted the summary because the supporting documents repeated it."

"Were they independent?"

"No."

"So we had one error wearing three different uniforms."

"Yes, Captain."

I leaned back.

"Now we're getting somewhere."

REG shifted uncomfortably.

"The station's public historical directory displayed you as commanding officer for forty-three seconds."

I was on my feet.

"Did that change anybody's authorization?"

"No. Captain Ashley Hawkes remains the station's commanding officer. The command system rejected the mismatch."

"Thank God."

"She is aware of the incident."

"Of course she is."

"She has sent a message."

"Read it."

REG paused.

"She said, 'Tell my husband that if he wants to command my station, he can start by attending tomorrow's waste-processing maintenance briefing.'"

I laughed so hard I had to sit back down.

"That's my girl."

REG allowed himself a smile.

Then his face went serious again.

"Captain, the same record has arrived three times through separate import paths."

"All from the same source?"

"We don't know yet."

"Get Tangly. No more silent corrections. Keep the original record, every version, and the complete chain. Ask Ashley to lock promotion into station command history until the sources are reviewed."

"Understood."

"And Reg?"

"Yes?"

"Don't erase the false one."

He tilted his head.

"Why preserve a false record?"

"Because someday somebody's going to ask how it got here. I'd like us to have an answer that isn't 'we deleted the evidence.'"

***

Tangly joined us through the bridge channel.

The lieutenant looked as though we'd interrupted a very good night for having absolutely nothing happen.

"Captain. REG. I've isolated the incoming files."

"What are they?"

"Three copies of a secondary historical document. The first entered through a personnel mirror, the second through a research cache, and the third through a Memory Beta comparison queue."

"Same original?"

"Most likely. Their wording is different, but the errors share the same ancestry."

REG frowned.

"The first is polished. The second is even more polished. The third cites both."

"So it cited itself twice," I said.

"Essentially."

"That's almost funny."

Tangly didn't smile.

"The same file contains a reference to Avalon's 2395 refit."

I looked at the chronometer.

The one-second anomaly.

Maren Voss and I had spent months refusing to call that test a success or a failure until we understood the independent recordings. We had stopped it precisely because we didn't know what had happened.

"What does the document claim?" I asked.

"That the missing second was recovered from another timeline."

"Evidence?"

"None attached."

"Then it's a claim. Not a discovery."

"Acknowledged."

Tangly hesitated.

"There's something else."

"Go ahead."

"The file contains four words that are not present in the earlier versions."

She placed them on my display.

**WHO ARE YOU, REALLY?**

For a moment, I was sixteen years old in Voyager's Sickbay again.

I remembered Q looking at me with an expression I had never expected to see on his face.

I remembered the Doctor. Janeway. The people who had become my family in the Delta Quadrant.

And I remembered the question.

Who are you?

I swallowed.

"Reg, source of the four words?"

"Unknown."

"Do we know whether they came from Q?"

"No."

"From a temporal branch?"

"No."

"From somebody playing a joke?"

"Equally unestablished."

I smiled.

"Good. That's our answer."

Tangly nodded. "Unknown. Quarantined. No attribution."

"Exactly. Now let's go see my wife before the personnel database promotes me to president of the Federation."

***

Jupiter Station sounded different from Avalon.

A ship has a rhythm, a sense of machinery taking people somewhere.

A station has the rhythm of people already where they want to be.

When I stepped off the transporter pad, I passed a family hurrying toward the recreation decks. Two junior officers were arguing about dinner. A maintenance technician was complaining that the decorative lights near the shuttlebay had been put in the worst possible location for access to an overhead panel.

That one made me smile.

The whole thing had started as a family project.

Lights. Arches. Something festive.

I could still picture everybody working together, the awkward connections, the tools scattered around, the ridiculous determination it took to get an ordinary decoration to cooperate with the laws of physics.

We had built it anyway.

Then the lights came on, and suddenly the effort disappeared behind the experience.

That was the part I wanted to remember.

Not just the dimensions of the arches.

The people.

Ashley was waiting for me in Operations.

"Captain," I said.

"Captain," she replied.

"Nice station you've got here."

"Thank you. I'd like to keep commanding it."

"I can see why."

She handed me a PADD.

"Your people identified the problem. Mine contained it. No command authority changed. Nobody gets to publish the corrected version until I've seen the audit."

"Agreed."

"And Noah?"

"Yeah?"

"Don't let REG turn this into a six-hour philosophical discussion."

I looked at the PADD.

"Could take seven."

She shook her head.

"Come home for dinner."

"Yes, ma'am."

***

Jake Sisko caught me outside the observation lounge.

He had a notebook in his hand, which meant trouble.

"I hear you commanded Jupiter Station for forty-three seconds."

"Jake."

"How was your tenure?"

"Very successful. No casualties."

"Any major reforms?"

"I left the station exactly as I found it."

"An achievement some admirals never manage."

I laughed.

He lowered the notebook.

"Seriously. Why did the system accept it?"

"Because somebody wrote a convincing story out of half-correct records."

"And that's different from what I do?"

"Yes. You tell the reader which parts you know."

He considered that.

"Usually."

"Jake."

"All right. I try."

We stood before the great window.

Beyond it, Avalon lay against Jupiter, her long Sovereign-class hull catching the light from the station.

I thought about the people who had brought me there.

Janeway, who had taken a chance on a boy with no business aboard Voyager.

Tuvok, who taught me that confidence wasn't qualification.

Admiral Paris, who made me read other captains' logs until I understood why a decision couldn't be separated from its circumstances.

The three years on the Bozeman-A.

The officers who had taught me to be a commander before I became a captain.

The person who commanded Avalon before me.

The crew who had watched me earn the chair.

"That's what the bad file stole," I said.

Jake looked up.

"What?"

"The years. It gave me the destination and erased the journey."

He wrote that down.

For once, I didn't tell him to stop.

***

Later, I returned to my ready room.

Not for another report. Not to solve the mystery of the strange words or the one-second discrepancy.

I just wanted to stand there.

The room smelled like it always did. The book was still beside the couch. My cold drink sat on the desk.

Jupiter was still outside the window.

And Jupiter Station was still home.

REG appeared in the doorway.

"Captain, we've retained the original sources and issued the correction record. The inquiry remains open."

"Good work, Reg."

"Thank you."

He lingered.

"What should we remember about tonight?"

I looked around the room.

"The wrong record. How it got here. The fact that we caught it. The fact that we didn't pretend to know what the four words meant."

"And?"

I smiled.

"The conversation with Ashley. Jake being annoying. The lights in the shuttlebay."

REG considered that.

"Those last details do not appear in the technical incident report."

"Then maybe we need a better kind of report."

For a while he said nothing.

Then he smiled.

"I believe we do, Captain."

I sat down and opened my personal journal.

Thirty-six years ago, a boy who knew the names of fictional starships asked whether a stranded crew could use an extra hand.

He could have taken a shortcut out of life.

Instead, he stayed.

He worked. He grew up. He made mistakes. He became a lieutenant, a commander, and finally a captain.

Tonight the computer tried to tell me none of that mattered because the ending was easier to store.

Well, the computer was wrong.

I took another drink.

Still cold.

Perfect.

"Computer. Begin personal log."

The recorder chimed.

"There's something I want to tell you about the people who brought me home."

**END CHAPTER**
