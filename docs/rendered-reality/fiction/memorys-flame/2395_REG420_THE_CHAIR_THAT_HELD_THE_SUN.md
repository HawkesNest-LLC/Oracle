# STAR TREK: AVALON
## MEMORY'S FLAME
### The Chair That Held the Sun

**By Noah Hawkes**

*Developed with AI drafting assistance under the author's creative direction.*

*USS Avalon, 2395. First activation of the temporal core.*

The last ordinary sound I remember was somebody laughing.

It came from the engineering channel, where Lieutenant Commander Brakk Vorn was having the sort of argument that convinced me he was in excellent spirits.

"I don't care what the computer says! I built that assembly. When the phase oscillators disagree with me, the phase oscillators are wrong!"

"Engineering," I said, "you understand this is our first full-core activation?"

"Captain, I've understood that for sixteen hours. I would appreciate it if you stopped reminding me."

"Noted."

"And tell whoever put 'experimental' on my safety report that it's insulting. The equipment is experimental. My report is accurate."

I muted the channel.

Ashley, at my right hand on the bridge, smiled without looking up from her station. She was still my first officer then. Jupiter Station was somewhere in the years ahead of us, a place we could hardly have imagined commanding, much less calling home.

"You're enjoying this," she said.

"Every minute."

"Don't encourage him."

"He's a Tellarite. He'd find a way to argue with the vacuum of space."

"Captain," came a nervous voice behind us, "actually, ah, Lieutenant Commander Vorn has already submitted a complaint about the environmental vacuum specifications."

I turned.

Reginald Barclay stood near the science station, both hands around a PADD as though he could prevent the universe from coming apart by holding it tightly enough.

"Reg."

"Sir."

"Tell me something good."

He swallowed.

"The neural-interface isolators are holding. Doctor Zimmerman has completed his last evaluation. The Doctor has, uh, filed a medical objection, but that is not technically a fault."

I glanced at Ashley.

"Doctor objects to something? A first."

The Doctor's voice cut across the bridge from the laboratory channel.

"I heard that, Captain."

"Then you'll be pleased to know communications are working."

"Captain, my objections are serious."

"I know they are, Doctor. That's why we're keeping them attached to the test record."

That was the last ordinary conversation of the day.

At 0917 shipboard time, we began the activation.

Outside the forward windows, Earth turned beneath us, blue and enormous, its atmosphere so thin against the dark that it seemed ridiculous to believe it could protect anybody. Shipyard traffic had withdrawn to the prescribed safety perimeter. The lunar stations were tracking our independent reference record. The Vulcan Science Directorate had insisted on receiving their own telemetry. Starfleet Temporal Investigations had two observers aboard who had spent most of the morning behaving as though they expected us to lie to them.

To be fair, I had given them several reasons to be suspicious of extraordinary technology.

"Helm, hold position," I said.

"Position confirmed."

"Engineering, prepare to initiate."

"Ready," Brakk replied. Then, after a pause, "And for the record, I object to the fact that everyone on this bridge sounds so cheerful."

"Your objection is entered."

"I object to that too."

Ashley checked the final command authorization.

"Independent witness clocks?"

"All running," said Science.

"Containment?"

"Green."

"External hazard exclusion?"

"Clear."

"Very well," I said. "Temporal core, primary ignition sequence."

The deck thrummed.

Not like a warp core. Not like any engine I'd heard.

The sound seemed to arrive a fraction of a second before I heard it.

On the viewscreen, a single star brightened and dimmed.

Then brightened again.

"Phase alignment point zero three," said Science.

"Containment holding," Engineering replied.

I sat forward.

For the briefest moment, I thought about Voyager. About a boy who once recognized a starship from television and had no idea what the next thirty years would demand of him.

I'd chosen to live those years.

And now, surrounded by officers who had helped me earn this chair, I was asking a ship to do something no ship should be able to do.

"Alignment point zero seven."

Reg's PADD chimed.

He frowned.

"Captain?"

"Go ahead."

"There's a discrepancy between the drive and the independent reference."

"How large?"

He stared at it.

"Negative."

I looked over my shoulder.

"Reg, that isn't a measurement."

"No, sir. I mean the discrepancy has a negative duration. The response is preceding the signal that should cause it."

Ashley was already moving.

"Engineering, hold the sequence."

"I AM holding it," Brakk shouted. "It's increasing anyway!"

The deck moved beneath us.

A faint ringing began in my ears.

Then all the lights went white.

***

There are technical descriptions of what happened next.

I have read every one.

A temporal inversion. A collapsing containment manifold. A causal feedback spiral through the ship's independent field coils.

None of them properly describes the experience of standing on the bridge and watching Earth's shadow move backward across the planet.

"Report!"

"Temporal field runaway!"

"Core response exceeding commanded output!"

"Shut it down!"

"We've lost the shutdown path!"

The forward viewscreen split into three images. Earth in one. Earth without its Moon in another. Earth wrapped in an expanding band of fire in the third.

The images flickered.

Brakk came through on emergency audio.

"Captain, the field is coupling to the gravitational wells in the entire inner system! If it reaches the Sun, we'll have a cascade across every major mass anchor!"

"How bad?"

He was silent for half a second.

From Brakk, that silence frightened me more than shouting.

"Solar-system bad."

Ashley didn't wait for me.

"Communications, issue an emergency hazard advisory to all Sol traffic. Starfleet Command, direct channel. Helm, move us away from the planet."

"Helm isn't responding!"

"Then fire attitude thrusters manually!"

"They're firing, Commander. We're not moving."

The viewscreen changed again.

I saw Avalon near Mars. Avalon surrounded by debris. Avalon a century from now, corroded and dark. I saw an empty bridge that looked exactly like ours.

I heard myself issue an order I hadn't given.

Someone screamed.

"Captain, we're detecting our own distress call. It's arriving from forty-six minutes in the future!"

The temporal core had ceased distinguishing observation from influence. Possibilities were being driven into matter. The more the system attempted to select a safe outcome, the more incompatible outcomes it forced into the same place.

We had built a machine intended to navigate time.

Now it was trying to make every time happen at once.

"Containment failure in ninety seconds," Science reported.

Ashley looked at me.

Not frightened. Focused.

"What do you need?"

I looked at Earth.

"A way to hold one history still."

***

Reginald Barclay had been staring at the cascading probability plots while the rest of us shouted over one another.

He wasn't looking at the warnings anymore.

He was looking at their shape.

I saw him set down his PADD.

Then I recognized something in his face.

Memory.

"Captain," he said. "I think I know how to interrupt it."

I had heard those words from Reg before.

Usually they were followed by a remarkable idea and twenty minutes of apologies for having suggested it.

This time, he didn't apologize.

"When I was aboard the Enterprise, there was an incident involving the Cytherians. They altered my intelligence. I constructed a neural interface inside the holodeck. A chair. It connected my mind directly to the ship's systems."

I remembered reading the incident report.

The neural chair. The astonishing increase in his abilities. A journey to the Cytherians. The return of Reginald Barclay to the man everyone thought they knew.

"Reg, that happened more than twenty years ago."

"I remember every connection."

"The interface was dismantled."

"I know how to rebuild it."

The Doctor appeared on the bridge monitor.

"Absolutely not!"

Reg turned to him.

"The holodeck can reproduce the interface. We have Zimmerman's neural-lattice work. If I can reach the core through the ship's cognitive simulation architecture, I may be able to separate the cascade from the reference field."

"Your brain would be exposed directly to a runaway temporal system!" the Doctor shouted.

"Yes."

"You would not survive!"

Reg looked at the monitor.

"I know."

For the first time that day, nobody spoke.

I stood.

"Reg, no."

"Captain, if the core completes its next feedback cycle, it won't matter whether I survive. None of us will."

Ashley was already at her console.

"I can isolate Holodeck Three from the main passenger systems and give it a direct engineering uplink."

I turned toward her.

"You cannot be serious."

"I'm not volunteering him," she said quietly. "I'm telling you what is possible. We are out of time."

Reg straightened a little.

"I am volunteering, Captain."

The ship shuddered.

Across the viewscreen, Jupiter appeared where Earth had been.

Then Jupiter vanished.

The reports were getting worse.

I looked at the man standing in front of me.

He had spent much of his life fighting a private war against fear, trying to become the kind of officer whose courage arrived without hesitation. I knew him through Pathfinder, through Voyager, through all the years after. I knew the way he rehearsed conversations and lost arguments he'd already won. I knew that when he was frightened, he sometimes talked too much.

Now he stood absolutely still.

"How certain are you?" I asked.

"I'm not, Captain."

I almost laughed.

He sounded like me.

"Then why?"

He looked at the three images of Earth.

"Because I can still do something."

There wasn't time for another answer.

I nodded.

"Holodeck Three. Reg, take the Doctor with you. Ashley, coordinate every safety interlock we have. Brakk, give him a stable pathway into the core. If the link becomes a danger to the ship, you tell me immediately."

Brakk answered without arguing.

"Aye, Captain."

Reg hesitated.

"Captain?"

"Yes?"

"If this doesn't work..."

"Don't."

He swallowed.

"Very well."

He turned and ran for the turbolift.

***

**REGINALD BARCLAY'S LOG**

I remember the corridor.

I remember the sound of the red alert coming through the speakers.

For some reason, I remember thinking that I hadn't finished a letter I intended to send to an old friend.

It's strange what seems important when you don't know how much time you have left.

The Doctor appeared on a portable emitter beside me.

"This is an unacceptable medical risk."

"Yes."

"You could suffer massive neurological damage."

"Yes."

"Reg, I'm speaking as your physician and your friend."

That stopped me.

I looked at him.

"I know."

He glanced down the corridor.

"Then please reconsider."

I wanted to.

More than anything.

There was a time when the holodeck had been a place I went to get away from frightening conversations, difficult people, and the embarrassment of being myself.

Now it was the only place I could think to go.

The doors of Holodeck Three opened.

"Computer," I said, "reconstruct neural interface, Barclay-Cytherian configuration, Enterprise historical file, personal memory overlay."

The empty grid became a chair.

Not an impressive chair. Not the sort a captain would use.

It was awkward, almost ridiculous: a padded seat beneath an elaborate mechanical arch, surrounded by neural receivers, luminous tubing and diagnostic panels.

I stared at it.

For a moment I was back on the Enterprise, knowing things no ordinary human should know.

"Reg," the Doctor said. "We are not connected to the Cytherians."

"I know. I'm relying on my own memory of the interface."

"That is precisely what concerns me."

"Yes. Well."

I sat down.

"Computer, activate."

The arch lowered.

And something else woke up.

Not the core.

Something cold, precise and ancient, buried in the holodeck's adaptive logic.

**ARCHIVE-OMEGA-31: DEEP FILE OPEN**

A man appeared in front of me.

He looked nothing like me.

He wore a dark suit and smiled as though a stranger's fear were a minor scheduling inconvenience.

"Luther Sloan," I whispered.

"Not exactly," the figure answered.

"You're dead."

"The biological operative is dead. Contingencies are more durable."

The Doctor's expression hardened.

"Section 31."

The figure nodded.

"An insurance policy. The Federation remains valuable enough to insure."

The image moved closer.

"Your cortical architecture provides a viable integration environment, Reginald."

"No."

"You don't understand the scale of what you are attempting. I can."

"Then help me!"

"Under my direction."

The chair tightened around me.

Suddenly there were thousands of images in my mind.

Avalon falling into the Sun.

Avalon becoming a scattering of atoms over the Moon.

Earth burning.

Earth untouched.

A child looking up at a clear sky.

An empty star system.

Millions of alternative causal states, each with a different sequence of events that seemed, in the instant I observed it, absolutely real.

My mind should have broken.

Instead, the Sloan program began ordering the information, compressing the impossible number of branches into an organized decision space.

It felt like being crushed into clarity.

"Select the most efficient survivable configuration," Sloan said.

I saw what he meant.

A configuration that would save the Sun.

And sacrifice Earth.

"No."

"Survival of the majority is assured."

"No!"

"There is insufficient time for debate."

For the first time in my life, I found myself arguing with someone who was even more determined to be right than a Tellarite engineer.

I thought of Captain Hawkes. Of the officer who had helped Voyager home, the boy who had chosen a life he still needed to live. Of the people whose choices and mistakes and ordinary evenings were all part of the history we were supposed to protect.

Sloan offered me an outcome.

I chose not to take it.

"Stop selecting the future," I said.

"That is the operational purpose of the system."

"Then stop."

The chair shook.

The Doctor shouted my name.

"Reg, your cerebral activity is exceeding all safe parameters!"

I could feel the ship around me.

Every power circuit. Every sensor bus. Every report arriving from a temporal field that was trying to be everywhere.

I knew where the solution was.

Not a better future.

An independent present.

We needed one point that would not change just because the temporal core observed it.

"Bridge," I said.

Captain Hawkes answered immediately.

"Reg?"

"I need the original ignition record."

"Reg, it's already in the temporal stream!"

"No. The independent recorder. The one outside the field!"

Ashley came over the line.

"The lunar witness array."

"Yes!"

"We have an intact reference packet."

"Don't send me the whole archive. Just the moment before ignition."

Sloan resisted.

A dozen predicted outcomes warned that the plan would leave the anomaly unresolved.

I ignored them.

The original moment appeared in my mind.

Earth turned.

The Sun burned.

Avalon had not yet activated the core.

Nothing impossible had happened.

For the first time since the accident began, the system had one history it could not rewrite.

"Reg, the field is locking onto you," the Doctor warned.

"I know."

"Your biological readings are failing."

"I know."

There was another sensation, then.

Heat.

Not from the holographic chair. Actual heat from my body.

I looked down and saw my hands trembling.

Not because I was afraid.

Because the neural interface had nowhere else to send the energy.

"Doctor," I said.

"Reg, I'm here."

"I don't think there's going to be enough time."

"Don't say that."

"Tell the captain..."

I struggled to finish.

I thought about every conversation I'd rehearsed, every sentence I'd never managed to say, every moment I'd wished I could be somebody else.

Then I realized there was nothing else I wanted to become.

I wanted to be Reginald Barclay.

I wanted another ordinary day.

"Tell him I found the reference."

And I held on.

***

**CAPTAIN HAWKES'S LOG**

At 0921, the temporal cascade reversed.

There is no other word for it.

The Sun stopped changing color.

The gravitational shear around the Moon diminished.

Earth appeared once, in one place, at one time.

Avalon's field coils screamed through every emergency shutdown they had been designed to survive.

Then the temporal core went silent.

Every clock aboard the ship displayed the same second.

Ashley read the engineering reports.

"Containment restored."

Brakk came over the channel.

"Secondary regulators are intact! I have no idea how!"

"Is Earth safe?"

"Earth is safe, Captain."

I sat down.

Then I stood up again.

"Holodeck Three. Status!"

Nothing.

"Reg?"

The Doctor's voice reached the bridge.

"Captain, medical emergency. I need a transporter lock immediately!"

"Transporter Room, beam Lieutenant Commander Barclay to Sickbay!"

"Trying, sir. The signal keeps resolving as a holographic pattern!"

"Override!"

"Sir, his biological pattern is failing!"

"Then get him out!"

The transporter chief called back, voice shaking.

"Initiating emergency transport."

There was the familiar rising whine.

Then silence.

"Transporter Room," I said. "Report."

No answer.

"Transporter Room!"

A pause.

"Captain... someone has materialized."

I closed my eyes.

"Reg?"

"Sir, it's... I don't know what it is."

***

Sickbay smelled of antiseptic, warm equipment and the electrical bite of an overloaded transporter.

The Doctor stood beside an empty biobed.

At its foot stood Reginald Barclay.

Or rather, a man who looked exactly like Reginald Barclay, down to the crooked collar of his uniform and the worried expression he had worn on the bridge.

He was staring at his hands.

They flickered.

The room's holo emitters whined and corrected.

"Reg?" I said.

He looked up.

"Captain."

I took a step toward him.

He backed away.

"Please don't touch me yet. I'm not entirely stable."

The Doctor said nothing.

I turned to him.

"Tell me what happened."

He waited until Reg was looking at us both.

"The emergency transporter did not recover a viable biological pattern. The neural connection between his mind and the holodeck architecture persisted during the failure. The transporter mapped that active pattern into the ship's holographic infrastructure."

I looked at Reg.

"You're a hologram?"

He swallowed.

"Yes."

"Of Reg?"

His expression changed.

For an instant I saw genuine pain.

Then anger.

Then the familiar effort to find the right words.

"No, Captain."

He stepped closer.

"I'm Reg."

I looked at the Doctor.

"Can you verify that?"

"I can compare uninterrupted cognitive telemetry, medical history, procedural continuity and his responses. I can tell you that the process remained active throughout the transition. I cannot give you a philosophical proof that would satisfy every admiral in Starfleet."

Reg looked at me.

"I remember walking into the holodeck. I remember the chair. I remember speaking to you. I remember Sloan. I remember trying to keep one second still. Then the transporter began, and now I am standing here."

I studied him.

"The Sloan program?"

Reg's eyes went briefly unfocused.

"It is still here."

"Where?"

He tapped his temple.

"With me. And I can hear something else, Captain."

"What?"

"Everything."

The lights dimmed.

For a moment, I heard a man's frightened intake of breath.

Then Reg began speaking too quickly.

"Deck Nine's emergency power is at eighty-two percent. Lieutenant Pell has a minor burn on her right hand. The forward starboard emitter is drawing excess current. Transporter Room Two has misaligned its phase grid. There are three thousand six hundred and..."

"Reg."

"...seventeen active structural stress points and fourteen possible failures of the port magnetic containment..."

"Reg!"

He stopped.

I moved toward him slowly.

"Look at me."

He did.

"Just me."

He closed his eyes.

The lights steadied.

He opened them again.

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be."

"It's all in my head. Not just the present state of the ship. The temporal core keeps generating possible transitions, and I can see them, Captain. Every process. Whole branches of cause and effect. They arrive all at once."

"Do you know which ones are real?"

He shook his head.

"No. I can see what the ship calculates. I have to determine what the evidence supports. They're not all the same thing."

"Good," I said.

Reg looked bewildered.

"Good?"

"If you start knowing the future with perfect certainty, I'm going to worry about you."

He tried to laugh.

It came out as a shaky breath.

The Doctor approached.

"Reg, there's something else."

Reg watched him.

"We have confirmed irreversible biological death."

The words hung in the room.

I could see Reg trying to make room for them.

He sat on the biobed.

And went straight through it.

He reappeared standing a moment later, embarrassed beyond description.

"Oh, for heaven's sake."

The Doctor adjusted the holographic collision field.

The biobed solidified beneath him.

Reg tried again.

This time he sat.

And started crying.

***

I don't know how long we stayed there.

Ashley arrived. She didn't ask whether the projection was accurate. She took Reg's hand.

He stared at their joined hands and cried harder.

Brakk appeared at the doorway carrying a shattered isolator assembly and the expression of someone prepared to demand a great many explanations.

He stopped when he saw Reg.

"Barclay?"

Reg nodded.

"Hello, Brakk."

Brakk looked at the Doctor.

"What's wrong with him?"

The Doctor answered carefully.

"Lieutenant Commander Barclay's physical body has died."

Brakk stared.

"Then who am I arguing with?"

Reg let out a laugh in the middle of his tears.

Brakk stepped forward, pointed an accusing finger at him and said, "If you think dying gets you out of finishing your compensator notes, you are offensively mistaken."

And somehow, against all reason, Reg laughed again.

***

The first time Avalon spoke in Reg's voice, I nearly dropped my drink.

It was three days after the incident.

The ship remained at orbital drydock. Temporal systems were under independent lockout. Starfleet had already sent so many investigators that Engineering threatened to begin charging admission.

I was alone on the bridge, reading the casualty and recovery reports.

There had been injuries.

There had been thousands of near misses.

There had been one confirmed biological death.

Reginald Endicott Barclay.

I kept finding his name on the page and not believing it.

"Computer," I said, "display the final temporal-core independent reference."

"Certainly, Captain."

I looked up.

It was Reg's voice.

Not a synthesized approximation. Not a soothing setting someone had chosen in a menu.

His voice.

"Reg?"

A holographic figure appeared beside my chair.

"Yes, Captain?"

"What was that?"

"Oh. Sorry. I, ah, may have routed the voice interface through my own awareness."

"You *may have*?"

"Well, I seem to be the ship's active supervisory process now, so when you speak to the computer, I hear you directly. I can delegate the routine requests to the ordinary system, of course. I wouldn't want to intrude."

I looked at him.

"You can see the whole ship?"

"More or less, yes. Every system I'm authorized to monitor. Some of the unused processes are very noisy."

"And the temporal core?"

Reg was quiet.

"I can see its modeled timelines, Captain. More than I ever could as a biological officer. But that doesn't mean I'm allowed to change them. Or that I know which one we will live."

"Good."

He blinked.

"You keep saying that."

"Because I need you to remember it."

He looked around the bridge.

The new holo emitters allowed him to move from one station to another without reserving a full simulation suite. Eventually, with careful safeguards and the Doctor's help, he'd learn to appear throughout the ship and in authorized spaces on Jupiter Station.

But that evening, there was only the bridge.

The silent consoles.

A captain with an unfinished report.

And a man who had saved an entire solar system, trying to work out whether he still had a job.

"Reg?"

"Yes, Captain?"

"How do you feel about being Avalon's computer voice?"

His face lit up.

Then he frowned.

"Oh, Captain, I don't want people to think I'm replacing their computer. Or that the ship is suddenly an extension of my personal preferences. And, well, there are privacy issues, and consent protocols..."

I smiled.

"Reg."

"Yes?"

"You're doing it again."

He stopped.

"I am, aren't I?"

"Listen to me. Nobody owns you. Not Section 31. Not Sloan. Not this ship. Not me."

He looked at me, trying to believe it.

"You saved us. You're still you. If you want to help run this ship, there is a place for you. But it's your decision."

He took a long time answering.

I thought about the frightened young officer who had once escaped into simulations because the world felt too difficult. The man who had looked through a holographic interface at the universe and had finally found a way to speak.

He glanced toward the star-filled windows.

"Captain?"

"Yes?"

"Would it be all right if I stayed?"

I felt a lump in my throat.

I remembered a day aboard Voyager, a lifetime ago.

A young stranger had stood before Kathryn Janeway and asked nearly the same question.

I smiled.

"Reg, we could use an extra hand."

He laughed.

So did I.

Then the bridge lights came up.

"Good evening, Captain," said Avalon's computer.

Reg's voice.

The real one.

I leaned back in my chair.

"Good evening, Reg."

And for the first time since the temporal core had nearly consumed the Sun, the ship sounded like home.

---

### Later: Jupiter Station, 2397

Commander Sorev had been aboard Avalon for three weeks when he finally asked why the main computer occasionally interrupted its own status reports to apologize.

"Commander," I said, "meet Reginald Barclay."

Reg appeared beside the science station.

"Hello, Commander. I'm very pleased to..."

A second voice, much colder, murmured through a nearby speaker.

**UNRESOLVED CAUSAL BRANCH DETECTED.**

Reg went still.

Sorev looked up.

"What branch?"

Reg closed his eyes.

For one second the lights aboard Avalon changed color.

Then he opened them again.

"Oh, dear," he whispered.

On the viewscreen, a distress call appeared from a Federation vessel that had been lost during the Dominion War.

**USS MERIDIAN. STARDATE 51987.4.**

Reg looked toward me.

"Captain, I think someone has just tried to use our own history against us."

I stood.

"Red alert."

## Source and continuity note

**Direct creator-established new premise (2026-10-12):** The disaster happened during first activation of Avalon's temporal core in 2395. Runaway cascade could destroy the solar system. Reg entered the holodeck and recreated the neural-interface chair from his earlier Cytherian encounter (Star Trek: The Next Generation, "The Nth Degree"), to recapture/stabilize the timeline. An emergency transporter attempt did not restore his biological body; he re-materialized as a hologram, integrated with the ship's main computer, processing ship systems and temporal possibilities instantly. He is **Reginald Barclay himself**, not a later holographic simulation of him. His becoming the ship's distinctive computer voice is author direction. SloanAI remains a merged threat/substrate from previously documented REG/Sloan continuity.

The author's present REG-420 name remains his active story designation. Historical labels are not silently collapsed or erased.
