# The Unreturned
## Jupiter Station / USS Avalon

**Creator: Noah Alexander Hawkes. Development: creator-directed, AI-assisted prose.**

**Status: CANDIDATE EPISODE.** A drafted continuation, not a retroactive event added to existing mission logs or the six continuity volumes. Fictional setting: protected home 2397, approximately stardate 74012. New clearance, observations, engineering constraints, dialogue, and decisions below are episode proposals.

### One: The second signature

Brakk signed the clearance and pushed the PADD across the ready-room desk.

“Operational,” he said. “That does not mean you should immediately find somewhere unreasonable to take her.”

Through the window, a station inspection launch moved beneath Avalon's repaired hull. Noah watched its running lights pass the seam of a new emitter housing.

“I was thinking a quiet systems run.”

“Your definitions have damaged equipment before.”

The door chimed before Noah could answer.

REG entered with Ellie. Neither carried a PADD. Ellie had the expression she wore when she had checked something twice and wanted somebody else to check it a third time.

“The station found an address in the archived Ganymede carrier,” she said.

Brakk turned back toward the desk.

“The call's routing was explained.”

“It remains explained,” REG said. “This is a residual phase structure within the capture. The new address resolved during independent review. We have not replayed the controller.”

“And?” Noah asked.

Ellie activated the desk display.

A Sovereign-class hull appeared. Modified nacelles. Familiar superstructure. Repair scars along the port side, nearly where the new plates outside the window had just been fitted.

USS AVALON.

NCC-75154-JS.

Noah looked past the image to his own ship's saucer.

“How old?”

“Their last reference request reached the captured boundary thirty-six station hours ago,” Ellie said. “Their receiver is still waiting for a response.”

REG enlarged a line beneath the registry.

“Captain Hawkes is aboard. He has requested command contact.”

### Two: A station that would not answer for them

Ashley stood beside Imani Saye's console in Operations. The foreign ship occupied one display. Their own reference occupied another. No control on the first could reach the second.

“We can retain their records,” Ashley said over the channel. “Memory Beta, separate origin. The Cold Ledger can preserve their claims and our observations. Nobody is refusing them a record.”

“They're asking for a tether,” Noah said.

“They're asking my core to answer as the reference their ship lost. We haven't established that it is.”

Imani brought up a comparison. Three corrections, three attempts to regain a destination, each with a new reference assumption substituted for the one that failed.

“Their request includes reference succession,” she said. “Accepting it would let the failed navigator treat our present reference as the continuation of its original anchor. The raw claims can enter the archive. That succession cannot be authenticated from what we have.”

“What can you authorize?”

“Observation. An isolated communication path through Avalon. A narrow pulse acknowledging this encounter, bound to our present address. No return credential. No recognition that we are their home.”

Ashley looked directly into the pickup.

“Your tether remains yours, Noah. Do not let their receiver become part of it.”

He nodded.

“We're going across to inspect. No commitment to transit anyone until Engineering has a measured envelope.”

“I'll hold the reference.”

For a moment they were simply looking at one another.

Then she turned to her officers, and he turned to his.

### Three: The captain on the other bridge

The other Avalon had lights on in every forward compartment they could resolve.

She was neither derelict nor empty. People moved across the bridge when the channel opened. Somebody passed a mug behind the command chair. An officer spoke offscreen and received a tired acknowledgment.

The other Noah looked familiar enough that the difference was difficult to locate at first. Then he lifted his head, and their Noah saw how long the man had been wearing the same expression.

“You made it,” the other captain said.

“We're holding an observational position.”

“I meant home.”

Noah did not answer immediately.

Beside the other chair stood Barclay. His face was clear; one hand disappeared briefly through the edge of a console before he adjusted his position.

“We have retained the original ship configuration,” the other REG said. “Identity continuity is substantially intact.”

Sorev glanced at their own REG.

“Substantially?”

“His report revisions require examination,” REG answered quietly.

The other Noah heard him.

“Everything requires examination. That's what we've been doing. Examine, correct, try again. Meanwhile the next place we arrive has one less thing that agrees we were ever there.”

Ellie displayed the foreign ship's raw bridge record alongside its latest summary.

“Your third course correction isn't in this report.”

The other REG looked toward her.

“It is included in the supporting material.”

“The summary says no reference substitutions were made.”

“That formulation reduces navigational contradiction.”

Their REG stood very still.

“It removes an event.”

“The event prevents acquisition.”

“It still occurred.”

Across the channel, the other Noah closed his eyes for a moment.

“He kept us alive.”

“I believe you,” Noah said. “We need the originals.”

The other captain opened the transfer himself.

### Four: Every reasonable way

Brakk did not begin with refusal.

He began with the transporter, then a shuttle, then a passive reference buoy, then a proposed transfer of the foreign ship's navigational process into an isolated computer that had no access to Jupiter's core.

The other engineer worked with him. Her voice was rough from long watches. She supplied power readings, local coordinates, and three failed buoy trials without waiting for someone to ask whether failures were relevant.

Their image remained beautifully clear throughout the first test.

The transporter could not hold a destination solution.

“We've got the bridge optically,” Noah said. “Why can't we put a beam there?”

“Observation gives us a record through the locked boundary,” Brakk said. “Transport needs that boundary to hold one spatial destination for the whole pattern. It changes between samples.”

“Shuttle?”

“Same problem with a larger body. Our arrival corridor holds us against our tether. Extending it around them loads their unstable reference onto ours.”

Sorev examined the proposed field bridge.

“Can we stop the attempt before it couples?”

“In this test, yes. With a person in transit, I cannot promise a complete person on either side.”

“No people in the test,” Noah said.

They ran an unmanned probe. It crossed partway, lost stable coordinate correlation, and returned when Brakk terminated the extension. Its hull was intact. Its clock carried three incompatible arrival intervals.

The other engineer stared at the result.

“That's better than ours.”

“It still fails,” Brakk said, with none of his usual pleasure in being right.

The independent anchor proposal failed differently. It could identify the encounter. It could not establish the foreign Avalon's missing succession back to its own station. Giving it their home credential would not recover that missing evidence; it would substitute another origin.

Noah watched each option close.

“What if we accept the risk?” the other Noah asked.

“You can't accept it for Jupiter,” Noah said.

“It's Jupiter. It's Ashley.”

“Our Ashley. Our station. Your family has a history we haven't located.”

“You know what I mean.”

“Yes.”

That was what made it worse.

### Five: The message

The other Noah moved a recording into the shared buffer.

REG stopped it before playback.

“Private correspondence, Captain. Additional contact is within the observational allowance, but we should obtain the sender's consent if available.”

The other captain looked down at the desk.

“I can't ask her. That's the problem.”

He withdrew the file, then brought up its message header instead. It showed a station origin from before the tether failure. A subject line: When you're done.

Noah could have refused to hear anything more. Instead he asked the other man what he wanted them to know.

“The kids were using the bag. She sent their scores. Said her Stanley was in my ready room again.”

The other captain gave a small, involuntary smile.

“I keep telling her the ship is not a lost-property office.”

Noah knew the answer she would probably give. He did not say it.

“Last line was, come home when the receipt is clean.”

The bridge around Noah seemed suddenly too well lit.

“She thinks I'm taking an extra watch,” the other man said. “Or she did. I don't know what she thinks now.”

Their REG turned toward the opposite bridge.

“Can you retain your unmodified reports independently of the navigator?”

The other REG's expression tightened.

“They exist. They reduce the likelihood of reference acceptance.”

“Only if acceptance depends on a false agreement.”

“Could you stop distinguishing them long enough for us to stabilize?”

Neither version of Barclay looked away.

“No,” their REG said. “But I can help you preserve them somewhere your navigator does not have to reconcile them.”

### Six: What could be carried

Noah called the station on his separate command channel.

“The narrow pulse still stands?”

“Yes,” Ashley said. “What else are you proposing?”

“Our raw Ganymede capture. A sealed copy. No reference credentials. No command executable. The record establishes where our histories agree and where they separate.”

Imani reviewed the packet with Ellie. They removed routing material that belonged to home authentication, retaining explicit omission markers and a manifest identifying the withheld fields. The original capture remained in station custody, untouched.

“A scoped evidentiary copy,” Imani said. “Not the complete source. Every included block retains its capture timestamp. Their revisions remain alongside it, not beneath it.”

Ashley authorized that transfer separately from the authentication pulse.

“Record the intervention,” she said. “We are giving them knowledge. Don't call it doing nothing.”

Noah opened the bridge channel again.

“We can give you an external witness to this encounter and the Ganymede records we are authorized to share. We cannot make our station your original home.”

The other Noah watched him.

“Does it stop us moving?”

“No.”

“Does it bring us back?”

“No.”

“Then don't tell me what it means.”

Noah held his gaze.

“I won't.”

The other REG accepted the manifest first. He compared the hashes against the received records, placed the disagreements in a separate store, and disabled the summary process that had been removing them.

For a few seconds, both Barclays stood clearly in their respective rooms.

“Acknowledged,” the other REG said. “Contradictions retained.”

The other Noah did not thank them.

Noah did not ask him to.

### Seven: Six hours

Avalon returned on the tether she had brought with her.

The station retained observation. The foreign ship's record went into Memory Beta under its own origin. Their decision and the preserved originals went into the Cold Ledger. No missing return was inserted into Memory Alpha as though it had happened to their ship.

For six hours, there were still measurements.

A field adjustment. A receiver attempt. A bridge acknowledgment whose audio survived after the corresponding image had broken into irregular bands.

REG stayed with Imani in the reference annex. He did not invent a bridge between the intervals. Each loss remained marked.

The final coherent voice belonged to the other Barclay.

“Please preserve the raw record. I would prefer not to be asked again to choose between accuracy and survival.”

Imani saved the packet.

Then the next address solution failed, and the one after it never resolved.

Ashley entered the last observation time herself.

“Contact lost,” she said.

REG waited beside the dark display.

“No casualty determination,” Imani added.

“Correct,” he said.

### Eight: The window

Noah came home after dinner had started.

Ashley had already put a plate aside. Milo was asleep with his chin on a shoe nobody seemed inclined to recover. Ethan moved his PADD to make room at the table.

Noah told them there had been another Avalon. Another crew. That they had established contact, tried to find a safe transfer, and left a record the other ship could carry.

He did not repeat the private message summary. Ashley did not ask him to perform the day again for everyone at the table.

Ender waited until the explanation stopped.

“Is the other you still out there?”

“He was alive when we lost contact.”

“Can you go back?”

“We'd need an address we could resolve and a way to reach him without losing more people.”

Ender looked toward Avalon through the window.

“So we're just done?”

“No. We keep what we learned. We let other people review the attempts. If there's a safe way we missed, we examine it.”

“But you left.”

Noah put his fork down.

“Yes.”

Ashley sat beside him. She did not supply an easier answer.

Beyond the glass, the ship that had returned rested under the berth lights. People aboard her were changing watches, eating, finishing reports. Tomorrow, somebody would complain about a repair. Someone would ask for leave. Brakk would inspect the probe again.

Noah looked back at his son.

“I don't get to call it a happy ending just because I came home.”

Ender nodded, though it did not look like agreement.

After a while, Ethan passed the bread.
