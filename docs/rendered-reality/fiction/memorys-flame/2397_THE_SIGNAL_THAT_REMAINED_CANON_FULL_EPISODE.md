# PART ONE: THE CADENCE THAT CHANGED

## Chapter One: The Quiet Ring

I had been called many things in my life, and in my life afterward.

Engineer. Coward. Genius. Friend. An emergency.

A particularly unpleasant Section 31 contingency once referred to me as an integration environment. I considered that its worst offense, though the attempt to sacrifice Earth came close.

For the three days following our return to Jupiter Station, however, I had settled into a more familiar occupation.

I was a man trying to sleep while the ship was making noises.

Sleep was not strictly required by my current architecture. Rest intervals were. Some of the old biological habits were useful enough that I retained them voluntarily. The Doctor believed this was a healthy concession to the continuity of ordinary life. Brakk believed it was evidence that my sleep algorithms required a better engineer.

At 0421 shipboard time, I abandoned the attempt.

The optical feed from Jupiter remained muted. The chronometric tether was still physically isolated. The hull had been patched after our defense of the station, but the replacement port sensor boom had not arrived. Our deflector would take another forty hours of testing before Brakk would certify it for full operation.

Jupiter filled the observation window beyond my quarters. From here, I could see the great cloud bands sliding past the station's lights. The atmosphere looked almost tranquil, though I knew that beneath the visible clouds were pressures and winds capable of dismantling a starship.

Somewhere inside those storms, an ancient-looking ring continued to transmit.

Three pulses.

A pause.

Four.

Every eleven seconds, more or less.

That was how the final record of our last mission described it.

The description was already becoming obsolete.

“Commander?”

Merlin's voice came through my private duty channel.

“Ensign. Is there an emergency?”

“I don't know yet, sir. That's why I'm asking you to come to the bridge.”

Something about his tone made me sit up.

He was not frightened.

He was trying very hard not to sound excited.

“I'll be there.”

When I materialized beside the bridge's starboard attestation console, Merlin had arranged six signal traces in chronological order.

His uniform was immaculate. There was an untouched cup of coffee beside his station. He had placed a handwritten note beneath the cup.

*Do not mistake regularity for origin.*

I smiled.

“I see you've been taking our conversations seriously.”

“Commander, I'd like to think I always do.”

“Then tell me why you're keeping the night watch company.”

He pointed to the traces.

“It's not repeating every eleven seconds.”

“Not anymore?”

“Not consistently.”

The first waveform arrived at 0420:18.

The next arrived eleven point zero three seconds later.

The third arrived nine point four seconds after that.

The fourth, eleven point zero three.

The fifth, nine point four.

The same alternation repeated across the observation interval.

Merlin looked at me.

“Eleven point zero three. Nine point four. Eleven point zero three. Nine point four. We've been watching an alternating signal and calling it the old eleven-second pattern because the software averaged the readings.”

“And the older reporting interval was eleven point zero three?”

“In the newer local copies, yes. The station's independent receiver has a different timing record. I haven't reconciled the sources.”

I leaned over his screen.

A second detail was more interesting than the first.

At the end of the third pulse, the tiny secondary fluctuation Sorev and Merlin had identified during the previous episode grew stronger during the nine-point-four-second interval. It weakened during the eleven-point-zero-three-second interval.

The change did not occur in every observation with equal strength.

But its tendency was visible.

“Is the feature physically measured or inferred by the reconstruction software?”

“Measured. I've kept the raw values alongside the plot.”

“Independent observers?”

“Lieutenant Sorev has the data, blinded to my preferred interpretation. Jupiter Station has its own readings. I haven't requested their conclusions yet.”

“Good.”

I studied the pattern again.

Eleven point zero three.

Nine point four.

A longer pause followed by a shorter one, as if two clocks were arguing over who had the right to end a sentence.

“Merlin, how long have you known?”

“Seven minutes.”

“And why aren't you celebrating?”

“Because a change in the signal may be important, and I don't yet know whether the sensors changed instead.”

I regarded him with sincere approval.

“An excellent reason.”

He smiled.

For one of the first times since we'd begun working together, I saw that he no longer needed to be told why a qualification mattered. He was beginning to find his own evidence discipline.

The turbolift opened.

Ellie Hawkes emerged carrying a portable optical record display. Her strawberry-blond hair was pulled loosely behind one shoulder, her green eyes narrow with concentration.

“Reg, I was about to call you.”

“You may have to take a number, Lieutenant. The ensign got here first.”

She smiled, but there was little humor in it.

“The quarantined mast module is resonating.”

I turned toward her.

“What do you mean by resonating?”

“The black material we found in the old sensor housing. It isn't transmitting data. It isn't connected to any active station power circuit. But its physical displacement is cycling with the nine-point-four-second period.”

“Measured by whom?”

“Imani's isolated optical cameras and a passive mechanical strain gauge installed under Captain Ashley's authorization. Both show the same period. I'm not treating the signal as a command, but the material is moving.”

Merlin sat up.

“Nine point four.”

“Exactly.”

“Not eleven point zero three?”

“Not within the instruments' current precision.”

I looked from one officer to the other.

The previous mission had taught us not to trust a familiar name, a plausible timestamp or a voice that knew too much.

Now an inert fragment of black material was moving to a rhythm the ship had not been listening for.

“Open an independent channel to Captain Hawkes,” I said. “And advise Sorev that we need a physical explanation before anyone touches the station module.”

Noah answered from his quarters.

“Reg? It's not even breakfast.”

“I apologize, Captain.”

“That sounded serious. How serious?”

“The outer signal has developed an alternating cadence, and a quarantined component aboard Jupiter Station is moving in time with the shorter interval.”

There was silence.

Then I heard him speak away from the communicator.

“Ashley? You may want to hear this.”

A moment later the Captain returned.

“No automatic tether reconnection. No remote activation. You have my permission to prepare a minimal probe. Ellie and Brakk can build it, if Ashley approves the observation corridor.”

“Aye, Captain.”

“And Reg?”

“Sir?”

“Talk to the biological Barclay again. He may remember the original knock differently now that we've stopped asking him to finish our sentences.”

I looked toward the display.

Nine point four seconds.

“Understood.”

“And don't wake the Doctor unless you have to.”

I considered that instruction.

“Captain, I'm afraid that may be unavoidable.”

Noah sighed.

“Then good luck.”

I closed the channel.

Merlin made another note beside the original report.

**OBSERVED:** Alternating intervals on independent available recordings.  
**INFERRED:** Two clocks, or a single changing oscillator.  
**UNRESOLVED:** Source, mechanism, intention.

Then he added one line in smaller type:

*Captain contacted before breakfast. No casualties.*

I laughed.

And he logged that too.

## Chapter Two: Knocking

The man in quarantine had my old hands.

That was how I thought of them.

The long fingers, the habit of touching one fingertip to another while considering a problem, the small scar along the right knuckle from an accident aboard the Enterprise that I had almost forgotten until I saw it again.

He was not necessarily my original body. Medical had not established his historical origin, and neither of us had the right to settle the question simply by looking at one another.

Still, I recognized the hands.

They were resting on the table when I entered the interview room at Jupiter Station Medical.

Voyager's Doctor stood beside the door.

“You are late,” he announced.

“By forty-one seconds.”

“And you have come to interview a patient who spent a substantial portion of yesterday answering questions about his own memories. I will not tolerate a repeat of the ordeal.”

“I came to listen, Doctor.”

“An admirable improvement. I shall record it as a clinical milestone.”

The biological Barclay looked up.

He smiled when he saw me.

“Reg.”

“Good morning.”

He glanced toward Merlin, who had arrived carrying a disconnected recorder with an optical timebase.

“You brought a witness.”

“An attestation officer,” I said. “Only with your permission. You may stop the recording at any time, and the Doctor can stop the interview.”

The man considered that.

“All right. But I don't want another test.”

“No test,” the Doctor said sharply.

Merlin placed his recording station on a stand near the door.

“Origin: medical interview room. Source: direct testimony. Status: unverified as historical fact.”

The biological Barclay almost laughed.

“You say that as though I were a weather report.”

Merlin looked appalled.

“I meant no disrespect.”

“I know. I'm teasing you.”

I sat opposite him.

“No particular answer is required,” I said. “Tell me what the knocking meant to you before anyone showed you the recent recordings.”

He looked at his hands.

“The first thing I remember is that it wasn't a voice.”

I waited.

“Not the kind of knocking people use at a door, either. It was softer. Three little impacts against the chair housing. A pause. Then four.”

“Could you locate the sound?”

“Above my head. From the chair.”

“Did it frighten you?”

“At first I thought one of the interface arms was loose. I was already worried about the resonance in the temporal core. So I opened the housing.”

The Doctor shifted slightly, but did not interrupt.

“I found a seventh connector. It wasn't supposed to be there. I knew the design. I had checked it myself.”

“Do you remember when that happened?”

The man closed his eyes.

“Before the activation. I think before. But every time I say that, I can also remember the emergency alarms, and the way the chair was shaking when I climbed into it.”

I nodded.

“Continue.”

“I touched the connector.”

His fingers tightened around the edge of the table.

“It was warm.”

“How warm?”

“Too warm for a disconnected ceramic fitting. I remember drawing my hand back.”

“And then?”

“I removed it.”

He stared at me.

“I was certain it was wrong. I told myself I would bring it to Brakk after the test.”

“Did you?”

“No. Because when I held it, it went cold.”

“How quickly?”

“Immediately. Like something had taken its heat away. I put it back.”

I waited.

“Why?”

His eyes shone with frustration.

“I don't know. I remember thinking that if it was cold, I'd broken something. That the connection mattered. I remember wanting to restore it.”

He looked at the Doctor.

“Both memories are there. Taking it out because it was warm. Putting it back because it was cold. I can feel both choices as if I made them one after the other. But I can't tell you whether there was time for both.”

The Doctor approached the table.

“It's all right to say that.”

The man drew a shaky breath.

“Every person who comes into this room wants one of those memories to be the answer.”

I began to speak.

He held up a hand.

“Not you. Not today.”

Then his voice grew steadier.

“I don't want to be proof. I want to be a person who gets to be confused.”

No one answered for several seconds.

I thought of my own survival, the confidence with which others had sometimes explained it, and the humiliating experience of being told what my memories must prove about the nature of existence.

I had once spent years trying to become a man whose presence never made anyone uncomfortable.

Now I watched someone wearing my old face asking for the right not to make the rest of us comfortable.

“You have that right,” I said.

The Doctor nodded.

“You do. And you don't need a court ruling from the temporal research department to exercise it.”

The man laughed softly.

“Thank you.”

Merlin lowered his stylus.

He did not write *Barclay removed the connector in 2395*.

He wrote:

**TESTIMONY:** Patient reports three soft impacts, pause, four; warmth before removal; cold before replacement. Chronology is uncertain and may have been influenced by later exposure to related material.  
**STATUS:** Not independently verified. Interview paused at patient's request.

The biological Barclay looked at the note.

“That's what I said?”

“Substantially, sir. I can preserve your exact words if you'd like.”

He shook his head.

“It's enough.”

I rose.

“Would you like another conversation?”

He considered the question.

“Yes. But next time, let's talk about something that has nothing to do with the chair.”

I smiled.

“An excellent idea.”

“Maybe the Enterprise.”

“Which mission?”

He looked at me.

“The ones where everything went wrong, but nobody tried to make me answer for the universe.”

The Doctor groaned.

“Reginald, I'm afraid that's going to be a short list.”

We laughed.

And this time Merlin closed the recorder before the laughter ended.

Some moments deserved to belong to the people in the room.

## Chapter Three: Sparrow

Brakk had not forgiven me for the destruction of Ghost One.

Nor Lark's port wing.

Nor the sensor boom.

He had a tendency to announce the total number of lost components as though someone in Starfleet Accounting might be moved to intervene.

“Three destroyed probes, one ruined shuttle wing, an industrial tug and an entire docking assembly,” he said as I entered Auxiliary Bay Two. “Now you want to launch something into the same atmosphere.”

“Chief, one of those was a station tug, and I didn't personally destroy all the others.”

“Then you should be proud of your delegation.”

On the workbench between us sat a small reconnaissance instrument with a narrow protective shell and a cluster of delicate external sensors.

It was scarcely larger than the equipment carried by Ghost One.

Ellie had designed its data systems.

Brakk had designed everything else, then objected to every gram Ellie removed.

“What are you calling it?” I asked.

“Sparrow.”

“Why?”

“Because it's small, expendable, and I'd prefer it not develop the habits of the last vessel I named after you.”

I looked toward Ellie.

“Am I being insulted?”

“Only in the affectionate Tellarite sense.”

“Very reassuring.”

Sparrow was not printed with a name or a registry. The designation existed only in our internal mission log. No part of the probe would present the designation to an external receiver.

It had no trusted identity.

No authorization certificate.

No command receiver.

No program capable of interpreting incoming symbols as instructions.

Its clock would count physical intervals.

Its magnetometer would measure the direction and strength of nearby fields.

And Ellie had added something I hadn't expected.

An acoustic contact sensor.

A tiny, heavily isolated transducer coupled to the probe's shell.

“In a gas giant?” I asked.

Ellie nodded.

“We've treated the pulses as electromagnetic signals. But the biological Barclay described feeling them as impacts on the chair housing. I want to know whether there's a mechanical pressure component in the atmosphere.”

“Very good.”

Sorev arrived carrying a fresh environmental model.

“An appropriate addition. Pressure waves can travel through the atmosphere even when their ultimate cause is electromagnetic.”

“Or,” Ellie said, “the old coupling could have converted an electromagnetic oscillation into mechanical vibration.”

“Also possible.”

Brakk leaned against his workbench.

“Or somebody built a very expensive knocking machine.”

Sorev considered that.

“A speculative but accurately described functional category.”

I laughed.

Our Captain entered just in time to hear it.

“Please tell me the bird is ready.”

Brakk pointed at Sparrow.

“Ready to be sacrificed to your scientific curiosity, Captain.”

Noah inspected the instrument.

“Can it return?”

“Possibly, if we don't insist on flying it into an opening with hostile appendages.”

“Then don't.”

The Captain turned toward Ellie.

“Any chance this thing could start thinking it's an ensign?”

“None. It has no general-purpose cognition, no identity record and no way to receive a promotion order.”

Merlin looked up from the nearby attestation terminal.

“An unfortunate limitation.”

“Ensign,” I said, “don't give Brakk ideas.”

Noah opened an optical channel to Jupiter Station.

Ashley appeared with Tessa zh'Renn and Imani Saye behind her.

“Captain,” he said, “request a passive probe corridor to the Jovian atmospheric disturbance.”

Ashley examined the trajectory.

“No proximity to the quarantined mast?”

“None.”

“No reactivation of the tether?”

“No.”

“No remote command pathway?”

Ellie stepped forward.

“None, Mom.”

Ashley regarded her daughter.

“Good. Imani, establish a separate station observation copy. Commander zh'Renn, clear civilian traffic from the projected corridor.”

Tessa nodded.

“Already underway.”

Ashley faced Noah.

“Your window opens in four minutes. Do not let the mission software decide to improve your flight plan.”

Noah smiled.

“Apparently everyone's read the last incident report.”

“Some of us wrote it.”

He nodded.

“Thank you, Captain.”

The channel closed.

We launched Sparrow on a shallow approach.

Its optical transmission was plain enough to fit on a child's classroom display: altitude, temperature, pressure, magnetic field, local acceleration, elapsed time.

The camera showed the upper clouds of Jupiter turning in slow, violent sheets. Lightning moved between towering formations. A pale storm bank filled the image, then parted around the still-air region where Ghost One had vanished.

Sparrow crossed into the edge of the quiet circle.

The atmosphere fell still.

The pressure sensor registered a low disturbance.

The pulse was mechanical.

Not loud.

Not even particularly strong.

But its interval was precise.

Three low impacts.

A pause.

Four.

And then, nine point four seconds later, the pressure pattern repeated in reverse.

Not as a phrase spoken backward.

As a wave returning through the atmosphere, its smaller features inverted by reflection.

Sorev leaned toward his console.

“Interesting.”

Merlin immediately looked at him.

“Lieutenant, when you say that, I start worrying.”

“The outgoing wave and the returning disturbance exhibit a lag consistent with a propagation path through Jupiter's deeper atmospheric layers.”

“Then it isn't simply repeating,” I said.

“No. The field structure appears to be measuring the interval between transmission and return.”

“Ranging,” Ellie said.

Sorev inclined his head.

“An appropriate description. It is measuring distance or propagation delay through the medium.”

Merlin watched the waveform.

“Then maybe it's trying to determine whether anyone is still there to hear it.”

I looked toward him.

“That is a possible intention, Ensign. The measured return does not establish the sender's purpose.”

He nodded.

“I know. I'm putting it in the margin, not the finding.”

The Captain stood beside us.

“Can we tell what it was ranging?”

Sorev brought up the acoustic map.

A broad set of possible reflections came from deep within Jupiter's atmosphere. The strongest return came from a region that did not correspond to a conventional cloud interface in our models.

The feature moved.

Slowly.

With the planet.

Or perhaps with something inside it.

Sparrow's acoustic sensor recorded another pulse.

Nine point four seconds.

Then an eleven-point-zero-three-second interval.

The longer timing displaced the return slightly, as though a second clock had stepped into the middle of the measurement.

Ellie brought up the mast trace.

The black material resonated during the shorter gap.

Never the longer.

For the first time, I began to see two systems where we had believed there was one.

One measuring a physical return through Jupiter.

Another imposing a schedule on the measurement.

One listening.

The other telling someone what to believe.

“Commander,” Merlin said, “shall I log a two-clock hypothesis?”

“Please do.”

“And which clock is the original?”

“We don't know yet.”

He looked toward Sparrow's returning wave.

“No,” he said. “But I think we know how to ask.”

The probe continued listening, its isolated recorder filling with the quiet pressure of Jupiter's upper atmosphere.

Above us, Jupiter Station remained lit and inhabited.

Its quarantined black material stirred with the shorter interval.

And somewhere beneath its oldest reference vault, something that had been silent for two years began to warm.

# PART TWO: THE ROOT

## Chapter Four: The Station's Core

There are places aboard a starship that sound like their names.

The engine room hums like power. The bridge has the familiar murmur of decisions taking shape. Sickbay has a quieter sound, punctuated by the clinical instruments of people trying very hard to keep other people alive.

Jupiter Station's central reference vault sounded like nothing.

We arrived by a personnel lift that descended through the station's central spine, past levels I had never visited. Captain Ashley Hawkes stood at the front with a physical access key in her hand. Captain Noah Hawkes walked beside me. Imani Saye followed with a custody case, and Tessa zh'Renn brought up the rear with station security.

No automatic identification opened the lower doors.

Ashley placed her key in a recessed lock.

Imani verified the seal number against her physical ledger.

A second key, held by Tessa, released the outer interlock.

The door turned inward on hinges so heavily balanced that the motion made no sound.

Beyond it lay a long chamber, lit by narrow bands of amber light.

It was built around a cylinder of pale composite material, broad enough to contain a small starship's engineering compartment, supported by enormous buttresses rising from the floor into the darkness above. Three tall clock housings stood around the central structure, each within its own sealed enclosure.

Behind thick glass, rows of tiny status lamps flashed without a shared rhythm.

Not a warp core.

Not a reactor.

A cathedral of time.

Noah stopped beside the threshold.

“Wow.”

Ashley looked at him.

“That is a better first impression than the last inspection team gave me.”

“What did they say?”

“Why isn't any of this automated?”

I watched the clock housings.

“Because automation makes it tempting to let a system decide which clock is right.”

Imani nodded.

“This vault was designed to keep clocks that can disagree. Three independent atomic references, each with a separate environmental enclosure and physical custody record. The original reference chamber was commissioned in 2350, before the present station took its full form. Later construction incorporated the old Jovian observatory into the central spine.”

She led us along a narrow walkway.

“The chronometers are not identical, Commander. Their calibration histories differ, deliberately. Every adjustment is recorded. No single control system can rewrite all three.”

“Why so much shielding?” Noah asked.

“Reference integrity. The housings are separated by meters of layered composite. Electromagnetic isolation, thermal stability, mechanical isolation, and protection against localized damage. The vault is an independent witness precisely because it does not borrow Avalon's answer.”

Ashley glanced at him.

“And because I'm the one responsible for it.”

“I know,” he said.

They exchanged a look that contained a marriage and two command structures, neither reducible to the other.

At the far end of the walkway was another door.

Its heavy warning plate had not been touched for years.

**INNER REFERENCE CHAMBER: PHYSICAL ENTRY RESTRICTED.**

Imani stopped.

“The last recorded physical entry beyond this door was in 2395, during the first Avalon temporal-core emergency. Routine monitoring and calibration afterward have been conducted through the external custody systems. No one has entered the inner chamber since.”

“Was anyone here during the activation?” I asked.

“A small station and lunar-witness coordination team. The contemporaneous records are preserved. Their reports were incomplete. Several measurements were marked as unexplained and left unresolved rather than interpreted.”

She opened her case.

Inside was a physical data plate, an old optical reader and two folded pages protected by transparent sleeves.

“I thought you might prefer the original format.”

Merlin would have loved her.

I almost wished he were standing beside us.

Then I thought of the responsibilities he was fulfilling on Avalon's bridge and left the thought where it belonged.

Imani placed the plate in the reader.

A graph appeared.

Seventeen minutes and forty-three seconds before our 2395 activation, one of the independent clocks registered an unexplained field excitation. The other two showed related disturbances at slightly different amplitudes, consistent with their different locations and shielding.

A sequence of pulses appeared.

Three.

A pause.

Four.

Beside it was a notation in the hand of the watch officer:

*Source uncertain. Not correlated with authorized Avalon test output. Preserve unmodified.*

I read the line twice.

“What happened to the report?” I asked.

“It was filed,” Imani said. “Then the activation occurred, the Sol-system crisis overwhelmed everyone, and the anomaly was placed in the unresolved technical annex.”

“Was it ever marked as the cause?”

“No.”

Noah studied the paper.

“Did someone decide it wasn't important?”

“Someone decided it could not be explained from the available evidence,” Imani answered. “Those are different decisions.”

I looked toward Ashley.

“Captain, Sparrow's nine-point-four-second return corresponds to a physical period in Jupiter's atmosphere. The old vault recorded a related signal before we activated the temporal core.”

“And you think this is where the station's coupling began.”

“I think this is where the earliest independent station record places a response. It doesn't tell us what was already present.”

Ashley nodded.

“Then we'll inspect what's physically here.”

Noah stepped forward.

“I'm coming.”

She raised an eyebrow.

“Into my reference vault?”

“If you authorize it.”

She considered him.

“No automatic links. No direct connection to your ship's temporal systems. No experiments without station permission.”

“Agreed.”

“And if we find anything I don't like, I decide whether my people proceed.”

Noah smiled faintly.

“I wouldn't have it any other way.”

Ashley turned toward Tessa.

“Prepare a sealed entry team. No networked tools, no transporter shortcuts. I want physical controls, independent watches and a medical team at the outer door.”

The Andorian nodded.

“Understood, Captain.”

I looked past the inner door toward the three clock housings.

Somewhere inside their shielding, a physical reference had kept time through the most dangerous experiment of my life.

I had always thought of the archive as a place that remembered.

Standing there, I realized how much work had gone into ensuring that it never tried to tell anyone what to believe.

## Chapter Five: The Black Vein

Brakk arrived from Avalon carrying a case of instruments large enough to suggest he had packed for a siege.

He had insisted on bringing his own portable field meter because station Engineering's equipment was “unnecessarily polite about impossible readings.”

The station engineer assigned to accompany us took this as an insult.

Brakk considered it a compliment that had not yet been properly appreciated.

We had permission to enter the service passage surrounding the inner vault. The chronometer housings themselves remained locked. Everything was recorded by disconnected optical cameras under Imani's custody.

No networked tricorders.

No live station diagnostic link.

The first suspicious reading appeared behind a service panel on the north side of the power and environmental conduit.

The panel had not been opened since the 2395 emergency, according to its physical seal and custody register.

Tessa read the seal number aloud.

Imani matched it to her copy.

Ashley gave the order.

“Open it.”

Brakk loosened the mechanical fasteners.

The panel came away.

I saw a narrow strip of black material, no thicker than a finger, running along the conduit housing.

At first it resembled a line of scorched insulation.

Then the light shifted.

A series of tiny ceramic contacts gleamed beneath the surface, set at irregular intervals. Each contact bore three grooves on one side and four on the other.

Sorev crouched beside the opening.

“No authorized station installation uses this contact geometry.”

Brakk checked the original drawings.

“Not part of the design. Not part of any repair I can find.”

The material moved.

Only a fraction of a millimeter.

But I watched it bend, as though it were breathing against the metal.

A thermal camera registered a bright line along its length.

Sorev placed a passive contact probe against the material without using a powered sensor circuit.

The temperature differential was opposite the camera's apparent brightness.

“Warm to the radiometer,” he said, “but drawing heat from the probe.”

“Cold to the touch,” Brakk translated.

Ashley looked toward him.

“Are you telling me it has a temperature and an image that disagree?”

“I am telling you the ordinary relationship between its emitted radiation and the energy it takes from a contacting object is not behaving as expected. I don't recommend putting a hand on it to settle the argument.”

“No one will.”

The vein twitched again.

At exactly the nine-point-four-second interval.

I checked my independent clock.

“Merlin, compare the local measurement with Sparrow's most recent return.”

His voice arrived through an optical relay whose electronics remained outside the restricted reference chamber.

“Commander, the shorter-period component matches within the two instruments' current timing uncertainties. The longer eleven-point-zero-three-second component is still present in the station field, but not in the contact displacement.”

“Thank you.”

Ashley studied the black line.

“What is it feeding?”

Brakk followed its path with a mirror mounted on an insulated rod.

“Not power from your grid. It's physically bridging the external environmental conduit toward the reference vault's shield boundary. The field associated with Jupiter is the most plausible energy source, but we haven't demonstrated the complete path.”

“And this line is the root?” Noah asked.

Brakk shook his head.

“This is the bit we can see. It might be a branch. It might be a continuation. It certainly isn't authorized station wiring.”

Ashley folded her arms.

“It's now part of my problem.”

We spent the next hour mapping the visible material without removing it.

The vein disappeared behind the inner shielding. Small contacts appeared at intervals along its length, some embedded in metal that had not been disturbed since 2395.

They did not resemble growth in any biological sense I could establish. They had not formed from anything familiar enough to call living tissue.

But they spread through seams and junctions with a patient regularity that was profoundly uncomfortable to watch.

Brakk studied the geometry.

“If we phaser this at the wrong point, the field could scatter through the environmental and shield-distribution conductors. We saw what happened when Avalon's boom acted as an unwanted coupler.”

“Could we isolate it?” Ashley asked.

“Yes. But not by throwing power at it.”

“Then how?”

“Manual shunts around the field path. Physical dampers. Then a cut with the current channel unloaded.”

The station engineer regarded him.

“You'll be working inside the vault's shielding.”

Brakk nodded.

“And if we touch the independent reference too hard, we could lose one of the clocks.”

Sorev looked toward the three housings.

“Potentially all three, if the coupling is common to their shielding structures.”

Ashley closed the engineering diagram.

“Then no one cuts anything until I approve an evacuation and reference-preservation plan.”

Noah nodded.

“Agreed.”

I looked at the black vein.

Its ceramic contacts flashed faintly.

Three.

A pause.

Four.

I remembered the biological Barclay saying that the chair's fitting had felt warm, then cold.

I remembered its presence in a design where it did not belong.

Now it appeared inside a vault that had been keeping an independent record for nearly half a century.

A station could have an intruder without hearing a door open.

And if we had not noticed the slight difference between two signal patterns, we might never have looked behind the panel.

# PART THREE: PEOPLE WHO ARE NOT PROOF

## Chapter Six: Thirty-Seven Plus One

Jupiter Station's temporary residency corridor was full of luggage.

Actual luggage.

The sort of objects people carried when they expected to return home but discovered that home had changed its address.

Some bags were ordinary Starfleet kits. Others were improvised bundles of clothing, medical supplies and the few possessions recovered from the damaged Avalon before it vanished into the doorway.

The thirty-seven survivors had been medically stabilized. They were no longer one room full of emergency patients. They were becoming people who needed places to sleep, rooms to claim, jobs to return to, families to contact and answers no one could promise them.

Commander Tessa zh'Renn was helping coordinate the housing arrangements.

She stood beside a registry terminal as two station officers carried fresh bedding into a temporary suite.

“Commander Barclay,” she said when she saw me. “Thank you for coming.”

“Can I help?”

“Yes. Stop the other Captain from assigning himself to maintenance duty before Medical clears him.”

I looked toward the corridor.

The injured Noah Hawkes had been examining a repair manifest with more enthusiasm than his immobilized shoulder warranted.

I smiled.

“That may be beyond my authority.”

“I had feared as much.”

Tessa's antennae angled toward the far door.

“Mara Venn asked for you.”

I found her in a small common room overlooking one of the station's inner gardens. Two other survivors sat nearby, talking quietly over replicated tea.

She stood when she saw me.

“Commander.”

“Lieutenant.”

Her expression held an uncertainty I remembered from the damaged Avalon.

“I thought REG was dead,” she said.

I waited.

“On our ship, the computer told us he was gone. Then the Captain said he was alive. Then we found a living man, and now I come to this station and everyone tells me the first officer is a hologram.”

“I can understand the confusion.”

“Can you?”

I looked toward the window.

“More than I'd like.”

She studied me.

“Can I meet him?”

“The biological Barclay?”

She nodded.

“Yes. If he wants to.”

I spoke with the Doctor first.

He insisted that both men be informed of the meeting's purpose and given the chance to refuse. He also announced that he would remain nearby because he had spent far too long watching temporal research teams treat human beings as interesting components.

The biological Barclay agreed.

We met in a small sitting room near Medical, with a transparent garden visible beyond the wall.

Mara entered first.

When she saw him, she stopped.

“Reg?”

He looked up.

“Mara.”

Her eyes filled.

“You were on the ship.”

“Yes.”

“I watched you go into the computer corridor.”

“I remember parts of that.”

She looked toward me.

Then back toward him.

“There are two of you.”

He shook his head gently.

“Two people who look alike. We don't know the rest.”

Mara sat down.

She had been aboard the other Avalon when our first rescue team reached her. She had watched the ship's pressure doors fail and carried wounded people toward a maintenance hatch while the computer announced predicted deaths.

Now she sat between two versions of a man she'd thought lost.

“What does it mean?” she asked.

I took the chair beside her.

“I don't know how these histories are related. What I know is that in 2395, my biological body died while I was connected to Avalon's systems. In the adopted history of my crew, I continued aboard that ship afterward. This man arrived three days ago from a damaged vessel whose source history remains uncertain.”

She listened.

“And you're both Reginald Barclay?”

“We each have grounds for using the name,” I said. “The name doesn't settle the history.”

The biological Barclay looked at her.

“I don't want you to choose which one of us was your friend.”

Mara's face tightened.

“I didn't come here to choose.”

“Then why?”

She took a breath.

“Because Dr. Hale died saving me. And I keep thinking that if we'd answered one of those messages differently, he might still be alive. I thought maybe the two of you would know how to live with something like that.”

For a moment I was back aboard my own ship in 2395, hearing Captain Hawkes tell me to get out of the chair.

I had not.

The Doctor broke the silence.

“Lieutenant Venn, the decisions people make during emergencies are not evidence of personal guilt merely because another outcome can be imagined afterward. You are entitled to grieve the doctor without turning every possible alternative into an accusation against yourself.”

Mara wiped her eyes.

“I know.”

“But you don't feel it yet,” he added.

“No.”

The biological Barclay reached out, then stopped, unsure whether she wanted his hand.

She took it.

I looked away, granting them the small privacy of the gesture.

Thirty-seven people had survived.

One medical officer had not.

We could preserve the count without letting the count become the story.

Later, after Mara had left, the biological Barclay asked the Doctor whether he could attend Hale's memorial.

The Doctor looked genuinely surprised.

“Of course.”

“Even though you haven't determined who I am?”

“You knew him as a colleague. You are grieving him. Neither fact requires a temporal origin certificate.”

The man nodded.

“Thank you.”

I watched him as he left the room.

There was a great deal of discussion aboard the station about how to treat individuals who arrived from other histories.

That afternoon, it came down to something much simpler.

A man asked permission to mourn a friend.

And someone gave it.

## Chapter Seven: Merlin's Second Five Questions

Merlin had become dangerously comfortable asking difficult questions.

I considered this one of my greater achievements as his superior officer.

He found me at the edge of Avalon's bridge after second watch, standing beside a display of the station's central reference vault.

The three clock housings floated before us in a schematic, separate and immense.

“Commander, may I ask you five questions?”

I raised an eyebrow.

“The last time you asked me five questions, we discovered that something was attempting to rewrite our historical record.”

“Precisely, sir.”

“Then by all means.”

He pulled a chair alongside the attestation console.

I sat opposite him.

He arranged the recent observations without assigning conclusions: Sparrow's acoustic return, the nine-point-four-second physical resonance in the quarantined mast, the old 2395 reference logs, the black vein in the station's central service passage, and the still-sealed cylinder from the other Avalon.

“First,” he said. “If the outer ring is a recorder, who was it built to record?”

I studied the question.

“We've observed an outgoing and returning signal consistent with ranging. We haven't demonstrated a storage system, let alone its intended subjects.”

“But if it is an archive?”

“Then we need to determine what it stores and how it would preserve evidence. Perhaps the delay between a pulse and its return. Perhaps changes in the environment across time. Perhaps something we don't yet recognize.”

“UNRESOLVED,” Merlin said, and entered it.

“Second. Why does the nine-point-four-second interval match the resonant frequency of the black material?”

“Careful,” I said. “Nine point four seconds is a period. A frequency would be its reciprocal.”

Merlin smiled.

“Quite right. The resonance period. Why does it match?”

“We don't know whether the material drives the signal, follows it, or responds to the same physical field.”

“UNRESOLVED.”

“Third. Did we bring the coupling to the station, or did the station's old antenna find the ring?”

I considered the 2395 archive.

“The preactivation record indicates a signal was already observable before Avalon's core was activated. It doesn't establish whether the mast had been interacting with that field for years, or whether the interface and the later editor changed a preexisting relationship.”

“And the new material?”

“Could have formed during the 2395 crisis. Could have existed before. We need a physical chronology rather than an attractive story.”

“UNRESOLVED.”

“Fourth. What does the cylinder from the damaged Avalon contain that we haven't opened?”

I looked toward the security case secured in Avalon's isolated evidence locker.

“An unanswered question we are finally prepared to examine.”

Merlin grinned.

“So, still unresolved.”

“For now.”

“Fifth.” His expression grew serious. “Who benefits if we leave the root alone because it seems quiet?”

I watched Jupiter turn beneath the station.

“That assumes there is a beneficiary,” I said. “But the risk is real without that assumption. We have evidence of physical material bridging a field into the station's independent reference vault. If it continues to grow, the station may lose the independence that protects its own history.”

“Could it restore the old editor's ability to forge authority?”

“Possibly. It could also cause a field discharge without any hostile intention. We must distinguish the hazard from its explanation.”

Merlin entered the final status.

**UNRESOLVED.**

Then he leaned back.

“Five questions. Not one answer.”

“Not yet.”

“That seems less satisfying than the first time.”

I smiled.

“It's more useful than five invented answers.”

He stared at his screen.

The cursor blinked beneath the final entry.

“I used to think my job was to produce a complete account,” he said.

“It is.”

“How can an account be complete when so much remains unknown?”

“By preserving the boundary between what happened and what we'd like to say happened.”

He considered that.

Then his face softened.

“I think I understand.”

“Good.”

“And Commander?”

“Yes?”

“I've left the laughter from earlier in the margin.”

I looked toward him.

“Which laughter?”

“Yours. When Brakk said the probe was expendable, and Ellie reminded him that so was his coffee mug.”

I laughed again.

Merlin smiled.

“Another marginal note?”

“Absolutely not.”

He typed something.

I chose not to inspect it.

Across the bridge, Jupiter Station's lights shimmered above the vast curve of Jupiter.

We had enough questions to fill a book.

But the fifth was already driving our decisions.

We could leave the black vein alone because it seemed quiet.

Or we could find out whether quiet was the same as safe.

The Captain would have to choose.

We would have to give him measurements worth choosing from.

# PART FOUR: THE EVIDENCE WE CARRIED

## Chapter Eight: The Cylinder

The cylinder lay on an examination table under six separate lamps.

It was a small thing.

A hundred and twenty millimeters long, with a sealed outer case, two manual releases and no electronic connectors exposed on its surface. It had been handed to me by a living Reginald Barclay in the damaged Avalon moments before that vessel vanished into the doorway.

For three days we'd kept it isolated.

Not because opening a cylinder was a difficult engineering problem.

Because we had already seen how easily a record could persuade a computer to treat a claim of identity as permission.

This time we would not open it in the presence of anything capable of making that mistake.

Laboratory Four had been converted into an isolated examination room. Its ordinary data network was physically disconnected. Three optical cameras ran on separate power supplies. A manual reader sat at the center of the table, capable of extracting stored images without following embedded commands.

Sorev inspected the seal.

“External surface intact. No detectable active field.”

Ellie leaned forward.

“That's not the same as saying it's harmless.”

“Correct.”

“I wanted to hear you say it.”

I watched them with affection.

“Before we begin, do we have a custody record?”

Merlin's voice came through a speaker that could not accept signals from the examination instruments.

“Commander, the cylinder has remained in an isolated locker since your rescue. Physical seal numbers match. The biological Barclay confirms that he gave it to you, but he cannot now verify every item it may contain.”

“His consent to read?”

“He agrees to examination of material relevant to the ship's emergency. He asks that private personal entries unrelated to the event be withheld from routine circulation.”

“Reasonable,” I said.

Sorev opened the outer seal.

A faint click sounded.

Nothing else happened.

Inside was a narrow optical memory plate.

Not an interface module.

Not a fragment of the missing eleven seconds.

A simple record.

I felt almost disappointed, then was ashamed of the feeling.

The most important evidence often arrived without making a dramatic entrance.

Sorev placed the plate in the manual reader. The display came to life with a sequence of text, timestamps and short video segments. Each entry was accompanied by a local clock value and a checksum registered when the original image had been written.

Merlin's recorder preserved those bytes before we interpreted them.

The first entry was made aboard the damaged Avalon during its approach to Jupiter.

The biological Barclay appeared on the screen, seated in a small engineering office.

He looked tired.

But not frightened.

“Personal log,” he began. “We are approaching Jupiter Station. The navigation receiver has acquired a repeating signal from the Jovian environment.”

He glanced toward a display out of frame.

“Three pulses, pause, four. Every nine point four seconds, as measured by the local clock. No station response yet.”

He smiled, embarrassed.

“We thought it was a navigation beacon. We answered it with our temporal reference as a courtesy.”

I felt something tighten in my chest.

The man on the recording continued.

“There's a secondary fluctuation at the end of the third pulse. It seems to change with our relative position. I want to compare it with a station instrument before we integrate it into the approach model.”

The video ended.

Another entry followed.

It was only a minute later.

A new waveform appeared beside the old one.

The repeat interval had changed.

Eleven point zero three seconds.

The smaller fluctuation now arrived at a fixed delay.

A message appeared at the bottom of the display.

**TRUSTED SOURCE RESTORED.**

Then the record ended.

I remained motionless.

Ellie was the first to speak.

“That's the transition.”

Sorev looked toward her.

“Evidence of a transition in the damaged vessel's observations, yes. The local record does not independently establish its cause.”

“Understood,” she said. “But there's a sequence. Natural-looking return first. They answer with a trusted temporal reference. Then the fixed pattern and command-like content appear.”

I studied the man's face on the frozen image.

He had intended courtesy.

A harmless acknowledgment.

The sort one starship sends another when it enters a busy system and wishes to announce that it has arrived.

A small act of politeness built into a machine.

And somewhere inside the machinery, that act had become a point of entry.

Noah's voice came through the isolated speaker.

“Reg?”

“Captain.”

“Is it what we thought?”

“Not precisely. It's not the missing eleven seconds. It is the other Barclay's approach log. It reports the nine-point-four-second physical signal before any observed command overlay, followed by a trusted-reference response and a change to the longer interval.”

“Could the record be false?”

“Yes, sir. We have not independently verified its origin or every value. But the sequence is testable against the other observations.”

A pause.

Then the Captain said, “We almost did the same thing when we came home.”

I remembered the indicator on Avalon's bridge converging with Jupiter Station's reference.

The satisfaction of seeing the words *branch authentication confirmed*.

Then the unauthorized transmission in my own voice.

“Yes,” I answered. “We did.”

I looked toward Ellie.

“Lieutenant, can you compare the eleven-point-zero-three-second interval with the destroyed editor's former fixed clock without letting the cylinder's software execute?”

She smiled grimly.

“That's why we built the boring reader.”

Sorev and Ellie worked for eighteen minutes.

Their comparison showed that the longer interval matched the fixed command-like timing of the former inner editor within the precision of the preserved records. The nine-point-four-second period matched the physical return Sparrow still observed.

It did not prove that the ring was conscious.

It did not prove why the editor had chosen that interval.

But it supported a model that explained more observations than our earlier one-clock interpretation.

The outer ring's period appeared physically coupled to Jupiter's magnetosphere.

The longer interval appeared imposed by the later command-generating machinery.

Two clocks.

One inherited from an environment.

One designed to manage, edit or override it.

I leaned toward the microphone.

“Merlin, update the record.”

“Status?”

“Observed agreement between the later imposed interval and the historical editor timing. Working inference that the original ring's resonance is approximately nine point four seconds. Do not call either inference proven from a single cylinder.”

“Aye, Commander.”

He paused.

“Commander, may I say something?”

“Of course.”

“The other Barclay recorded the signal before the thing began telling him what to believe.”

“Yes.”

“So for a moment, he was just curious.”

I looked at the image.

A tired engineer looking at a strange signal from Jupiter.

“Indeed,” I said.

“And then he answered.”

I closed my eyes.

“Yes.”

How many catastrophes began not with malice, but with an unexamined courtesy?

I wanted to know.

Not because I blamed him.

Because I remembered being that engineer.

## Chapter Nine: Cutting the Branch

The old sensor mast had already been removed from the inhabited ring during the previous emergency.

That point was settled.

It had not been reinstalled.

What remained was a smaller quarantined module, a section of the mast's instrument housing containing the black trace. Station tugs had towed the module to a sealed service frame beyond the lower engineering ring. It remained attached to that frame by a physical maintenance clamp, isolated from the station's data and power systems but still within the electromagnetic corridor passing the reference vault.

Ashley's people had retained it because they were trying to learn what it was.

Now it had become part of the problem.

At every nine-point-four-second interval, the module flexed.

Its ceramic contacts registered a minute change in radiation.

And in the vault's service passage, the black vein responded at nearly the same moment.

I presented the latest observations to a joint command conference.

Ashley listened beside Tessa and Imani.

Noah listened from Avalon's bridge with Sorev, Ellie and Merlin.

Brakk's engineering model occupied the center of the display.

“Captain Ashley,” I began, “we may be able to distinguish a branch of the coupling from its root by physically separating the quarantined module while preserving an independent record of both its response and the vault's.”

She studied the model.

“You're recommending another station hardware release.”

“Yes.”

“Last time one of these devices started turning into something else.”

“I remember.”

“And we lost an entire docking assembly saving the station.”

“Yes.”

She looked toward Brakk.

“Can the module be released without disturbing the reference vault?”

“There's no active electrical connection,” he answered. “We can move it using station tugs under manual control. But the unknown field may still respond. I can't promise it won't.”

Tessa crossed her arms.

“Then the tugs must be unmanned. No people near the module when it releases.”

Ashley nodded.

“Agreed.”

Imani raised a question.

“What happens to the custody record if the module vanishes again?”

“We retain the physical seal numbers, original camera files and independent observations,” I said. “But we should not pretend a lost object remains available for future testing.”

She nodded.

“Then the tugs use their own clocks and local optics. No automatic synchronization against the vault.”

The Captain looked toward Ashley.

“Your station. Your decision.”

She considered for several seconds.

“Approved. Detach the module and tow it beyond the station's electromagnetic exclusion boundary. I want at least two independent optical cameras and no crew aboard the handling craft.”

Her officers moved.

The station's external cameras showed two remote tugs approaching the small quarantine frame. Jupiter filled the background, the red and cream bands of the planet turning beneath the silver structure.

The tugs closed mechanical grapplers around the module's carrying cradle.

The restraint clamps released.

Nothing happened.

For a moment the module hung between the craft.

Then its black trace moved.

Three small pulses.

A pause.

Four.

Ashley watched the timing indicator.

“Tow it away.”

The tugs fired their thrusters.

The module began moving outward.

The resonance weakened.

At five kilometers, it was barely detectable.

At eleven, the strain gauge registered no coherent nine-point-four-second motion.

Sorev looked up.

“The module is no longer resonating above instrument noise.”

Merlin checked the independent station observation.

“Confirmed within the current detection limits.”

A relieved murmur moved through Operations.

Then Ellie spoke from Avalon's bridge.

“Wait.”

She enlarged the vault's field record.

The black vein inside the reference passage was still responding.

Nine point four seconds.

Again.

And again.

The mast module had gone quiet.

The station's core had not.

Ashley looked toward me.

“Branch removed.”

I nodded slowly.

“And the root remains.”

Noah leaned forward.

“What do we know about the root?”

I stared at the vault diagram.

“We know it enters the shielding around the station's independently maintained clocks. We know it responds to the Jovian field without station power. We know the mast module's removal did not stop that response.”

“Is it getting worse?”

Sorev's answer came immediately.

“The signal's longer eleven-point-zero-three-second component is becoming more regular at the vault boundary.”

Ellie added, “And it's appearing closer to the station's protected timing comparison circuits.”

Ashley folded her arms.

“How much time?”

“No reliable estimate,” Brakk said. “But if the material reaches a circuit the station uses to establish its own time, somebody may once again be able to present a physical signal as though it were a trusted reference.”

Noah's expression darkened.

“The editor is gone.”

“The original inner device is no longer transmitting,” I said. “That does not prove it left no residual coupling or that the same behavior cannot recur elsewhere.”

Ashley stared toward the lower station.

There was no alarm.

No approaching warship.

No one shouting that the habitation ring would break apart in ninety seconds.

Just a quiet vein of black material behind a sealed wall, moving to a clock that had been there before anyone knew what it was.

She made her decision.

“Prepare an entry team.”

Tessa looked toward her.

“Captain, we can send a station engineer.”

Ashley shook her head.

“I'm going.”

Noah immediately spoke.

“Ashley.”

She met his eyes.

“This is my station's independent reference vault. I am the one who decides what happens to it.”

“You don't have to be the one holding the cutter.”

“No,” she said. “But I have to be there when the decision is made.”

I watched the two captains.

Neither was wrong to care about the other's safety.

Neither could resolve that concern by taking the other's command.

At last Noah nodded.

“Then Avalon will provide whatever support you authorize.”

Ashley softened.

“Thank you.”

The screen dimmed.

I looked toward the chamber's three clock housings.

A station's heart had been built to preserve its own time.

Now we were about to open it.

And I had a terrible feeling that whatever lay inside had been listening longer than any of us realized.
