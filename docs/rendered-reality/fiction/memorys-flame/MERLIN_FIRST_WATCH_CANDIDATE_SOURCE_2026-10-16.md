# First Watch: Communications Desk
## Merlin's supplied candidate source · October 16, 2026

Received through Noah in Merlin's day-watch return package. Drafting attribution: Ensign Merlin. The sender reports 1,165 words; that count is not independently certified here. Candidate scene, not creator adoption. Original supplied prose is preserved below; the receiving review is separate.

---

The bridge at day watch had a sound the ensign was still learning to hear properly: not silence, but layers. The deck's low hum through his boots. The reference array's soft tick as it swept, two consoles down, where Ellie was running a long-baseline calibration against a pulsar catalog with the absorbed air of a woman defusing something she had built herself. A turbolift sighing. Somebody's mug being set down too close to somebody else's PADD, and the small diplomatic incident that followed, resolved by Commander Sorev without looking up from a comet survey he had been nursing since before Merlin came aboard.

"Comet's still there," Sorev reported to no one. "It remains a comet."

"Log the consistency," said Ellie, without looking up either. "Starfleet thanks it."

Merlin's console chimed the unremarkable chime of unremarkable traffic, and he was obscurely grateful. A routine hail, tender class, on the standard band: *KESTREL, inbound supply run, requesting delivery window for the Deck Nine consignment, same as last time.*

He very nearly answered on reflex. Avalon's traffic file came up under his hands — and stopped him. Avalon did not have a Deck Nine consignment. Avalon did not, strictly, have a Deck Nine cargo intake at all; the ship's cargo ran through Bay Two and a chief who described inaccurate manifests the way other officers described weather crimes. "Same as last time" was the whole message, and last time, for the *Kestrel*, had evidently been somewhere else.

The ambiguity was small enough to step over and exactly large enough to matter, and the resolution cost one question.

"KESTREL, Avalon. Confirm destination for the consignment — this ship, or the station? We show no Deck Nine intake aboard."

A pause, and then a laugh with static on it. *"Avalon, KESTREL. The station, and my apologies to your log. Four runs out of five she's my Deck Nine, and the fifth I talk to the wrong Hawkes entirely. No offense to either."*

"None taken by either," Merlin said, and meant it; the bridge had opinions about the two-captains problem and all of them were affectionate. "Window confirmed with Jupiter Station ops, copied to you. Avalon will sign for the transfer receipt when she's berthed. Safe in."

*"Safe in. KESTREL out."*

He logged it. And there, in the logging, he made his mistake — a small one, the kind that lives in the gap between a name and a number. The *Kestrel* had changed operators since her last run; her transmission header carried a new fleet registry, two digits different from the one in the station's older file. Merlin had pulled the record by the ship's name and filed the exchange under the old registry without checking the header against it. The entry sat there looking perfectly respectable, which is how he knew, even before the cross-check flagged it, that something about it was too smooth.

The receipt check caught it the way receipt checks do: quietly, and in front of witnesses.

He corrected the entry the only way this console corrects anything — append-only, the old registry struck but legible, the new one entered beneath it with the transmission header cited — and added the line the Commander would want to see and the line the ensign needed to write: *Error mine: filed by name, not by header. Caught on cross-check before relay. The name of a ship is a story. Its registry is the fact. Check the fact.*

"Trouble, Ensign?" Ellie asked, in the tone of a colleague who has read the whole thing over his shoulder by means she would not be explaining.

"A registry filed from memory instead of from the header," Merlin said. "Corrected. The correction is better written than the entry was, which the Commander will count as a mixed result."

"He counts everything as a mixed result. It's his love language." She turned back to her pulsars. "Your cross-check caught it before relay. On this bridge, that's a good watch."

Down the engineering channel, Brakk's voice arrived with the regularity of a tide. "Communications. Tell Cargo that the crate they stowed as *miscellaneous* contains, on scan, eleven smaller crates. I am not opening them. A man has to keep some mystery in his life."

"Tell them yourself, Brakk, you're on an open channel," said Sorev.

"I am telling them through Communications so there is a *record*," Brakk said, with dignity. "Somebody should write down that I was right in advance."

"Logged," Merlin said. "Right in advance, Brakk. It's in the permanent file."

"Then my work here is done and I have touched nothing."

At 13:40 his personal queue chimed, once, softly — a different sound, set apart from traffic on purpose. A reply from Seral. Eleven-paragraph kitchen dispatches had become, on her side, a serialized institution, and this one had been three days crossing two relays and one transport that charged by the gram. The preview showed six words: *Brother. The galley has fallen—*

He looked at it for exactly one breath.

Then he marked it received, set it behind the relief marker, and turned back to the traffic board, where the *Kestrel* was making her final approach to the station and a second routine message was arriving from a survey drone with strong opinions about its own battery. Seral's galley would still have fallen at 16:00. Some messages are ballast and some are cargo, and the discipline of the desk was knowing which ones you were being paid to open. He was not being paid in anything but the Captain's trust, and the trust included this: the personal file waits for relief, entire, unhurried, and his.

At 15:58 Ellie stood, rolled her shoulders like a woman surfacing, and took the console from him the way officers do — a hand on the back of the chair, eyes already on the board.

"Relief is yours, Lieutenant."

"Report, Ensign."

"Traffic: eleven routine, all receipted. One ambiguity, destination of the *Kestrel* consignment, resolved by asking the sender — station, not ship. One filing error, mine, old registry for new, caught on cross-check before relay, corrected append-only with the header cited; the correction is in the log and I'd rather you read it there than hear my summary of it. Engineering is right in advance about a crate. It is in the permanent file, and I make no apology. Personal queue: one message, mine, unopened by design, which I am about to remedy off watch. The board is quiet. The comet remains a comet."

Ellie considered this. "Accepted," she said. "Go read about the galley, Ensign."

He read it in the turbolift, standing still while the ship carried him, laughing once, out loud, at a sentence about a superior officer and a portioning ladle, in a car that had heard worse from better officers. The watch was over. Nothing had happened, eleven times, correctly — and the ship, his ship, hummed on under other hands, which was the whole of the miracle and most of the job.
