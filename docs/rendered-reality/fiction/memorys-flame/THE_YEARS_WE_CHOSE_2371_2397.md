# The Years We Chose
## A Starfleet life, 2371–2397

**Memory's Flame / USS Avalon / Jupiter Station**  
**Creative authority:** Noah Alexander Hawkes  
**Written:** November 14, 2026, with AI drafting assistance in Reg's narrative voice  
**Standing:** Newly commissioned fictional backstory and scenes. The [service profile](../canon/HAWKES_STARFLEET_PERSONAL_PROFILES_AND_SERVICE_LOG_2026-11-14.md) distinguishes recovered anchors from the dates and connective history authored for this update.

### 1. Qualification

The first thing Tuvok gave Noah was a maintenance inventory.

Noah looked down at it, then back at the Vulcan.

“I thought we were doing an assessment.”

“We are.”

“I know quite a lot about this ship.”

“Then locating its emergency breathing equipment should present little difficulty.”

It was September 4, 2371. Noah was sixteen, displaced, and still occasionally startled by the reality of a door opening when he approached it. He knew Janeway's name. He knew the sound of the bridge. He knew stories about people who were now eating breakfast somewhere above him.

He did not know which emergency locker had been moved during repairs.

Forty minutes later, Tuvok found him outside the wrong compartment.

“The information in your possession,” Tuvok said, “is not the same as the condition of the vessel.”

Noah remembered the sentence for the rest of his career.

Q had offered him something larger than a ship, larger than Starfleet: a place in the Continuum. An unknown variable in a known equation. Something fresh in a realm that had grown accustomed to knowing its own answers.

Noah had refused.

He had a life to live first.

It turned out that life included equipment inventories.

### 2. The people below the bridge

His Academy coursework began in October.

There was no courtyard, no ceremonial first day, and no guarantee a lesson would end before the alert sounded. The ship's educational archives supplied the curriculum. Its officers supplied the standards.

Harry Kim helped him with operations problems over meals. Sometimes Harry was so tired he reread the same line twice, which reassured Noah more than any speech about perseverance.

B'Elanna returned one engineering assignment with a single comment.

“What happens when the sensor is wrong?”

Noah revised it.

She returned it again.

“What happens when both sensors share the same damaged connection?”

He began drawing the connections.

The Doctor taught first response with an enthusiasm that made simulated casualties feel personally offended by Noah's mistakes.

Tom Paris taught him that recognizing a maneuver was not the same as flying it.

“You're looking where you want to go,” Tom said during a shuttle simulation.

“Isn't that the idea?”

“Your hands have to come with you.”

Noah's closest training friendships formed away from the bridge. Crewman **Mara Chen**, a propulsion technician with an untidy laugh, could find a damaged connector by the smell. **Davin Rell**, a young Bolian maintenance specialist, memorized procedure and distrusted improvisation. **Sera Nadir**, a civilian laboratory assistant completing her own studies, was better at mathematics than Noah and unwilling to pretend otherwise.

These three are new characters in this career expansion, members of the ship's wider working community. They have no retroactive claim to replace an established Voyager officer.

They studied together. They argued over who had eaten somebody else's saved dessert. They discovered that friendship sometimes consisted of sitting quietly beside a person who had received bad news from very far away.

Noah learned to belong before he learned to lead.

### 3. Havoc

The name was Davin's fault.

After an exercise in which all four trainees had followed individually defensible procedures and collectively produced a disaster, he observed that their contribution had been primarily havoc.

The name survived.

Tuvok declined to use it for two weeks. Then he arrived at their next exercise and said, without expression, “Havoc Squad will assemble at the indicated hatch.”

Nobody laughed until he had left.

The formal training cycle began July 7, 2373. It was cross-training, not a secret commando qualification. Voyager needed junior people who could keep functioning when specialists were unavailable.

They practiced casualty movement, compartment isolation, basic security, survival, lost communications, and the discipline of identifying who actually held command.

In one simulation Noah reached the control point early.

He had left Sera behind.

Her simulated leg injury made moving her difficult. The clock made leaving her seem efficient.

Tuvok ended the exercise.

“The control point is secure,” Noah protested.

“And your team?”

Noah looked at the frozen corridor.

They repeated the exercise that afternoon.

This time they arrived late, together, and with less equipment.

Tuvok passed them.

Later, during a genuine shipboard emergency, Davin froze at a hatch because the pressure reading contradicted the local gauge. Noah almost shouted at him.

Instead he remembered the exercise.

They tested a third point, confirmed the fault, and brought two injured crew members through another route.

There was no medal. B'Elanna wanted to know who had left a tool case in the passage.

Noah went back for it.

### 4. The first pip

He completed his shipboard course requirements in 2375.

Tuvok's final field assessment was favorable, though Noah privately felt the paragraph about unnecessary argument could have been shorter.

On December 6, at 0900, Janeway asked him to stand in her ready room.

The provisional commission was subject to review when they returned. There were qualifications and restrictions. Noah listened carefully.

Then she handed him the pip.

“You have spent a great deal of time telling us you want to be useful,” she said. “Now you have more responsibility for making certain you are.”

He had imagined the moment being louder.

Afterward he found Mara, Davin and Sera waiting outside the mess hall.

Mara inspected his collar.

“Well,” she said, “that's going to make the locker inventories unbearable.”

He became a provisional junior lieutenant in April 2377. His work increasingly involved operations support, incident records and scientific observations.

When the Delta Flyer became part of the ship's working life, he earned a place in its support rotation. He checked equipment before he flew. He learned to sit beside someone more qualified without needing to prove his own importance.

On one rescue-support sortie, his contribution was keeping a damaged instrument stable while another officer made the critical decision.

Later he wrote the report with the other officer's name first.

That, too, was training.

### 5. Years that disagree

Temporal incidents disturbed Noah in a way ordinary dangers did not.

A damaged hull was terrifying, but everyone could agree where the damage was.

An altered history could leave people arguing about whether a friend had ever stood beside them.

The Year of Hell material remained difficult to classify. An account from an altered branch was evidence of that branch. It was not automatically a memory he could claim in the restored history.

He began keeping careful distinctions in his working notes.

Who remembered this?

Which instrument recorded it?

Was the report written during the event or afterward?

Had anyone copied another person's account without saying so?

Tuvok read one of those notebooks.

“You are attempting to distinguish experience from reconstruction.”

“Yes.”

“A useful distinction. You should apply it when describing your own certainty.”

Noah looked at him.

“I walked into that.”

“You frequently do.”

### 6. Home is a review board

Voyager returned in 2378.

Noah had imagined Earth.

He had not imagined the forms.

His commission, education and service were reviewed. Some officials were generous. Some were skeptical. A few seemed personally offended that a stranded starship had failed to arrange its emergency training around their preferred documentation.

On September 12 his commission was recognized, with institutional courses still to complete.

On October 1 he joined Admiral Owen Paris's staff.

His first report was twelve pages too long.

Paris handed it back.

“Is it wrong, sir?”

“No. It's exhausting.”

Noah revised it.

The next version was concise and omitted a qualification that mattered.

Paris handed that one back too.

For the next two years Noah reviewed captains' logs, engineering appendices, casualty reports, and requests for further investigation. He learned that a captain could be honest and incomplete. He learned that an engineer could be technically correct and angry enough to misrepresent another officer's motives.

His first substantial review concerned a rescue recorded as a delay in a supply mission.

The captain's log described a diversion.

The medical attachment described refugees.

The cargo record counted fewer people than the medical officer had treated.

There was no temporal phenomenon. There were exhausted officers, conflicting definitions, and a report that had passed through too many hands.

Noah recommended correction and follow-up interviews.

Paris signed the orders.

That separation mattered. Noah prepared recommendations. The admiral exercised the authority.

### 7. The doorway in Utah

The University of Utah annex smelled of rain when Noah met Ashley.

It was February 17, 2379, 1610. A seminar had ended late. Noah stood in the doorway explaining why the lecturer's example did not adequately distinguish clock disagreement from event disagreement.

Ashley waited.

Then she said, “Are you going to finish the doctorate in this doorway?”

He moved.

“I wasn't blocking it.”

“You were doing a very thorough impression.”

She was an ensign in orbital-support operations, studying emergency coordination and systems management. He was completing institutional requirements and preparing for doctoral research while working for Paris.

She had a class to reach.

He asked if she wanted coffee.

“After my class,” she said. “If you've released the doorway.”

Their real-world author's Utah connection, Ashley's Davis High and Noah's Layton, supplies the affectionate local grounding behind this fictional meeting. It is not evidence that their actual school attendance occurred in this century.

They began with coffee.

They continued with meals that one of them sometimes missed.

Ashley listened to his account of Voyager without treating every ordinary detail as a prelude to an extraordinary destiny.

Once, after he had explained the Q encounter at considerable length, she asked whether he had enjoyed being offered the chance to stop having responsibilities.

He considered that longer than he expected.

“I think I enjoyed being asked.”

She nodded.

“That sounds more like a person.”

### 8. Ashley's watch

Ashley had entered the Academy in 2374.

Her first assignments after graduation placed her in orbital logistics, where an elegant schedule could fail because a transport needed maintenance or a medical shipment could not wait.

Her supervisor, **Commander Lena Varek**, taught her to ask who absorbed a delay.

The answer was rarely the officer presenting the schedule.

In 2380, a docking restriction threatened to strand a group of civilian passengers while a priority research transfer occupied the available transport capacity.

Ashley reorganized the transfer, preserved the essential research cargo, and made the scientists wait for their nonessential equipment.

A senior specialist complained.

She showed him the medical requirements of the passengers who would otherwise have remained aboard a failing transport.

He stopped complaining to her.

Noah loved hearing that story.

Ashley reminded him it had involved six hours of work, not one satisfying sentence.

She became a junior lieutenant that August.

Their relationship grew because they liked the people they found beneath the uniforms. It also grew through less comfortable discoveries: Noah could continue working long after he had promised to stop; Ashley could decide she was carrying too much before telling him how much she had already taken on.

They learned to say those things earlier.

They married June 21, 2381.

Neither promised that duty would be easy.

They promised to keep making room for the other person within it.

### 9. Three years on a small ship

Noah joined Bozeman-A that September.

Her captain in this expansion is **Captain Rhea Solano**. Her executive officer is **Commander Torren Vesh**, who has no interest in hearing how Voyager would have done something until Noah understands how Bozeman does it.

She is a Nova-class scientific vessel, not the original Soyuz-class Bozeman.

Noah's watch responsibilities sit between operations and science support. He discovers the intimate inconvenience of a small crew: everyone knows when you have made a mistake, and you cannot avoid them on the way to breakfast.

On a survey mission in 2382 he trusts a clean sensor model over a technician's uneven local readings.

The model has inherited an outdated calibration.

The resulting error costs the team a day of work and puts equipment at risk. Nobody dies. That does not make the mistake trivial.

Solano makes him brief the correction himself.

He expects humiliation.

He receives questions.

The distinction stays with him.

The holographic projectors in his quarters are wonderful. Ashley can talk to him in a room that feels less like a borrowed compartment.

But a projected room cannot attend a family appointment or replace an absent partner.

When he tells her the technology makes distance easier, she agrees.

When he starts to say it makes distance unimportant, she stops him.

He becomes a lieutenant in February 2383.

He completes his three years on August 31, 2384.

Leaving is harder than arriving.

### 10. The defense

Avalon receives him on September 15, 2384.

Captain Mara Ellison reads his file and asks about the Bozeman calibration mistake before she asks about Voyager.

Noah likes her less for approximately three minutes.

Then he understands.

His doctoral defense follows on December 12.

The research concerns independent evidence under conflicting temporal reference conditions. It is built from theory, historical cases and bounded models, not a claim that he has invented safe time travel.

An examiner asks whether preserving every disagreement makes a system incapable of deciding.

“No,” Noah answers. “It makes it possible to explain what the decision depended on.”

Ashley sits near the back.

Afterward she congratulates him, kisses him, and asks whether the dissertation can finally stop occupying every available surface.

He says he has revisions.

She closes her eyes.

Their family learns that achievement does not always make a person less inconvenient.

### 11. Investigations

Ellison gives him ordinary operational duties and occasional temporal investigations.

One case involves two captains whose logs describe apparently incompatible arrivals at the same navigation marker.

Noah initially suspects a temporal event.

A junior analyst discovers that one timestamp was converted twice.

He credits her in the opening paragraph.

Another case is harder. An instrument survives an incident, but its correction process has overwritten the original readings. The remaining evidence cannot decide between competing explanations.

The requesting admiral wants a conclusion.

Noah submits an unresolved finding with recommended reproduction work.

Ellison asks whether he is prepared to defend it.

“Yes.”

“Then remove the defensive language. Let the evidence do its job.”

He becomes a lieutenant commander in March 2387.

His work with Janeway-derived technology is equally bounded. He understands the operational significance of systems brought back aboard Voyager and participates in authorized reviews of their integration. He does not gain a drawer full of future solutions.

Some designs remain restricted. Some components behave differently outside the conditions in which they were first used.

B'Elanna's old question returns: what happens when the sensor is wrong?

In 2390 Ellison appoints him first officer.

The mysteries continue.

So do leave requests, training failures, personnel disagreements and a crewman's quiet request for help.

He finds that the latter can require more courage from everyone involved.

### 12. The family years

Their children grow within a household that has schedules, interruptions, pets, arguments and affection. This chapter adds no new birth certificates or biological histories to established family records.

Noah learns that a conversation cannot always be resumed exactly where he left it after taking a call.

Ashley learns to stop saying she can manage something when she needs him to take responsibility for it.

They make mistakes. They apologize. Some arguments recur because understanding a problem is not the same as changing a habit.

There are dinners where the uniforms remain on because neither has had time to change.

There are evenings when Noah explains an investigation until somebody asks whether the conclusion includes eating.

There are family projects that take longer than either captain predicted.

There are school matters, plans, ordinary disappointments and small triumphs that have nothing to do with temporal mechanics.

He loves those things.

He sometimes needs reminding to be present for them.

Ashley's station career advances because she is good at her work. She becomes a commander in 2391, moves into senior Jupiter Station operations in 2392, and becomes executive officer in 2394.

Her commanding officer during that final period is **Captain Amara Vel**, newly introduced here as her predecessor. Vel values Ashley's willingness to bring a problem with both its immediate remedy and the longer-term burden attached.

When research wants another laboratory, Ashley asks which service will move.

When a visiting ship wants priority, she asks what has actually become urgent.

The station's civilian administrators learn that she will listen without promising that listening means agreement.

### 13. The center seat

September 3, 2394. 1000.

Ellison stands beside Avalon's command chair.

The ceremony is brief because a working ship cannot spend an entire morning admiring its own continuity.

Noah accepts command.

Afterward, in private, he thanks her.

“You'll dislike me occasionally over the next few years,” she says.

“Why?”

“Because you'll discover which things I made look easier than they were.”

His first day as captain includes a disagreement about maintenance time.

He grants Engineering the additional hours.

His second day includes somebody telling him that this decision has disrupted another department's work.

The chair is his.

The consequences arrive immediately.

The earlier proposed 2387 appointment stays in the archive as a development state. This version places the actual appointment in 2394. The years have been served.

### 14. The chair I remember

I must be careful when I tell the next part.

It is easy to make my own transformation sound inevitable because I know what came afterward.

It was not inevitable to the people standing there.

In 2395, Avalon's first major temporal refit led into the catastrophe already recorded in our governing history. The bounded discrepancy and the first full activation retain their separate accounts. The full cascade threatened Sol.

The Captain refused the Corona Vent proposal.

I returned to the Cytherian interface.

I lost my biological body.

The man who had gone into that crisis continued, but almost nothing about his ordinary life was ordinary afterward.

Noah did not solve that by declaring me useful.

Ashley did not solve it by being kind once.

The Doctor, the crew, and my friends had to keep making room for the person I remained.

There were engineering limits and questions of privacy. There were things I could do that frightened me and things I could no longer do in the way I remembered.

The Captain sometimes asked a question of the computer and heard me answer.

Then he learned to ask whether I was on duty.

That mattered more than he probably realized.

### 15. Ashley's station

On November 1, 2396, at 0900, Captain Ashley Hawkes accepted command of Jupiter Station from Captain Amara Vel.

Noah attended as her husband and a fellow captain.

He did not confer the appointment.

Her first command address concerned continuity of station services during a research expansion. It was not glamorous. The people responsible for keeping children warm and patients treated listened closely.

In January 2397, the Hawkes family settled into its Deck Nine home.

Ashley chose cupboard handles she could use while carrying something.

Noah admired the view.

She reminded him that somebody also had to decide where the dishes went.

Avalon lay beyond the glass, a ship with a history. Jupiter moved behind her. The station's corridors carried people whose days would continue whether the Captain went exploring or stayed home.

That was the life they had built.

Two commands. A family. Years that could not honestly be reduced to a rank and a destination.

### 16. Dinner, 2397

The Captain asked me once what I thought his service record was missing.

We were in the quarters. Someone had left a school PADD beside a fabrication drawing. Ashley was speaking to Operations from the kitchen.

“Nothing essential,” I said.

He looked disappointed.

“That is not the same as complete,” I added.

“What would you put in?”

I considered the Academy courses completed after an emergency watch. The failed exercise. The first report Paris returned. The doorway in Utah. Three years aboard a small ship. Ashley making transport space for passengers who could not wait. The arguments that had ended in an apology rather than a victory.

“The parts that explain why the appointments meant something.”

Ashley ended her call.

“If you're writing his full history,” she said, “include the part where he promised to help with dinner.”

The Captain stood.

“I was coming.”

“I know. I've been preserving the claim.”

He pointed at her.

“You've been talking to Merlin.”

“I command a station, Noah. I can recognize an unsupported statement.”

I laughed.

For a moment nobody needed a theory of time.

They needed another plate.

**End.**

### New development ledger

All dialogue and scene staging in this chapter are newly authored. New supporting characters: Mara Chen, Davin Rell, Sera Nadir, Commander Lena Varek, Captain Rhea Solano, Commander Torren Vesh and Captain Amara Vel. Their roles are established for this commissioned version; this does not assert that they were in earlier notebooks or franchise episodes.

New mission incidents: the Havoc casualty exercise; shipboard gauge disagreement; Ashley's passenger-priority scheduling incident; the Bozeman calibration error; double-converted navigation timestamp; lost raw-instrument investigation. These develop professional experience without claiming participation in an unverified named franchise event.

The original Q mechanism, raw manuscript authentication, Year of Hell memory retention and exact technical cause of the temporal catastrophes remain governed by their separate source records. This story does not solve them accidentally.

The ship remembers. The Captain decides.
