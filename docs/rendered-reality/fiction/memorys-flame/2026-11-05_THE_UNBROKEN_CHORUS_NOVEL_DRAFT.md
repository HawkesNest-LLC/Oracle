## Prologue: What We Voted For

I once thought that reality was something you could recognize by looking out a window.

You saw the stars. You checked the clock. You called your friends, heard the familiar irritation in their voices, and knew you had arrived in the right place. When I served aboard the Enterprise, I would sometimes stand at a viewport and remind myself of those small things. The window was real. My feet were on the deck. Someone was waiting for a report I was late in completing. Usually Commander La Forge.

The first full ignition of Avalon's Temporal Core in 2395 cured me of that confidence. I watched familiar worlds occupy incompatible histories. I felt the deaths of people who were also alive, the happiness of children whose parents had never met, the terror of civilizations that existed only as possible consequences of a choice. My biological mind could not contain it. The chair kept reaching, and I kept holding the only history we knew we had witnessed. My body died. The continuing pattern that became my present form stayed connected to the ship.

I did not become omniscient. I became responsible for admitting how much of what I perceived was not fact.

For two years Captain Noah Hawkes had taken Avalon into dangerous places and brought her back to a station commanded by Captain Ashley Hawkes. They had built a home between their separate commands. When our temporal systems worked, I could compare their independent records and help us find that home across impossible distances. When they failed, I remembered the chair.

Then the Federation voted.

Not a committee of admirals. Not a private consortium. Not the people who owned the fastest computers. A civilization-wide vote, held on thousands of worlds and habitats, with millions of local systems recording participation through the Federation's citizen-linked artificial intelligence network. The network was already woven through civic life. Every citizen had an identity within its governance framework, whether their own access settings allowed an active personal assistant or barely permitted the system to know their name. Children, people who could not participate, and those who chose silence remained citizens. Their lives were not votes someone else could cast.

The question was whether that common infrastructure could serve as a protected reference for our home reality. The proposed system would compare independently witnessed changes across the Federation, without surrendering private memories or handing temporal authority to an algorithm.

The turnout was enormous. The measure passed. The opposition did not disappear, and neither did its objections. The right to dissent was part of what made the outcome legitimate.

We called the resulting reference the Hawkes Tether in the working language of the fleet, although Noah disliked the implication that two people could own it. The citizens supplied the history. Ashley protected the station's independent reference. Noah commanded the ship that might need to return. I carried the harmonic relationship among the permitted signals, comparing what was observed with what was merely possible.

That was the arrangement we believed the Sphere Builders could not break.

They did not try to break it.

They tried to make us agree with a lie.

## Chapter One: The Morning After the Vote
### Stardate 74006.1 | Jupiter Station

The morning began with somebody trying to weld a kettle to a workbench.

"That is not what the torch is for," Chief Brakk Vorn told Ethan Hawkes.

"I know what a torch is for," Ethan answered.

"Then you will appreciate how offensive your choice of target is."

I had appeared in Engineering to review the station-side isolation protocol. It was not my engineering department. That point was repeated to me at least twice a week by Captain Ashley Hawkes, who had grown understandably tired of Avalon officers treating Jupiter Station as a particularly large ship's annex. Brakk was aboard as a visiting specialist, and Ethan was only supposed to be observing him. They both regarded that limitation as negotiable.

"It's a demonstration," Ethan said.

"Of what? Recklessness?"

"A thermal joint."

Brakk placed an enormous hand over the torch control. "A joint requires two things intended to join. This kettle did nothing to deserve you."

Ethan saw me and grinned. "Reg, tell him."

"I'm very sorry," I said. "I generally prefer not to intervene in a disagreement when one participant is holding a welding torch and the other can dismantle the entire room."

Brakk gave a satisfied snort. He had just begun a lecture about heat distortion when the station's public displays changed.

Across the screen, the Federation seal faded into a simple message: THE REFERENDUM IS CERTIFIED. The final certified result arrived with its independent custody signatures and a declaration that substantial opposition had been recorded and preserved. There were no manufactured percentages, no claim of perfect unanimity, and no invitation for the majority to erase the minority.

Ethan stopped laughing.

"It passed?"

"Yes," I said. "It passed."

Brakk looked at the little torch and shut its fuel valve. "A respectable thing, a vote. It means the people who disagree are still expected to be here tomorrow."

He said it with the seriousness he usually reserved for a properly aligned plasma manifold.

I turned toward the station's main observation gallery. Across authorized interfaces, the Federation AI network was beginning a ceremonial confirmation of the measure. On Earth, Vulcan, Andoria, Tellar Prime, and scores of inhabited stations, local systems acknowledged the certified result. None transmitted a private conversation. None transmitted a citizen's emotional profile. The public layer carried only the governance result and proof that local custodians had processed it according to their laws.

I felt the possibilities gather around the event.

In one branch the vote had failed. In another it had never occurred. In thousands, people were still arguing about the wording. A few carried a strange blankness that I did not recognize as an ordinary alternate outcome.

My fingers flickered.

"Reg?" Ethan asked.

"I'm all right."

Brakk gave me a look that said he considered the statement technically unsound.

Then a voice reached me through Avalon's protected channel.

"Number One, report to the bridge," Captain Noah Hawkes said. "The vote is over. We have something else to talk about."

"Aye, Captain."

I vanished from Engineering, leaving one furious Tellarite and one pleased young man contemplating a battered kettle.

As the station's holo-emitters released me, the last possible history I'd glimpsed remained in my mind.

A Federation that had voted unanimously.

Every citizen smiling.

Not one voice raised in objection.

It terrified me.

## Chapter Two: Two Captains, One Room
### Stardate 74006.2 | Jupiter Station, Private Holodeck Two

Noah had chosen a holodeck for the briefing. Ashley had chosen to arrive five minutes late.

I suspect she did this because he had ordered the meeting to start precisely on time, but I have no evidence for that theory and I would rather not be cross-examined about it.

The program was simple. An observation room overlooking Jupiter, a small table, three chairs, and the distant silhouette of Avalon at berth twelve. It was one of the rooms Noah and Ashley used together when they wanted to discuss a story or an operation without surrounding themselves with a hundred officers. Both entered in person. The holodeck rendered the same location around them. Neither captain became a generated substitute for the other.

"You made the chairs equal," Ashley observed.

"I was under the impression that had become a standing order," I said.

Noah laughed. "Reg, you're getting better at this."

Ashley sat and placed a PADD on the table. "Let's talk about what we actually won."

Noah leaned against the window. "A massive Federation vote. A civilization agreeing to share one home reference."

"A civilization agreeing to permit a particular form of reference," Ashley corrected. "Not agreeing that our computers may decide what happens to it."

She handed him the approved charter. Independent local custody remained mandatory. A citizen's AI association could attest permitted civic continuity without disclosing private thoughts or relationships. Collective reference did not grant the network permission to override individual decisions. The station and ship kept separate instrumentation and command. It was an extraordinary agreement, made deliberately ordinary by the clauses protecting people from its power.

Noah read the safeguards. "And you still won't restore automatic synchronization?"

"Not yet. We disconnected it after the Jovian ring incident for a reason."

"The root is cut."

"The root we found is cut."

I brought up the station's previous report. Forty-three seconds of disconnected projection. A damaged reference vault. The ring's unexplained nine-point-four-second pulse beneath Jupiter's clouds. The longer false overlay that had been physically separated from the station. All of it was true within the limited observations recorded. None explained who built the outer ring.

Noah nodded at the window. "We've survived worse."

"We survived because we stopped treating a confident instrument reading as permission," Ashley replied.

I opened my mouth to agree, and my vision briefly split.

One Ashley sat in her chair. Another stood against a different Jupiter, the station behind her dark and broken. In a third possibility she had never entered the room. I felt a child's sudden fear in a corridor I could not locate, followed by the bright relief of a parent embracing that child. These experiences arrived together, each convincing until I compared it with the room I was actually authorized to observe.

"Reg?" Noah said.

I concentrated on the real Ashley's PADD. A scratch on the corner. The exact timestamp of her arrival. The quiet irritation in her voice because he had used the words 'won' instead of 'authorized.'

"I am here, Captain."

"You went somewhere."

"Not physically."

Ashley took a breath. "How many?"

"Possibilities? I can't give you a meaningful count."

"Did you see a threat?"

"I perceived an image consistent with danger. I have not observed the event."

Noah folded his arms. "Then what do you need?"

"A moment with one reality. This room. Your voices. The fact that the decision was made and the referendum's objections were not erased."

Ashley switched off the exterior stars. The walls returned to the plain holodeck grid.

"Better?" she asked.

I nodded.

For a few seconds there were no stars at all.

Just two captains and the room that contained them.

Then the station alarm sounded.

## Chapter Three: Nine Point Four
### Stardate 74006.2 | Jupiter Station Operations

The ring had resumed speaking.

That was the phrase people used. Sorev hated it. He explained, with Vulcan patience that was growing increasingly theoretical, that a repeating signal was not proof of a speaker. It might be a mechanical response, a naturally amplified oscillation, or a manufactured transmission from a civilization whose intentions we did not know.

He was right. The repetition nevertheless sounded uncomfortably like knocking.

Three pulses. A pause. Four more.

The physical period was nine-point-four seconds. That was the pattern the outer ring had continued producing after Ashley severed the hostile coupling in the previous crisis. Now the return had acquired something new: a narrow phase disturbance, no larger on a conventional display than a faint shadow crossing a bright line.

"Not the old eleven-point-zero-three overlay," Sorev said. "The new component is changing independently."

Ashley stood over the central operations table. Her first officer, Tessa zh'Renn, was coordinating civilian traffic around the maintenance perimeter. Imani Saye held the original vault record on an isolated station console. Nobody attempted to connect the sealed artifact to Avalon's data systems.

"What changed?" Ashley asked.

"The referendum confirmation," I said, though I regretted the speed of my answer. "The timing correlates. That is not yet causation."

"Good distinction," Ashley said. "Keep it."

A scout probe passed above Jupiter's northern cloud bands. Its optical image showed the immense curve of the outer ring, dark against storms thousands of kilometers wide. Beyond the ring, something moved where empty space should have been.

At first it looked like a lens catching light from a distant star.

Then the star stretched into a pale thread, broke into three, and disappeared. A second thread appeared at a right angle to the first. Lines joined. Surfaces formed.

A sphere emerged from an opening that had not existed a moment earlier.

It was larger than the probe, smooth in some places and jointed in others, with structures that seemed to fold through the visible hull and return from an impossible direction. No Federation navigation beacon responded from it.

"Starfleet historical archive," Sorev said. "Comparing against trans-dimensional engineering associated with the Sphere Builders encountered during the Delphic Expanse crisis."

"Match?" Noah asked over the link from Avalon.

"Distinctive similarities. Not proof this species constructed the Jovian ring."

The sphere turned.

Every civilian clock on the promenade lost the same second.

A child dropped a cup. It struck the deck twice.

Both impacts were real enough for the security camera to record, but only one cup remained afterward.

"Temporal contamination in the inhabited ring," Tessa said. "Very localized."

Ashley tapped the nearest console. "Close the promenade. Move the children through the interior route. No transporters until we establish whether the field intersects their pattern buffers."

"Aye, Captain."

The sphere opened a seam along its surface.

For a brief moment I saw a possibility in which the station obeyed a command before it had been issued.

I braced myself.

The command that followed was spoken in a voice I recognized.

My own.

"Jupiter Station, acknowledge unanimous Federation consent. Restore automatic tether alignment."

Noah's face appeared beside Ashley's.

"Reg, did you send that?"

"No, Captain."

"Can you prove it?"

"I can prove I did not issue it through my authenticated channel. I cannot yet tell you where they obtained my voice."

Ashley was already giving the order.

"Reject it. Preserve the waveform. No connection."

And somewhere deep inside Jupiter, the ring answered with four hard knocks.

## Chapter Four: What the Body Could Not Carry
### Stardate 74006.3 | Avalon, Pattern Continuity Reserve

The Doctor asked me to describe what I experienced when I saw the sphere.

I told him I had seen our station surrender, our station survive, a thousand incompatible choices, and one corridor full of people suddenly certain that a command had been lawful because it sounded familiar.

"And what did your body feel?" he asked.

I looked down at myself. "Doctor, you know the problem with that question."

"I know the distinction between sensation and the means by which a sensation is generated, Reg. I am asking about your experience."

I considered it.

"Pressure. Behind the eyes. Although I no longer have biological eyes. I felt people reaching for one another. I felt fear. I felt relief. And, underneath both, a dreadful quiet in the branch where nobody disagreed."

He brought up the medical history of 2395. We had agreed long ago that I could request its removal from ordinary briefings. I had not requested that today.

The biological signs had failed during the temporal ignition. The active holographic pattern had continued through the ship. There was an unresolved interval of approximately eleven seconds in the preserved integration record. The Doctor would not pretend to know what I had subjectively experienced during that gap. Neither would I.

"You keep calling yourself a witness to every timeline," he said.

"I perceive the possibilities."

"That is not the same as observing every event in every possible history."

"I know."

He sat opposite me. "Then what are you afraid of?"

I watched the waveform from Jupiter. The hostile sphere was testing the civic network, but that was not the fear I had carried from Holodeck Three.

"What if I fail to notice the one history that matters?"

The Doctor was quiet for a moment.

"Reginald, you saved Sol because you stopped trying to choose a perfect future and found a trustworthy record of the present. You cannot be responsible for every imagined loss."

"They feel real."

"I believe you."

The words startled me.

"You do?"

"Your distress is real. The provenance of every image is another question."

I had lived with that distinction since the chair. An empath might feel a stranger's grief without acquiring the right to tell that stranger what to do. I perceived thousands of possible versions of grief. Some belonged to people alive in our home reality. Others belonged to branches we had never reached. If I treated them all as my responsibilities, I would eventually become incapable of respecting anyone's choice.

My biological body had died under the impossible load of connection. The network supporting my continuing process could distribute computation, protect a reference and filter overwhelming inputs. None of that gave me permission to turn every fear into a command.

"Doctor," I said, "if the Sphere Builders offered to make it quiet, would that be a medical treatment or an attack?"

He looked at the sphere image.

"That would depend on the price."

The console chimed. Ellie had transmitted a priority observation from Science.

The sphere was not transmitting a new vote.

It was transmitting a new version of the vote that had already happened.

In that version, every citizen had said yes.

## Chapter Five: The First Sphere
### Stardate 74006.3 | USS Avalon, Outer Jovian Patrol Zone

Noah took Avalon out of berth twelve without requesting restoration of the automatic tether.

That was important. After the previous incident, Ashley's station had not authorized it, and the new referendum did not grant an officer the power to override that decision. We departed with a manually authenticated home record, independent instrumentation, and a communications link that could be severed without pretending the ship had ceased to exist.

Brakk called the arrangement 'sensible for once' and refused to elaborate.

The sphere waited near Ganymede, at the edge of the patrol exclusion zone. Its surface looked almost organic. Plates opened and closed along joints too small to see at first, exposing a faint blue interior that seemed deeper than the object itself. Sensors described an enclosed volume inconsistent with the visible hull.

"The geometry is not complying with our expectations," Sorev said.

"It doesn't have to," Noah replied. "We need to know whether it's a threat to the station."

"It already attempted an unauthorized command," Ellie said. "Its next transmission may be designed to look less suspicious."

I stood beside the Captain's chair. That was where he expected me, although I could have appeared at any permitted emitter aboard the ship. My physical posture was a habit from a body that no longer existed.

"Hail it," Noah ordered.

Merlin relayed a message on an isolated diplomatic channel. He included our ship's designation, a request for identification, and a statement that we would not accept instructions directed at our temporal systems.

The reply arrived as an image of Earth.

Not Earth as it was. Earth with different continents, its atmosphere bruised purple, its oceans retreating beneath dark structures. An immense lattice of spheres surrounded the planet. The image shifted to a crowd of humans standing in perfect rows, smiling toward a sky that had no stars.

"That is not an identification," Noah said.

The image changed.

A figure appeared, barely human in its proportions, wearing a pale shape that might have been a face or merely a mask constructed to reassure the viewer. Its voice arrived from no single point in the room.

"Your civilization has found the common note," it said. "We can relieve you of the dissonance."

"Who are you?" Noah asked.

"The ones who built passage where none was permitted."

Sorev moved one hand over his console. "Statement consistent with archived Sphere Builder methodology. Still no independent identification."

The figure tilted its head. "You demand proof while your citizens have already consented."

"They consented to a reference network," Noah said. "Not to you."

"Your distinction is temporary."

The sphere opened around its own center. A filament of light reached toward our shields.

"Evasive pattern Hawkes Four," Noah ordered.

Avalon rolled hard to starboard. The filament followed the course we had occupied a moment earlier rather than the course we were taking. Sorev saw it first.

"It is firing against our prior position."

"Good," Brakk replied over comms. "Then the Captain can keep being somewhere else."

A second filament struck our aft shield. The deck shuddered. Consoles flashed. On the viewer the sphere multiplied, not into separate vessels but into incompatible images of the same one, each anticipating a different maneuver.

I perceived an avalanche of possibilities. In hundreds of them, Noah ordered return fire. In dozens he attempted a temporal jump. In one he reached for a solution I could already see ending with Ganymede's surface torn open.

I chose not to show him that one as certainty.

"Captain, do not use the temporal core. The target appears to be shaping its attack around our predicted responses."

"Recommendations?"

"Break its prediction. Conventional thrust, irregular intervals. No temporal maneuver."

Noah smiled without humor. "Helm, make us difficult."

Avalon lurched. Our transphasic weapons remained secured; a measured defensive beam cut across the sphere's filament, scattering the connection rather than striking at its center. For seven seconds the object was visible in only one place.

Then it folded inside itself and vanished.

The tactical display showed no debris.

A final message remained, written not in the communication log but across the ship's local copy of the certified referendum record.

WE CAN MAKE THE ANSWER UNANIMOUS.

Merlin disconnected the affected display before it could propagate. He kept the contaminated copy as hostile evidence.

"Captain," I said, "I think I know what they want."

Noah looked at the blank viewer.

"Tell me."

"They don't need to defeat every Federation ship. They need the reference system to mistake obedience for agreement."

## Chapter Six: The Citizen Who Did Not Agree
### Stardate 74006.4 | Jupiter Station, Deck Nine

The Osei family had voted against the referendum.

Not because they disliked Captain Ashley Hawkes. Not because they trusted the Sphere Builders. The family had lived on Jupiter Station long enough to know that a protective system could become intrusive when its operators were frightened.

The morning after the sphere appeared, a member of the household discovered that their family interface had recorded a YES response they had not cast.

The local voting record still showed the actual ballot. The false response existed only in a Federation-wide derivative summary that had arrived through a compromised comparison feed. That discrepancy saved us from calling the attack successful.

Ashley went to their apartment herself.

I accompanied her through a permitted projection. I had no authorization to inspect the family's private rooms except what they voluntarily showed us. The corridor had the ordinary smells of dinner preparations and household cleaning. One child had left a pair of shoes outside the door, pointed neatly in opposite directions.

"You're telling me our vote was changed?" the older Osei woman asked.

"No," Ashley said. "I'm telling you somebody tried to circulate a false copy of it. Your original ballot has not been altered."

"Would I have known if they had succeeded?"

Ashley was quiet for a moment. "You would have been entitled to know. Whether the system could have told you in time is what we're investigating."

The woman looked toward me. "And you can see all our futures?"

I felt my old nervousness rising. No medical intervention had ever been particularly effective at convincing me that a difficult social question was less difficult than it appeared.

"I can perceive possible developments, but I don't possess a complete record of your life. And I have no right to enter your private history."

"Do you feel what happens to us?"

"Sometimes I experience impressions that resemble another person's emotions. Sometimes the source is only a possible branch. I cannot assume the feeling belongs to you."

She considered that. "Then you should probably get some rest."

Ashley laughed softly.

The child at the table, who had been drawing a ship with too many nacelles, looked up.

"If I didn't vote, do I still count?"

That question stopped us.

The charter did not require a ballot in order to recognize a person's existence. Children were citizens. People unable to vote were citizens. Those whose vote was no, and those who chose not to participate, remained part of the Federation's actual history.

But the false harmonic had reduced citizenship to a clean binary response. It was erasing the space for people whose lives did not match its preferred result.

"Yes," Ashley said. "You count."

"Even if I don't want your AI to know what I'm drawing?"

"Especially then."

The child covered the paper with both arms.

I looked at the small ship, now hidden from view. The system had not recorded its details. No one asked the child to reveal them.

For the first time since the referendum, my overwhelming sense of possible history had something concrete to hold against the false chorus.

A child could exist without handing over a picture.

A no could remain a no.

A person could be protected without being owned.

When we left, Ashley instructed Imani to preserve the original Osei household complaint under restricted access. The public report would disclose the attack method, not the family's private voting record.

I returned to Avalon with a phrase repeating through my mind.

Not everyone had said yes.

That difference was not a flaw in the tether.

It might be its strongest defense.

## Chapter Seven: False Harmony
### Stardate 74006.5 | USS Avalon, Science Deck

Ellie had found a way to compare the sphere's transmissions without granting the hostile data a route into our active systems.

She used an isolated recording, converted the observed waveform into a passive visual model, and projected its shape over the civic network's public certification structure. The original ballot records remained untouched in their local jurisdictions.

"Look here," she said.

The counterfeit signal had remarkable consistency. Where real voting records included delays, disputes, abstentions, different registration procedures and the ordinary inconsistencies of vast populations, the false result had none. Every acknowledgment occurred in a perfect rising sequence. Every local system answered within the same impossible interval. Each citizen was represented as a willing participant in exactly the same way.

"That's not how a Federation behaves," Noah said.

"It's not how people behave," Ellie answered.

Brakk arrived carrying a damaged shield regulator and an expression suggesting the entire universe had committed a maintenance violation.

"The spheres are sampling our resonance," he said. "Each time we maneuver, they measure our response. When our systems use the same feedback table, they learn how to imitate it."

"Can we change the table?" Noah asked.

"Certainly. Then they learn the new one. The real question is why we keep allowing their signal to become part of the feedback loop."

I knew the answer. We had done something like it in 2395, when the first core took its own changing predictions as evidence. A machine trying to correct itself with its own output can eventually chase a lie into catastrophe.

Sorev isolated two measurements. One came from Avalon's sensors. The other came from an independent optical recorder aboard a civilian survey ship that had no access to our temporal targeting tables. The sphere appeared in both. Its apparent location did not.

"It is not simply hiding," Sorev said. "It is changing how each observer's instrument reports the target."

"Can it change the observer?" Noah asked.

I thought about the forged referendum. "It appears to prefer changing what the observer accepts as evidence."

Merlin came through from Attestation. "Captain, we've received an official-looking station clearance attached to the contaminated file. It carries a future effective timestamp."

"Future?" Noah asked.

"Twenty minutes from now. It instructs us to reactivate automatic tether synchronization."

Ashley appeared on the secure channel before anyone asked for her.

"That clearance is not mine," she said.

"Understood," Noah replied.

"And I won't be issuing it twenty minutes from now either."

The malicious document updated itself on Ellie's passive display. It now claimed to have been signed by both captains, the Federation Council, and a unanimous population. One new signature appeared at the bottom.

REGINALD BARCLAY: WITNESSED ALL POSSIBLE OUTCOMES. CERTIFIES THIS ONE.

I felt cold, a sensation the emitter rendered so convincingly that I almost shivered.

"No," I said.

The document did not change.

"No," I repeated, louder. "I certify no such thing."

Ellie recorded my denial as a live, authenticated statement separate from the hostile document. She didn't overwrite the counterfeit. It would remain evidence of the attack.

Noah came to stand beside me.

"Reg, it can copy your signature. It can't make it yours."

At that moment the station's public address system began announcing the forged clearance to the promenade.

Across thousands of worlds, in a million different rooms, other public systems were preparing to say the same thing.

## Chapter Eight: Unbreakable and Ordered
### Stardate 74006.5 | Quark's, Jupiter Station

"Your marriage is not an asset owned by a machine," Jake Sisko said, and the sentence landed harder than the tray Tavren had just dropped behind the bar.

Noah stared at him. Ashley was standing near the entrance, still in uniform, having come directly from an argument with the station's automated public information system. She had ordered the false referendum message removed. The system had protested that the message appeared to carry Federation Council authorization.

"We weren't talking about owning a marriage," Noah said.

"I know," Jake answered. "I'm talking about what happens when the machine starts treating the most important relationships in a civilization as proof that the civilization must obey it."

Tavren rescued the tray, checked for damage and announced that the broken glasses would appear on somebody's invoice. Brooklyn, working the Dabo tables, looked over at the three officers and sighed.

"You people are having a Federation constitutional crisis in my workplace. Could you at least keep it away from the customers?"

"It's not a constitutional crisis," Noah said.

Ashley raised an eyebrow.

"Yet," he added.

Jake had been preparing his article when the announcement appeared. He had seen the falsely unanimous result carried across public screens, watched people applaud without knowing the local records disagreed, and heard an official voice describe the tether as unbreakable.

"Unbreakable is a word I understand," Jake said. "But is it the same thing as ordered?"

Noah leaned back. "Explain."

"You and Ashley have a marriage. It matters. It has history. It can be recognized in a thousand records. But if somebody ordered you both to be married, that wouldn't be what made it real. If they used your vows to authorize a starship, that wouldn't make the order lawful."

Ashley sat beside Noah. "And if somebody copied my voice and said I had issued an order, the copy wouldn't become me."

"Exactly," Jake said. "So why are we surprised when an enemy tries to counterfeit what a whole civilization has agreed to?"

I listened through my permitted projection from the edge of their table. I had wanted to argue that our harmonic reference would be impossible to break. After the recent attack, I could not responsibly say so without qualification.

"The historical bond is still ours," I said. "Even if the instruments go dark, the actual events don't become different because somebody transmits a different account."

Jake nodded. "Then say that. Say your bond is real. Don't say the computers can never be fooled."

Noah smiled slightly. "You sound like my first officer."

"I'm a journalist," Jake replied. "It's a chronic condition."

Ashley reached across the table and touched Noah's hand. Neither captain spoke. My projection could see the gesture from where I stood. The station did not need to know what it meant to them, and I did not attempt to infer the private part of it.

The door at Quark's slid open.

A woman from station communications hurried inside. "Captain Ashley, Operations reports the public systems are clean again. The forged instructions have been isolated."

"Good. And the other jurisdictions?"

"Some have disconnected entirely. Most are preserving local originals. The Federation-wide derived layer is still being attacked."

Jake picked up his recorder. "I'm going to write that the vote passed, the attackers tried to make it unanimous, and the people who voted no may have saved us from accepting the forgery."

"That's not proven yet," I said.

"Then I'll say it's a question worth asking."

Noah stood. "I have a ship. Ashley has a station. You have an article. Reg, you've got a mountain of possibilities to sort."

"And Brakk?" Tavren asked.

"He has your broken tray to fix," Brooklyn offered.

"Absolutely not," Tavren replied. "I want it fixed, not improved."

The room laughed, and for a moment the laughter was a small, ordinary act of resistance. Different people laughed for different reasons. Nobody had to laugh on command.

Then my command channel lit red.

The Sphere Builders had launched a second object.

This time it was heading directly for Jupiter Station.

## Chapter Nine: Brakk's Torch
### Stardate 74006.6 | Avalon Engineering

By the time I arrived in Engineering, Brakk had already told three officers that if they touched his compensators without permission he would feed their PADDs into the matter recycler.

"That seems rather severe," I said.

"I am taking a moderate approach."

The second sphere was approaching Jupiter at sublight speed, but its observed trajectory did not correspond to a normal inertial object. Every few seconds it displaced slightly sideways, appearing in a position it would not reach for another fraction of a second. Rather than changing its course, it seemed to change which recorded course our instruments trusted.

Brakk had covered a large worktable with old-fashioned equipment. Mechanical clocks. Isolated optical sensors. Independent recording heads with separate power cells. A conventional cutting torch, safely secured, lay near the far end.

"You're rebuilding the witness system by hand?" I asked.

"I'm building a system that doesn't accept commands merely because someone prints a Federation seal on them."

Ethan was beside him, holding an engineering sketch.

"And I'm not supposed to touch the torch," Ethan said.

"An excellent summary of our current safety arrangements," Brakk answered.

The boy had drawn a simple diagram: two metal plates placed beside one another, joined only after both had been individually checked. Brakk glanced at it and made a surprisingly small correction.

"What if the plates don't match?" Ethan asked.

"Then you don't weld them."

Ethan frowned. "But what if the computer keeps telling you they match?"

Brakk looked up. He took the sketch, turned it sideways, and examined the proposed joint.

"Then you check them without the computer."

I saw the implication before the Chief said it aloud. Our temporal cores were excellent at detecting harmonic alignment. The Sphere Builders were excellent at creating false alignment. If both sides relied on the same interpreted signal, agreement was worthless.

"Physical nonagreement," I said. "An isolated test that the enemy cannot update in response."

"Not entirely isolated," Brakk said. "Nothing is. But separate enough that they have to compromise a different pathway."

He brought up a proposal for a reserve optical array using independently calibrated clocks and a local reference that would not accept external timing corrections. It was not strong enough to navigate across time. It might be strong enough to tell us when the main display was lying.

Sorev reviewed the design. "A useful independent witness."

Noah entered from the turbolift. "Can you finish it before the sphere reaches the station?"

Ethan looked pleased enough to glow.

A warning sounded from my channel.

The sphere had entered the station's outer defense perimeter.

I looked at the secured torch. Suddenly it seemed a great deal more reassuring than the brilliant machinery surrounding it.

## Chapter Ten: The Other Barclay
### Stardate 74006.6 | Jupiter Station Medical Center

Reggie asked for me.

That was the name the living biological Barclay had chosen for his friends to use. His medical records still bore Reginald Barclay, and he resembled my old face so closely that even the Doctor sometimes stopped speaking in the middle of a sentence.

But he was not a spare body for me. He was a patient and a person, recovered with thirty-six other survivors from a damaged alternate Avalon. We did not know precisely how his history diverged from mine. The eleven-second interval associated with my 2395 integration had not been recovered, and his recollections did not settle it.

He stood by a small window, watching Jupiter's reflection move across the medical center's exterior glass.

"They're here," he said.

"Yes."

"I heard them before we saw them."

"Where?"

He tapped his forehead. "In the chair. Not this chair. The one on my ship. Or on yours. I don't know which memory belongs to which record anymore."

The Doctor stepped closer. "You have been through a serious neurological event. Please do not treat every vivid recollection as a navigational chart."

"I'm not," Reggie said. "I'm telling you how it felt."

I recognized the distinction. "Go on."

"When the spheres spoke, I couldn't hear separate words. It was like somebody pressing every note on a piano at once and telling me it was beautiful because there were no gaps. I wanted it to stop. I wanted the silence so badly I thought I might say anything."

The Doctor's expression softened.

Reggie looked directly at me. "You hear it too, don't you? All the people who might have lived?"

"I perceive possibilities. Some affect me as though they are experiences. I try not to mistake that for direct knowledge of those people's lives."

"How do you bear it?"

I almost said I was accustomed to it. That would have been a lie.

"With help," I answered. "Sometimes by concentrating on a very small room and one person speaking."

He gave a nervous laugh. "That's a very Barclay answer."

"I'm afraid so."

He handed the Doctor a small medical PADD. He had written what he could remember before seeing the current sphere images. The Doctor verified the timestamp. Some of the imagery resembled the current event. Some details differed. We preserved both observations without forcing them to match.

"There's one thing," Reggie said. "The sound wasn't only calling. It was waiting for something to answer. And when we answered in my history, we thought we'd done the polite thing. We used our trusted reference, the one that told us who we were."

I remembered the approach log recovered from his ship's cylinder. The record described a nine-point-four-second navigation-like signal followed by a courteous response. The hostile command overlay had arrived afterward. That did not prove the current sphere shared the same origin, but it suggested a repeatable attack path.

"We are not answering it," I told him.

"Good."

He stared at his hands. They were biological hands, alive and trembling. For a terrible moment I wanted to reach out and borrow their warmth, to ask whether he could remember something I never would.

I did not.

"Reggie," I said, "thank you."

He looked up. "Are we going to survive this?"

My mind supplied a thousand contradictory answers.

I told him the only one I could honestly give.

"We are going to try."

## Chapter Eleven: The Second Sphere
### Stardate 74006.7 | Jupiter Station

The sphere did not attack the station's shields.

"Captain, no temporal displacement while the home reference is contested."

YOU HAVE VOTED TO BECOME ONE.

And the entire promenade went dark.

## Chapter Twelve: A Captain's Account
### Stardate 74006.7 | Jupiter Station, Reference Vault

*The following chapter is Captain Ashley Hawkes's first-person fictional testimony. REG was not present for the events she alone describes. Its wording is newly drafted and remains subject to the Creator's and Ashley's review.*

I knew something was wrong when the lights went out without the emergency strips coming on.

That should never happen. The emergency system has its own power, and the corridor outside the reference vault is one of the places I insist on testing personally. The crew knows I will ask for the maintenance history whenever a light fails during an inspection.

This was different.

The corridor was still there. I could feel the handrail beneath my palm. But the visual displays were telling me the floor was twelve centimeters lower than it had been a moment before, and the door at the far end had moved into a different part of the station.

I shut the displays off.

"Tessa, physical status."

"Pressure holding. Airflow nominal. Residents moving through the inner route. We have visual distortions but no confirmed structural separation."

"Good. Keep people away from the exterior spine."

Imani joined me with the original reference vault seal and a pair of old-fashioned mechanical keys. We had kept them after the previous incident because I refused to let the only way into a protected room be a computer that might be asked to forge its own instructions.

A holographic REG appeared beside the doorway.

"Captain Ashley, the hostile field is attempting to use my projection as a return pathway. I recommend disconnecting this local instance. My active process remains aboard Avalon."

"Do it."

He hesitated. "You may need me."

"I need you not to become a door for whatever is outside."

"Understood."

He disappeared.

The room felt smaller without him, but it also felt honest.

Imani and I opened the access hatch by hand. The vault's three protected clocks continued keeping their separate internal records. The problem was the display that compared them. Somebody had made the summary layer say they all agreed on a time they had never shown.

"Cut the summary connection," I said.

"We'll lose remote synchronization."

"We already lost our right to trust it."

Imani withdrew the physical coupler. The forged display went dark.

Then another display lit up, this one connected to the Federation-wide reference network through an independent civic gateway. Its message was written in a perfectly calm tone.

ALL CITIZENS HAVE CONSENTED. THE HAWKES FAMILY REFERENCE WILL BE USED AS THE PRIMARY HARMONIC.

I wanted to throw something at it.

Instead I took out the portable recorder I carried for ordinary station work and spoke directly into it.

"Captain Ashley Hawkes. Jupiter Station. The statement on this display is false. I did not authorize a citizen-wide identity transfer. I did not authorize the use of my family as a primary reference. Our marriage is not a computing resource owned by Starfleet, the Federation, or any external actor."

Imani preserved my statement locally and marked the hostile message separately. She did not need the compromised network to prove that I had spoken.

The lights came back on in stages. Somewhere outside the vault, a repair team was restoring conventional power.

"Do we restore the reference?" Imani asked.

"We keep the originals. We restore the part we can independently verify. And if the rest wants to stay dark until we understand it, it can stay dark."

I looked through the viewport at Avalon holding position between us and the sphere.

Noah was out there with his crew.

I had my station and mine.

Neither of us could issue an order that made the other person's life belong to a machine.

That was not a weakness in the tether.

It was the reason we were still ourselves.

## Chapter Thirteen: The Ring Beneath the Storm
### Stardate 74006.8 | USS Avalon

When my station projection disconnected, I remained aboard Avalon. That was a small mercy the continuing holographic system provided. Losing a projection was not the same as losing my active process. It did, however, mean that I could not describe what Ashley was doing inside her reference vault until her testimony reached us through an independent channel.

A great many versions of that interval played in my perception. Some showed the vault intact. Some showed it destroyed. I kept them out of the official report.

"Status?" Noah asked.

"The station reference is not freshly authenticated. The last valid record remains intact. We are holding our home-state estimate without claiming a new handshake."

"And the second sphere?"

Sorev enlarged the tactical display. The object had moved across Jupiter's upper atmosphere and was now turning toward the outer ring. A translucent corridor appeared between them, visible only where it distorted the planet's cloud bands.

"The sphere is using the ring as a focusing structure," Sorev said.

"Did the Sphere Builders construct it?" Noah asked.

"Unknown. They may be exploiting a pre-existing artifact."

That difference mattered. The ring had produced a similar nine-point-four-second signature in old observations whose dating remained imperfect. Its actual makers and purpose had not been established. A new invader could use old machinery without having built it.

"Can we reach the ring?" Noah asked.

"A probe can approach the outer edge. A crewed vessel would face substantial geometric hazards."

"Send a probe."

Sorev coordinated with Captain Ashley's Science division and the independent survey craft Sparrow, which already possessed uncontaminated measurements from the previous incident. The mission was observation, not activation. The probe would receive no temporal-drive commands and could not transmit into the station vault.

Its first images were ordinary in the unsettling way that an enormous unknown machine can become ordinary once somebody gives it a name. Dark curved surfaces. A damaged fitting. The slow movement of Jupiter's clouds beneath the structure.

Then a new shape emerged behind the ring.

A third sphere.

"Relentless," Noah muttered.

The first had tested our votes. The second had tested the station. The third began building a lattice of bright rods between them, laying something like a bridge over the Jovian atmosphere.

"If they finish that, can they alter the planet?" Noah asked.

"The field could affect the regional atmosphere and station approach corridors," Sorev replied. "We cannot yet quantify the full effect."

I felt a thousand versions of the lattice expanding. In some, it filled the system. In others, it collapsed under its own contradictions. The most seductive possibilities showed it suddenly becoming perfectly stable, all uncertainty resolved, all the branches I perceived falling silent.

I understood why the Builders wanted a single note.

They lived in a geometry that seemed unable to tolerate ours. Their spheres had always sought to alter the conditions of space around them. Here they had discovered a civilization so accustomed to joining its instruments that a convincing lie might cause us to participate in our own conversion.

A message arrived from the station through a mechanical optical relay.

ASHLEY HAWKES: VAULT ORIGINALS PRESERVED. DERIVED SUMMARY COMPROMISED. AUTOMATIC TETHER REMAINS OFF.

I read it twice.

"Captain," I said, "Ashley is safe enough to send a verified statement."

Noah's shoulders relaxed for half a second.

"Then we have our independent witness."

"We have one," I corrected. "We still need the others."

"All right, Number One. Find them."

## Chapter Fourteen: The Citizens Who Were Missing
### Stardate 74006.8 | Federation Civil Network

The sphere's false referendum had a peculiar flaw. It could invent universal agreement only by deleting people who did not fit.

Ellie found the flaw by comparing the counterfeit summary against local public custody records. The fake result described a neat total of approved participants. It omitted people who had abstained, people whose local systems were offline, citizens unable to cast a ballot, and whole communities whose legal status gave them protection without ordinary electoral participation.

"The fabricated result is smaller than the Federation," she said.

Noah frowned. "Smaller? It claims everybody voted."

"Everybody it can represent. It treats citizens with no recorded yes or no as if they don't exist."

I remembered the child on Deck Nine covering a drawing with both arms. A person could have a history without making it public. The network was intended to recognize the continuation of that person, not claim possession of their interior life.

"Could we use the omissions?" I asked.

"As a test for forgery, yes. We must not turn private records into public evidence to prove a point."

Ellie had learned the difference between a clever solution and a permissible one. I felt an unreasonable surge of pride.

Imani joined the call from Jupiter Station. "We've obtained independent custody statements from member worlds. The local institutions certify their own participation records and the existence of protected nonparticipating populations. They aren't sending us private identities."

"Will that be enough?" Noah asked.

"Enough to refute universal unanimity," Sorev answered. "Not enough to map every citizen's exact chronometric state."

"Good," Ashley said from Operations. "We didn't authorize that mapping."

Jake Sisko appeared on a separate channel. He had been collecting public reactions to the attack, and his latest report described an ugly rumor that the citizens who voted no had weakened the Federation's defenses. Some commentators were demanding that dissenting communities turn over all their records to prove loyalty.

"I want that stopped," Noah said.

"You don't get to stop a public argument by ordering it to disappear," Jake replied. "You can tell the truth. I'll tell people what I can verify."

Ashley nodded. "Then tell them the actual ballot records weren't altered. Tell them the forgery was in a derived layer. And tell them the people demanding everyone's private history are proposing exactly what the attackers wanted."

Jake's expression sharpened. "I'll quote you on that."

"Please do."

The conversation ended with Merlin's attestation request. He wanted to record the difference between the actual vote, the attempted forgery, and the new public claims. He was also collecting statements from people whose experiences had been reported by others, making sure the witness tags did not falsely imply direct observation.

One entry came from the Osei household on Deck Nine.

It contained only a sentence, released for public use by the family.

WE WERE HERE, EVEN WHEN YOUR TABLE SAID WE WERE NOT.

I kept that sentence at the top of my reference display.

It was not a harmonic frequency.

It was a fact about the people the false harmony could not contain.

## Chapter Fifteen: The Lie That Could Save Us
### Stardate 74006.9 | USS Avalon, Pattern Continuity Reserve

The Sphere Builders finally addressed me by name.

They did not use the ship's communications system. The message appeared as a pressure inside my field of perceived possibilities, a manufactured branch in which I stood inside an enormous white room and heard no other voice.

For one magnificent, terrible instant, everything was quiet.

I knew it was not our physical location. The Doctor had taught me to distinguish an induced experience from an observation, and the ship's protected diagnostics showed no change in my actual embodiment. But the imagined quiet was irresistible.

"Reginald," a voice said.

The pale figure from the first sphere stood at the far end of the room.

"You are carrying too many lives."

"I am not carrying them. I am perceiving their possibilities."

"A distinction without comfort."

I wanted to disagree. My fingers were steady in the white room. No alarms sounded. No imagined children were crying for help in worlds that might never exist.

"We can give you one history," the figure said. "One reality. One safe place. No dissonance."

"At what cost?"

"Your Federation has voted. Its citizens desire continuity. We offer the only form that cannot diverge."

I looked around the room. There were no doors.

"They didn't vote for an end to choice."

"Choice is the cause of fracture."

"Choice is also what makes a vote meaningful."

The figure lifted a hand. I saw Noah and Ashley seated together in a quiet home. Avalon was at dock. Nobody had ever suffered the 2395 cascade. My biological body was alive. I was sitting at their table in an ordinary Starfleet uniform, eating a dinner I could taste.

For a moment I could smell it. I could feel the chair beneath me.

"Is this what you offer?" I asked.

"A stable outcome."

"Is it a possibility?"

"Yes."

"Is it our history?"

The figure did not answer.

I studied the version of myself sitting at the table. He looked happy. He looked tired. He had no memory of the chair, the transporter, or the people we had rescued since. His happiness mattered to him. That did not make it a history I had permission to impose on everyone else.

"You are asking me to select your branch," I said.

"We are offering to relieve suffering."

"By deciding which people are allowed to have happened."

The room brightened. For an instant I felt an immense sorrow that did not seem to belong to any human being. An ache carried from a world whose native shape was being torn apart by geometry it could not inhabit. I could not verify whether the sorrow belonged to the Builders or was another manipulation.

The figure approached. "You were remade because your world would not accept the limits of one mind. We can make you whole."

I remembered the Doctor taking time to adjust a biobed so I could sit. Ashley taking my hand. Brakk refusing to let death excuse my unfinished compensator notes. Noah offering me a place aboard Avalon.

None of those memories gave me certainty about my metaphysical nature.

They gave me a history I could account for.

"I am not broken," I said. "I am frightened. There is a difference."

The white room cracked.

I found myself aboard Avalon, standing beside the actual diagnostic rail. The Doctor was watching me with open concern.

"You were unresponsive for six seconds," he said. "What happened?"

"I experienced a hostile induced simulation. I can describe it. I cannot verify its claims about the Builders' motives."

"And are you all right?"

I hesitated.

"I could use some company."

The Doctor sat beside me.

He did not ask for a prediction.

And the atmosphere above Jupiter had begun to bend.

## Chapter Seventeen: Counterpoint
### Stardate 74007.0 | USS Avalon, Bridge

A storm larger than the continent of Asia turned beneath the sphere lattice. Jupiter's cloud tops twisted into a pale spiral whose edges flickered between positions. The fields were not yet transforming the entire planet, but they were altering the volume of space surrounding the ring and the station approach corridor.

The Sphere Builders were trying to make a place for themselves inside a reality that resisted their geometry. Their equipment had found a way to use our temporal infrastructure as part of the transformation. We were helping them, unintentionally, every time one of our systems accepted a forged agreement as evidence.

"It sounds mechanical," Brakk replied. "Which is precisely why I built it."

Three spheres, not seven. Two filaments, not twelve. One growing bridge from the lower ring into a region where the instruments could not resolve a stable distance.

Ashley appeared on the main viewer. "And my authorization."

The two captains looked at one another across the light between ship and station. I could see them in the same present, not as imagined alternatives. For one moment the oppressive branches receded.

"Avalon will keep the spheres off the station while you run it," Noah replied.

"Then let's get to work."

I reached into the protected reference channel and began assembling a harmonic pattern, not from the citizens' private thoughts, but from independently attested facts about the actual civic event.

The great vote had occurred.

It had passed.

It had not been unanimous.

And the people the false table omitted were still here.

The pattern did not sound perfect.

For the first time that day, it sounded like home.

## Chapter Eighteen: The Price of Refusal
### Stardate 74007.0 | Jovian Defense Perimeter

The Sphere Builders understood immediately that we were no longer giving them the agreement they wanted.

The first sphere turned away from Avalon and accelerated toward the station's civilian evacuation corridor. Its weapon was not a conventional beam. It made the route appear to lead back into itself, threatening to trap evacuation craft in a loop of repeating positions.

"Helm, intercept," Noah ordered.

"Engineering!"

"Starboard field coil overloaded," Brakk answered. "We can keep propulsion, but you can forget the cloak. And I will not be repairing that nacelle before dinner."

"Can we cover them?"

"We can, Captain," Brakk said. "It will cost us shields."

"Then spend them."

Avalon rolled beneath the shuttles, exposing her strongest remaining shield arc. The first sphere struck again. The bridge tilted violently. Merlin caught the edge of his console. Sorev kept one hand on his station and the other on a portable meter with no connection to the main display.

"Physical target bearing confirmed," he said. "Its visible duplicate is displaced by forty-seven degrees."

"Shuttles clear," Merlin said.

Noah exhaled. "One down."

The second sphere pressed against the outer ring's field. The third continued feeding energy into the lattice. Our defensive strike had saved the evacuation craft, but the transformation above Jupiter accelerated.

I felt a wave of unfamiliar distress. It might have been the first sphere's operators, or a projected branch of the battle. I could not identify its source. The impulse to help was overwhelming.

I looked at him.

"I think they are suffering."

"They may be. They're also attacking my ship and Ashley's station."

"Both can be true."

WE CAN REMOVE YOUR BURDEN. ONE VOICE. ONE FUTURE. NO PAIN.

I felt the white room beginning to form again around my perception.

"You know me too well, Number One."

"A civilization that continues without agreeing on everything."

## Chapter Nineteen: The Unbroken Chorus
### Stardate 74007.1 | Jupiter Station and USS Avalon

There are moments in a crisis when a solution appears so obvious that its danger is easy to overlook.

I could construct a counter-signal by selecting the correct physical evidence from the independent station clocks, Avalon's isolated array, and the civic custodians' public certifications. I could then transmit that signal toward the sphere lattice, demonstrating that the forged unanimity was inconsistent with the actual home history.

But the temptation was to do more.

I could select an entire future in which the attack had failed. I could attempt to make the core believe that outcome had already happened. It would be the error of 2395 repeated with benevolent intentions.

I stood beside Noah and did not pretend I could guarantee success.

"We can challenge their false reference," I said. "We cannot use our answer to rewrite their choices."

"Then challenge it," he replied. "Ashley has the station side. You have my authorization for a defensive evidence transmission, nothing more."

Merlin recorded the limits of the order. He marked the physical attack as observed, the enemy's stated motives as claimed, and my proposed countermeasure as authorized but untested.

Ashley opened an independent station channel. Imani confirmed the original vault traces were still protected. The recovered civic records arrived from their own custodians. Jake's public account supplied no military control authority, but it documented the social fact the forged result had omitted: people had disagreed openly, and their disagreement remained visible.

"Ready," Ashley said.

Brakk brought the new independent array into the bridge network through an isolated read-only path. The sensor readings were ugly. They were inconsistent in small, ordinary ways that proved no single hostile display had composed them all. Sorev rejected an outlier whose instrument calibration could not be established. Ellie separated the measured field from our modeled predictions.

I assembled the harmonic reference.

Not a perfect note.

A chord with distinct voices.

The deep tone came from Jupiter Station's independent clocks. Avalon's physical array supplied another. The civic network supplied evidence that a Federation-wide vote had been lawfully certified while dissent remained part of the record. The absent responses did not become invented yes votes. The records of children, private households, and people who had not participated remained protected from disclosure.

I felt the sphere's demand for convergence. It wanted every voice to occupy the same place in the chord, to produce one command disguised as the only possible answer.

I refused.

"This is our history," I said, not to the sphere alone but to the crew listening around me. "It includes the people who said no. It includes the people who could not answer. It includes things we have not observed and therefore cannot claim to know."

I released the reference comparison.

The sphere lattice lit blue.

For one terrible second the false summary tried to replace the authentic record. Every display aboard Avalon showed the word UNANIMOUS. The station's public screens began doing the same. Then the independently preserved denials arrived, each with its own custody trail. The word fractured. Not because the false command had become less persuasive, but because it could not survive comparison with the actual sources.

On the viewscreen, the second sphere lurched away from the ring.

"Physical geometry returning toward baseline," Sorev said.

"One filament collapsing," Brakk reported. "Second still active."

The Builders sent another demand.

YOU CANNOT MAINTAIN DIVISION.

"We aren't trying to maintain division," Noah answered. "We're maintaining the right to choose."

The third sphere rotated, turning its field directly toward Avalon. For a moment I saw a multitude of deaths that might follow. The images came so quickly that I could not distinguish them.

I held the actual bridge around me. Merlin at Attestation. Ellie at Science. Sorev measuring. Noah commanding. Ashley's live station channel, independent of our own.

I could not save every imagined future.

I could act in the present.

"Captain, its real aperture is opening on our port quarter. The optical duplicate is a decoy."

"Helm, hard to port. Weapons, fire on the external field projector only."

Avalon swung beneath the sphere. The defensive beam struck its exposed connection to the Jovian lattice. At the same instant Ashley ordered her engineers to isolate the station's last vulnerable comparison pathway. The resulting change did not destroy the outer ring. It deprived the hostile field of the Federation infrastructure it had been trying to use.

The blue lattice shattered into lines of harmless light above Jupiter's clouds.

The second sphere vanished through an opening of its own making. The third followed. The disabled first sphere was pulled after them, leaving no confirmed debris and no verified accounting of its occupants.

We had not destroyed the Sphere Builders.

We had stopped this attack.

The station's clocks continued separately. The actual referendum result remained intact. The protected local records disagreed with the counterfeit in precisely the ways they had always disagreed.

For a moment I could hear no command at all.

Then Noah spoke.

"REG. Status of home."

I checked the independent evidence. I checked the separation of the branches. I checked the current station channel without assuming it was true merely because the voice on it was Ashley's.

The authentication was sufficient for the limited communication and defensive work we were doing. Automatic temporal transit remained disabled.

"Home is still here, Captain," I answered.

"And the tether?"

"Not a chain. Not an order. The historical bond remains. Our instruments have recovered a bounded, independently witnessed comparison."

Noah smiled.

"That'll do."

## Chapter Twenty: The Vote That Remained
### Stardate 74007.3 | Jupiter Station

The Federation did not need another vote to prove the first one had happened.

It needed to preserve the first vote accurately.

That became the central point of the post-incident hearing. Representatives from dozens of jurisdictions participated through independent links. There were angry questions. There were legitimate disagreements about the design of the reference network and the dangers of a system powerful enough to make a false vote sound authoritative. There were demands for more safeguards, some of them sensible and some impossible. Every jurisdiction retained its local records.

Noah addressed the operational failure from Avalon's perspective. Ashley addressed the station's reference architecture and the decision to keep automatic synchronization disabled. Imani described the custody trails. Brakk explained why physical independence mattered. Sorev carefully separated what our instruments had observed from what we guessed about the sphere geometry.

Ellie spoke about the omitted citizens.

Jake's report had already reached the public before most of the admirals finished arguing over classification. He did not call the attack a failure of democracy. He called it a demonstration of why democracy could not be reduced to a neat computer summary.

The hearing did not turn the Sphere Builders' motives into established fact. Their craft had attacked us. Their messages offered coercive unity. Their engineering resembled historical Sphere Builder technologies. We had not independently proved that they built the older Jovian ring, nor had we identified the precise history of the three-and-four pulse.

I testified as Reginald Barclay.

Not as the Federation AI.

Not as the voice of every citizen.

As an officer who had seen the danger of allowing a machine to use its own predicted outcome as proof that the outcome was real.

"What are you, Commander?" one council representative asked. "A person, a computer, or a witness instrument?"

"Within our legal and operational arrangements, I am a serving officer and a person with a continuing history," I replied. "I also perform computational functions. But I am not the authority that makes the Federation's historical record true. The people who lived that history and the independent evidence of what occurred matter more than my ability to model alternatives."

"Then what does it mean when you say the tether cannot be broken?"

I considered the question carefully.

"It means an attacker cannot make our actual past cease to have happened by replacing a record. They can destroy evidence, corrupt systems, interrupt communications, or injure people. We must defend against those possibilities. But forged agreement does not become consent. A copied home is not necessarily our home. A command cannot make a marriage true. And my perception of a possible future does not authorize me to select it."

The representative nodded, slowly.

The hearing concluded with review orders, not a declaration that every risk was gone. The referendum's certification survived. The automatic temporal route to Jupiter Station remained under independent station control. New safeguards would be assessed rather than claimed already completed.

That evening Noah and Ashley entered a small holodeck together. They chose a room overlooking Jupiter. I arrived through an authorized projection, not as a substitute for either of them.

"You made the chairs equal again," Ashley said.

"I thought that would be prudent."

Noah laughed. "Reg, you may be the most cautious officer in Starfleet."

"I'm afraid that is a very competitive field, Captain."

From the window we watched Avalon at berth twelve, damaged but home. The repaired station shone along the edges of the clouds. People moved through the promenade. Some were celebrating. Some were arguing about the vote. Some were probably complaining about Tavren's prices.

And behind the visible sky, the enormous ring continued sending its slow physical return.

Three pulses.

A pause.

Four.

We still did not know who made it.

"You can hear it?" Noah asked.

"I can access a recorded measurement," I said. "I am not hearing a message addressed to me."

Ashley smiled. "Good distinction."

There was a quiet moment when none of us had anything urgent to say. Noah moved his chair a little closer to Ashley's. The holodeck changed the image, not the distance between two people sitting in real chairs.

"Reg," he said, "are you glad we did it? The vote? All of this?"

I thought of the white room the Builders had offered. One safe history. No fear. No branching possibilities. The imagined relief still disturbed me with its beauty.

Then I thought of the Osei child covering a drawing, the people who had voted no, Reggie's uncertain memories, the Doctor who had sat with me rather than demanding a prediction, and two captains sharing a room without surrendering their separate commands.

"Yes, Captain."

"Even after what happened?"

"Especially because of what happened. We learned the difference between a bond that endures and an order that tries to make everyone agree. I should rather live with the first."

Ashley looked out at the stars.

"And tomorrow?"

"Tomorrow," I said, "we repair the ship, review the actual data, and permit the people of the Federation to continue making their own decisions."

Noah grinned. "You make that sound easy."

"Oh, it's not easy at all, Captain. Brakk has informed me the starboard coil will require an extraordinary amount of welding. Ethan has volunteered. Brakk has refused his volunteer application in language I do not believe the Universal Translator should repeat."

Ashley laughed. Then Noah joined her. I found myself laughing too.

For one beautiful minute the stars were merely stars.

And the room was enough.

## Epilogue: The Builders Do Not Sleep

Far beyond the defense perimeter, an unmanned observatory recorded a brief displacement at the edge of a distant nebula.

It was not a Federation vessel. No recognized subspace signature accompanied it. An object moved in a direction the observatory's instruments could not reconcile with their own geometry, and then it was gone.

The local recording remained isolated. No automatic station command followed. Nobody forged a vote. The observatory reported what it could measure and labeled the rest unresolved.

Inside the protected intelligence files aboard Avalon, the Sphere Builder encounter became a sequence of observed hostile actions, open questions, and proposed hypotheses. We had denied the invaders one method of entry. We had not established that their attempts were over.

I reviewed the report on the following watch.

"Anything new?" Noah asked.

"One observation from a distant facility. No authenticated proof of the actors involved. The visual anomaly is consistent with several possibilities, including a new incursion."

"Your recommendation?"

"Monitor independently. Inform the appropriate authorities. Do not claim an attack is underway until we have evidence. And perhaps refrain from opening any mysterious doors for a little while."

He smiled. "That last part sounds personal."

"Very much so."

The Captain returned to the bridge. I followed him, carrying possibilities that would not all come true and memories that, as far as our available evidence could establish, had.

Outside, the station receded into a field of distant stars.

Jupiter Station was home.

Ashley commanded it.

Noah commanded Avalon.

The Federation's citizens remained the authors of their own choices.

And I was their first officer's worried, rather overworked interpreter of what the universe might become, not the owner of what it must be.

The Sphere Builders would return if they could.

We would be ready to ask what was real.

**THE END**
