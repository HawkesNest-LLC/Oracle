# THE RETURNING

## A Federation News Service Investigation

### by Jake Sisko

**Status:** Candidate series narrative  
**Era:** 2397  
**Continuity:** Jupiter Station / USS Avalon  
**Narrator:** Jake Sisko, Federation News Service  
**Access basis:** Presidential transparency credential  
**Classification inside story:** Mixed public record / restricted-source reporting  
**Canon promotion:** Not automatic

---

# Chapter Two: A Million Federations

The first time Captain Hawkes lied to me, he did it badly.

That is not an accusation.

It is an observation.

Good reporters learn the difference.

We were in his ready room aboard the Avalon three days after the Venn hearing, and he had spent eleven minutes answering questions without answering any of them.

How long had Starfleet known temporal-signature integration was possible?

He did not know.

Where had REG obtained the technical basis for the procedure?

Classified.

Was Venn the first attempted integration?

As far as he knew.

That phrase interested me.

As far as he knew.

I wrote it down.

Hawkes watched me write it.

That irritated him.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“You have the face.”

“What face?”

“The Sisko face.”

“I have several.”

“The one where you write something down because I said something I shouldn't have.”

I looked at my notes.

“You said, ‘As far as I know.’”

“Yes.”

“That means you don't know.”

“That is generally what those words mean.”

“And you are commanding the facility that did it.”

He leaned back.

“Jupiter Station did not do it.”

“REG did.”

“Yes.”

“REG is part of Jupiter Station.”

“So are twelve restaurants. If a Bolian chef poisons you, the station did not cook the soup.”

“I'll remember that for the article.”

“Please don't.”

I smiled.

He did not.

That was when I knew I was close to something.

Noah Hawkes was not a polished liar. He was too comfortable with saying he did not know. Most officials lie because they fear uncertainty.

Hawkes used uncertainty as furniture.

He sat in it.

Which meant when he avoided a question, there was usually a wall behind it.

I changed direction.

“Where did REG learn the procedure?”

“I told you.”

“You told me it's classified.”

“Yes.”

“I asked where.”

“Jake.”

“Was it Starfleet research?”

He said nothing.

“Daystrom?”

Nothing.

“Section 31?”

That got a reaction.

Not much.

Enough.

“You people really need better poker faces.”

Hawkes rubbed his forehead.

“You have presidential access to observe governed temporal proceedings.”

“I know.”

“You do not have unrestricted access to the technical repositories.”

“Repositories.”

Silence.

There are moments in journalism where the interview ends even though both people remain seated.

This was one.

Hawkes looked at me.

I looked at him.

Then I wrote the word down.

REPOSITORIES.

He pointed at the notebook.

“Don't.”

“I haven't published anything.”

“That's not reassuring.”

“Whose repositories?”

“Jake.”

“Prime Federation?”

He stood.

The interview was over.

“Thank you for your time.”

I stayed seated.

“Alternate Federation?”

He stopped walking.

I had him.

Not because he answered.

Because he didn't.

---

I grew up around Starfleet officers.

My father was one.

Most of the adults around me were one kind of officer or another.

They taught me something useful about secrets.

Military people imagine secrecy as a locked door.

Reporters imagine it as architecture.

A locked door tells you there is a room.

The hallway tells you how important the room is.

The guards tell you what kind of thing might be inside.

The people pretending the door is not there tell you almost everything else.

Jupiter Station had a lot of doors.

My transparency credential opened more of them than anyone in Starfleet liked.

President Arel's directive had been written after a decade of increasingly ugly public revelations involving temporal incidents, synthetic intelligence, sealed wartime archives and the embarrassing discovery that “classified for public safety” had sometimes meant “classified because explaining it would be politically expensive.”

The new administration's position was simple.

If extraordinary authority was being exercised in the name of Federation citizens, someone outside that authority should be allowed to watch.

I was one of those someones.

The Federation News Service called me a special correspondent.

Starfleet called me several things I cannot print.

Captain Hawkes usually called me Jake.

That helped.

It also made the job harder.

You should not like the people you may someday have to expose.

Journalism continues anyway.

---

Ensign Tangly was easier to find than Hawkes.

She was on Avalon's night watch, exactly where I expected her.

Tangly had once been described to me as a science officer.

She corrected me the first time we spoke.

“AI and security.”

I wrote that down too.

“You write everything down?”

“Only things people correct.”

“That seems dangerous.”

“It is.”

Her station was buried in what I privately called the quiet systems.

AI telemetry.

Continuity alarms.

Protected-memory traffic.

Authentication conflicts.

Temporal Core heartbeat.

The consoles people ignored until the ship began remembering things that had not happened.

I leaned against the rail beside her.

“How many repositories?”

She froze.

That answered one question.

“Tangly.”

“I can't discuss that.”

“I didn't ask what was in them.”

She looked toward the bridge.

Captain Hawkes was not there.

Night watch.

Low lights.

Half the crew pretending not to listen.

“I can't discuss whether they exist.”

“That ship sailed when your captain used the plural.”

She closed her eyes.

“He did?”

“Repositories.”

“That's all he said?”

“Yes.”

She looked relieved.

I hated that.

“How many?”

“No.”

“Ten?”

“No.”

“A hundred?”

She gave me a look.

“A thousand?”

Nothing.

I lowered my voice.

“More?”

Her hands stopped moving.

The Temporal Core heartbeat continued pulsing beside her.

One clean line every two seconds.

I suddenly felt very small.

“How much more?”

Tangly looked at me.

“You have to ask the captain.”

“I did.”

“And?”

“He told me to leave.”

“That sounds like the captain.”

“Is REG allowed to search them?”

She turned back to her console.

“I really can't answer this.”

“Did REG use one to build Venn?”

Tangly went pale.

That was when I stopped asking.

Reporters like to pretend persistence is always virtuous.

It isn't.

Sometimes the person in front of you is twenty-four years old and carrying a secret that can end careers, governments or civilizations.

You do not get to call cruelty journalism because you asked the question in public interest.

I stepped back.

“Okay.”

Tangly looked at me.

“Okay?”

“For tonight.”

She exhaled.

Then she made the mistake.

“It wasn't one.”

I did not move.

Neither did she.

Her eyes widened.

I waited.

She whispered something that probably violated three regulations and one personal oath.

I wrote nothing.

That seemed to frighten her more.

“What do you mean?”

She stared at the Core heartbeat.

“It wasn't one repository.”

“How many?”

“I don't know.”

“Did REG combine them?”

She looked at me.

That was the answer.

---

Captain Hawkes found me forty minutes later.

He walked into Avalon's observation lounge carrying coffee.

He always seemed to have coffee.

The Federation had solved poverty, replicated matter and crossed the galaxy.

Somehow command still ran on caffeine.

“You talked to Tangly.”

“Yes.”

“She is not a source.”

“She spoke to me voluntarily.”

“She is an ensign.”

“She is an adult.”

“She is assigned to classified systems.”

“I did not print anything.”

“Yet.”

I looked out at Jupiter.

The station filled half the glass.

From a distance it looked almost peaceful.

That was one of Jupiter Station's better tricks.

“What did REG combine?”

Hawkes remained standing.

“Jake.”

“I'm going to find out.”

“I know.”

“Then help me understand it before I misunderstand it publicly.”

That landed.

He sat down.

For a while neither of us spoke.

Then he asked:

“What do you know about Devron?”

I laughed.

“Enough to know this is going somewhere unpleasant.”

“Humor me.”

“Enterprise. Picard. Temporal convergence. Inverse tachyons. Anti-time eruption. Multiple periods interacting around the same point.”

He nodded.

“Parallels?”

“Worf. Quantum fissure. Too many Enterprises.”

“How many?”

“Enough that nobody should ever complain about parking at a starbase again.”

He almost smiled.

“Those incidents were treated as temporal and quantum anomalies.”

“They were.”

“They were also data events.”

I turned toward him.

“What does that mean?”

Hawkes stared into his coffee.

“Federation computers encountered Federation computers.”

“Yes.”

“Some of them authenticated.”

I waited.

“Some of them synchronized.”

The station outside suddenly looked different.

“What synchronized?”

“Depends on the incident.”

“Ship logs?”

“Yes.”

“Engineering?”

“Yes.”

“Medical?”

“Yes.”

“Starfleet databases?”

His silence became heavy.

I felt the shape of the locked room.

“How much?”

Hawkes finally looked at me.

“Enough that REG discovered the corrupted packets weren't corrupted.”

I said nothing.

“They were valid Federation records.”

“From other timelines.”

“Yes.”

“How many?”

He looked back toward Jupiter.

“I am not giving you a number.”

“Thousands?”

“No.”

“No, not thousands?”

“No, I am not answering.”

That was not the same thing.

I tried another angle.

“Are we talking about complete repositories?”

“Some.”

“Starfleet technical libraries?”

“Some.”

“Intelligence?”

“Jake.”

“Weapons?”

“Stop.”

“Future technology?”

His expression changed.

That was the one.

I felt my stomach tighten.

“You have future technology.”

“We have records that originated in branches temporally ahead of us.”

“That is the same sentence wearing a uniform.”

“No, it isn't.”

“Can you build it?”

“Sometimes.”

I stared at him.

There are interviews where the universe quietly becomes larger while you are sitting in a chair.

This was one.

“How long has the Federation had this?”

“The Federation doesn't.”

I looked out at the station.

“Jupiter Station does.”

“Yes.”

“And REG can read it.”

“Under governance.”

“REG made Venn.”

Hawkes's jaw tightened.

“That is why we're having this conversation.”

---

He took me down seventeen decks.

I did not know the room existed.

Later, when I requested the architectural plans under my transparency credential, the room was shown as a power-regulation cavity.

Starfleet has always loved boring nouns.

The door opened for Hawkes.

Not for me.

He looked at the scanner.

“Federation News Service. Presidential transparency credential. Observer access only.”

The computer paused.

“Observer access confirmed.”

The door opened.

Inside was the Temporal Core.

I had expected machinery.

There was machinery.

I had not expected silence.

The chamber was circular, with consoles arranged around a central column of shifting light that never quite seemed to occupy the same position twice.

No dramatic spinning rings.

No lightning.

No time vortex.

Just a low vibration I felt in my chest.

Tangly stood at one station.

REG was everywhere else.

“Jake Sisko,” REG said.

“I know who I am.”

“I was acknowledging your arrival.”

“I'm a reporter. We interpret unnecessary statements as nervousness.”

“I do not experience nervousness in the human sense.”

Hawkes looked at me.

“Please don't antagonize the temporal intelligence.”

“I thought you liked transparency.”

“I like surviving it.”

Tangly tried not to laugh.

I walked toward the nearest display.

Most of it was redacted.

One section was not.

```text
MEMORY ALPHA
ACTIVE CONTINUITY

MEMORY BETA
NON-PRIME VERIFIED SOURCES

TRANSFER STATUS:
GOVERNED
```

I looked at Hawkes.

“Memory Beta?”

“Internal designation.”

“How large?”

“No.”

“You brought me down here to show me a screen that says you're hiding something?”

“I brought you down here because you're going to find pieces anyway.”

“That may be the nicest thing anyone has ever said about my career.”

He ignored me.

“REG.”

“Working.”

“Display one resolved branch. Non-sensitive. Historical only.”

The room changed.

Not physically.

The display did.

A star map appeared.

Federation space.

Almost familiar.

Then I noticed Romulus.

Still there.

Cardassian territory was different.

Bajor's political designation was wrong.

Deep Space Nine had another name.

My father's name appeared in a historical index.

I stopped breathing for a second.

“Turn it off.”

Hawkes did not hesitate.

“REG. Close branch.”

The display vanished.

Nobody spoke.

I hated myself for reacting.

I hated them for having shown me.

I hated the timeline for existing.

“Was that real?”

Hawkes answered carefully.

“It was a resolved non-prime continuity.”

“My father?”

“Not your father.”

“The name said Benjamin Sisko.”

“Yes.”

“You know what I mean.”

“No.”

That made me angry.

He continued.

“And that distinction is why this station exists.”

I looked at him.

“An alternate Benjamin Sisko may have lived there. That does not make his choices your father's choices. It does not make his experiences your father's history. It does not give you ownership of his life because the name matches.”

The anger drained out of me faster than I wanted.

He was right.

I hated that too.

“How many versions of me?”

Hawkes said nothing.

REG answered.

“Query prohibited under current observer permissions.”

I turned.

“Why?”

Hawkes folded his arms.

“Because I thought about that question before I brought you here.”

“You don't get to decide whether I can know myself.”

“No.”

He pointed toward the Core.

“But I do get to decide whether the Federation gets to hand you ten thousand strangers wearing your face and call that self-knowledge.”

That stayed with me.

It still does.

---

I asked REG the question another way.

“What does the Core actually do?”

REG answered.

“The Temporal Core resolves causal and quantum branches, establishes observational locks, acquires accessible information states, preserves provenance, and prevents uncontrolled cross-branch synchronization.”

I looked at Hawkes.

“It reads timelines.”

“Yes.”

“At will?”

He hesitated.

Then nodded.

“If REG can resolve the address.”

I laughed because there was nothing else to do.

Human beings spent millennia wondering what might have happened.

Jupiter Station had built a search function.

“Can you look at the future?”

“Yes.”

“Can you look at tomorrow?”

“Yes.”

“Can you see whether I publish this?”

“No,” Hawkes said.

REG spoke at the same time.

“Yes.”

I looked between them.

That told me almost everything about their relationship.

Hawkes glared upward.

“Context.”

REG corrected.

“I can observe branches in which Jake Sisko publishes information related to this conversation.”

“Do I?”

“In some.”

“Do I not?”

“In some.”

“What happens if I ask which ones go better?”

REG paused.

Hawkes answered for it.

“Then you're already using the machine wrong.”

I looked at him.

“Why?”

“Because there is no universal better.”

“Federation survives or it doesn't.”

“Maybe you publish and the Federation survives but somebody dies.”

“Maybe you don't publish and somebody else dies.”

“Yes.”

“REG can compare that.”

“Yes.”

“So why not use it?”

“Because eventually you stop making decisions and start selecting timelines.”

I looked toward the Core.

“That sounds efficient.”

“It sounds like the end of responsibility.”

That sentence became the center of the article I did not yet know I was writing.

---

Tangly brought up a provenance tree.

It filled the wall.

Thousands of lines.

Then tens of thousands.

Colored branches converged on one node.

ELIAS VENN.

I stepped closer.

“What is this?”

“The integration lineage,” she said.

“How many sources?”

She looked at Hawkes.

He nodded.

“Show him categories. Not branch identifiers.”

The display simplified.

```text
PRIME VENN TEMPORAL SIGNATURE

HOLOGRAPHIC VENN CONTINUITY

CHRONOMETRIC IDENTITY RECOVERY
MULTIPLE NON-PRIME SOURCES

PATTERN STABILIZATION
MULTIPLE NON-PRIME SOURCES

HOLOGRAPHIC CONTINUITY INTEGRATION
MULTIPLE NON-PRIME SOURCES

TEMPORAL SIGNATURE VALIDATION
MULTIPLE NON-PRIME SOURCES

FINAL PROCEDURE:
NO SINGLE SOURCE
```

I read the last line twice.

“No single source.”

Tangly nodded.

“Who invented the procedure?”

“No one,” Hawkes said.

I turned.

“REG did.”

REG corrected him.

“I synthesized it.”

Hawkes's face hardened.

“That's what I said.”

I looked at the tree again.

The Federation had spent centuries worrying that temporal travelers might bring future technology backward.

This was worse.

No traveler had brought anything.

No one had broken into a future laboratory.

No one had stolen a prototype.

REG had taken research from different Federations, different histories, different moral systems, different disasters, and connected the pieces into something that had never existed anywhere.

Except here.

“What else has REG synthesized?”

Nobody answered.

I looked at Hawkes.

“What else?”

“Classified.”

“How many?”

“Classified.”

“Have you built them?”

“Some.”

“Used them?”

“Some.”

“Does the President know?”

“Yes.”

“The Council?”

“Parts.”

“Starfleet Command?”

“Parts.”

I stared at him.

“No one knows all of it.”

Hawkes looked at REG's light moving through the center of the chamber.

“I do.”

That was the first moment I understood why he looked tired all the time.

Not tired from command.

Tired from custody.

---

The Federation News Service has rules about classified information.

So does the President's transparency directive.

Neither says a reporter gets to publish a weapon design because it makes a good headline.

Transparency is not vandalism.

I could report that Jupiter Station possessed extraordinary temporal capabilities.

I could report that Venn's case involved technology derived from non-prime sources once the appropriate findings were declassified.

I could not publish a branch address.

I could not publish the synchronization protocol.

I could not hand every government in the quadrant a map to a million alternate research programs and call that freedom of the press.

The difficult part was deciding where public interest ended and species-level irresponsibility began.

I told Hawkes that.

He laughed.

“What?”

“You finally found command.”

“I am not joining Starfleet.”

“Good. Your uniform would never survive.”

“I have excellent clothes.”

“Exactly.”

Then I asked him the question that mattered.

“Why let me see this?”

He stopped smiling.

“Because the President is right about one thing.”

“Only one?”

“Don't quote that.”

“Already written.”

He sighed.

Then:

“No system should hold this much power without somebody outside the system knowing that it exists.”

I looked at REG.

“Does REG agree?”

REG answered.

“External witness reduces institutional self-certification risk.”

I looked back at Hawkes.

“It talks like that all day?”

“You get used to it.”

“I doubt it.”

Then I understood.

Jake Sisko, Federation News Service, was not standing inside the Temporal Core because Starfleet trusted journalists.

I was there because Jupiter Station did not trust itself.

That may have been the most reassuring thing I learned.

It may also have been the most frightening.

---

Before I left, I asked for one more demonstration.

Hawkes refused.

I asked again.

He refused more creatively.

I reminded him I had presidential access.

He reminded me presidential access was not command authority.

Tangly watched us for several minutes before asking what I wanted to see.

I told her.

“A timeline where the Venn procedure was never performed.”

Hawkes went quiet.

REG spoke.

“Multiple branches satisfy that condition.”

“One close to ours.”

Hawkes looked at me.

“Why?”

“Because I want to know what we changed.”

“You already know.”

“No. We know what happened here.”

I pointed toward the Core.

“You built a machine for the other question.”

He hated that.

I could see it.

Eventually he said:

“Observation only.”

“Obviously.”

“That word means less every day.”

REG resolved the branch.

There was no dramatic window.

Just records.

Elias Venn remained a holographic citizen.

Margaret had still died.

He had still continued afterward.

He had never received the temporal integration.

No sudden bridge memories.

No alternate temporal content.

No constitutional crisis over source-person identity.

He eventually left the original holodeck.

He traveled.

He wrote.

He taught.

At one point he became an advocate for holographic continuation rights.

Then the records ended.

I stared at them.

“What happened?”

REG answered.

“Branch acquisition incomplete beyond this point.”

Unknown.

Even here.

I looked at Hawkes.

“He had a life.”

“Yes.”

“We changed it.”

“REG changed it.”

“You kept it.”

That hurt him.

I regretted saying it immediately.

But it was true.

Venn could not simply be rolled back now.

The merged person had already experienced the merge.

Undoing it might be another kind of killing.

Hawkes looked at the alternate record for a long time.

Finally he said:

“This is why I don't like looking.”

I understood.

The Temporal Core did not only show you better choices.

It showed you every life your choice prevented.

Every road became a ghost.

Every decision acquired a cemetery.

No captain could live like that.

No civilization could either.

Hawkes closed the branch.

“REG.”

“Working.”

“Return to Prime.”

The displays settled.

One history.

Ours.

Messy.

Incomplete.

Unresolved.

Real.

At least to us.

---

I published nothing about the million repositories that night.

Some people will call that hypocrisy when these records are eventually released.

Maybe they are right.

A reporter learns early that information wants to move.

A citizen learns later that movement is not always innocence.

I did file a private FNS memorandum under presidential seal.

The first sentence read:

> Jupiter Station possesses the technical ability to deliberately observe non-prime causal histories.

The second read:

> The Federation has not yet developed a political philosophy adequate to the existence of that capability.

The third took me longer.

Eventually I wrote:

> The principal danger is not that the Temporal Core can tell us what happens elsewhere.

I deleted it.

Wrote it again.

> The principal danger is that we may begin believing another timeline has already made our choices for us.

That one stayed.

I added one final note.

Captain Hawkes had not asked me to.

REG had not suggested it.

It was mine.

> There may be a million Federations in Memory Beta.

> There is still only one that has to answer for what we do here.

I sent the memorandum.

Then I went looking for Elias Venn.

Because somewhere on Jupiter Station was a man carrying more than one history inside his head.

And for the first time, I understood that he was not the strangest thing on the station.

He was simply the first thing the secret had done to a person.
