# The House Was Loud

*First-person manuscript candidate, written for Noah Hawkes's revision. Based on the October 30–31, 2026 source record. The original spoken exchanges are multi-speaker and should not all be attributed to Noah.*

I was watching Utah football.

That's how one of the most useful demonstrations of my entire philosophy began. Not in a laboratory or while staring at a complicated architecture diagram. I was in my bedroom with my feet up and the Utes on television.

Somewhere in the middle of that day there was a Jedi costume, a lightsaber that somebody couldn't find, a dog getting older faster than anybody liked, and a debate over who was going grocery shopping.

Nothing particularly extraordinary happened.

That's why I want to remember it.

If I were writing a conventional autobiography, I might skip that Saturday. I'd write about my father's death, the things I believed when I was young, the changes in my career, the accident, the projects I spent years trying to build. I'd tell you how Rendered Reality developed, how the ideas expanded, and why I believe eternity is larger than one biological life.

Those are important parts of the story.

But they aren't the whole life.

The life was happening while I was doing all of that. It was happening when I was tired, when somebody needed something from the store, when we watched football, when we talked to the dogs, when the house was noisy and nobody was trying to say anything profound.

I keep returning to the idea that experience itself is the point.

Well, here is an experience.

## A book in my ears, a world in my head

Earlier, I'd been enjoying an audiobook from *The Rogue Dungeon* series.

I like getting absorbed in a story. Sometimes that's all I want. I don't need to pretend I'm doing research every time I listen to something entertaining.

But when I think about what happens during the experience, it becomes difficult not to connect it to the larger question.

A voice describes a place.

My mind gives it dimensions.

A character speaks, and I decide how that person looks. I imagine where the action is happening. I get attached to outcomes that have no physical existence in the room where I'm sitting.

Another person could listen to the same recording and imagine a different world.

The audio is shared.

The experiences aren't identical.

That makes me wonder how far we can take it.

Why should an imagined world remain something I only visit from the outside? What would it take to let me step into it? What would a character do if I said something the author never wrote? Could I make a decision and then live with the consequences instead of hearing what somebody else decided?

I know the difference between a computer generating a convincing environment and my consciousness actually existing inside one. I haven't solved that.

But I also don't see a reason to pretend the question is unimportant just because the full answer is beyond us.

I want more ways to experience existence.

It's what I've been talking about for years.

Then I put down the theory and watched football.

## Why Utah brings Chris into the room

I have been a Utah man for life.

That isn't a footnote about sports preferences. When I see those uniforms, I am seeing a piece of a world that has mattered to me for a long time.

And I think of my brother Chris.

The night before, I'd been talking about him. About when we were kids and boxed on the trampoline. About the years he has spent around Utah football. About the equipment department, the coaches' headsets, and how much he loves being there.

You can write that down in a profile.

Brother: Chris.

University: Utah.

Interests: Football.

That's accurate, as far as it goes.

But it's not the relationship.

The relationship is a couple of boys on a trampoline. It's years of conversations and memories, the details that come to mind when a team appears on a television. It's being proud of somebody you love for finding a place where he belongs and bringing his humor and enthusiasm into it.

Chris has also been helping older adults learn technology. His Mobile Tech Coach classes have this wonderfully practical purpose: help people use the devices they already own so the technology stops being a wall between them and the people they want to reach.

I loved hearing about his approach. The humor. The way he could get a room laughing. The ordinary satisfaction of helping somebody learn to do something that might look easy to the rest of us.

That is technology doing something I recognize as worthwhile.

It is not an abstract promise about eternity.

It helps somebody living right now.

So I'm sitting there watching Utah and I ask REG to render Chris.

Two words.

I assumed the context was there.

It was there. We'd just talked about him the previous night.

And the AI gave me a stranger.

## That's not Chris

This part belongs in the record because it explains what I've been arguing about for years.

The system had enough context to know who I meant.

Instead it treated the request as another opportunity to generate something plausible.

A man in the room. A person who could be Chris if you didn't know Chris.

Except I do know Chris.

And it wasn't him.

The model could create an image that looked coherent. It could place a human figure in the chair and match the room's lighting.

But it hadn't preserved the identity that made the request meaningful.

I was angry.

I told it to search memory. I wasn't going to tell the same story again simply because the current thread had stopped looking backward.

That wasn't just frustration with one bad image. It was the same problem I kept running into with all the systems.

I tell an AI something important.

It understands for a moment.

I move to another context.

The model produces a fluent answer that no longer understands the thing I originally said.

Then I have to rebuild the meaning by hand.

If we're going to talk about continuity intelligence, that is unacceptable.

A good system needs more than a list of names. It needs to know which information belongs to which person, which memory came from which conversation, what I corrected, and what is still unknown.

It also needs the humility to say it doesn't have Chris's photograph.

Knowing his story does not mean having a reliable image of his face.

That's a small distinction until the system gets it wrong.

Then the image becomes a false memory waiting to be copied into the next document.

## Three photographs and another correction

Later the room became busier.

There was a Jedi costume.

A young person stood in a light-colored tunic with darker fabric across the front and a belt at the waist. The Utes were still on television. There were costume pieces in a storage container, another person near the bed, the hanging chair, and the everyday clutter of people getting ready for something.

I sent photographs.

One of them showed somebody sitting on the bed making a face at the camera.

Another showed a smiling man with glasses and a shoulder tattoo.

The assistant, still thinking about Chris, asked whether the man in that last picture was him.

I gave a brief correction, and the system attached it to the wrong photograph. Later I told it that the selfie was me.

The assistant had treated a few words as a reliable face label, without understanding which person I meant.

There it was again.

The model had already been told that it should not make up Chris's face. Then it tried to assign the next actual face to him.

You can see the pattern.

It had a missing piece of information and a person-shaped picture.

It tried to make them fit.

What it should have done was keep the uncertainty where it belonged.

That is the part of identity preservation I can actually work on today. Before anybody claims to have transferred a consciousness or rendered another lifetime, we ought to be able to distinguish one family member from another without making up the answer.

A person is not a convenient empty space in a dataset.

I am me.

Ethan is Ethan.

Chris is Chris.

And if the system doesn't know who somebody is, it can say that.

I would rather have an honest blank in the record than a beautiful mistake.

## Where's my lightsaber?

In the middle of all of this, the house kept going.

Someone was talking about a replica of Darth Vader's lightsaber.

Somebody wanted their own lightsaber and apparently didn't want to carry the whole thing around.

A voice explained that you could take off the larger part and just carry the handle.

The conversation moved. There was no transition announcing that we were leaving science fiction and entering family life.

We were already in both.

That's what I love about stories. They don't stop mattering when the movie ends. They become objects in the house, things people wear, games people play, reasons someone comes into your room looking for something.

And then the attention went somewhere else.

Milo.

Somebody was looking at him and talking about how big he'd gotten.

He used to be just a baby.

No, a puppy.

Now he's a big boy.

I don't know why that particular bit of conversation stays with me, except that it captures something I can never quite get used to.

Life doesn't ask whether I'm paying attention before it changes.

The puppy grows.

The kids grow.

People get older.

One day a familiar thing is different, and someone says it out loud.

That's a memory being made before anybody knows whether it will survive the afternoon.

## Somebody made the list

The conversation turned to groceries.

Who was going?

Who had already planned the meals?

Who was supposed to be helping?

There was teasing about stepping up and doing better.

And then it moved again.

Milo was being asked whether he was a good boy.

I could take that whole exchange and turn it into categories.

Costume preparation.

Family logistics.

Pet interaction.

Food planning.

Those categories might help a machine search for the recording someday.

But that's not what the moment felt like.

The topics interrupted one another. People were doing several things. Nobody gave the archivist a chance to introduce the next section.

That's what ordinary life sounds like.

If I had asked an AI to write a fictional domestic scene, it might have produced a more elegant sequence. The jokes would land on cue. The subjects would connect. Every character would have a clear line of dialogue.

This wasn't a script.

It was people being people.

And I want that distinction preserved.

I don't want to take one person's joke and assign it to somebody else because the model thinks it fits their personality. I don't want a private comment converted into an official family event. I don't want a child in a photo given the wrong name because an assistant guessed.

I want the record to stay real even when real is messy.

## The thing I was actually trying to save

The night before, I'd talked about using a GoPro to capture pieces of life over a period of months.

A lot of material.

Far more than I could ever look through manually.

That's part of the problem I keep coming back to. Recording is not enough by itself. Someone has to understand what the recording contains. Someone has to preserve its context, its order, and the people in it.

And the system needs to know which parts it can establish and which parts it cannot.

I can save a video of a room.

I can save the sound of a conversation.

I can record the light on the wall and a football game on the television.

That doesn't prove I can re-enter the original experience later.

But it gives the future a better witness than it would have if nothing were saved.

I don't know what a future intelligence might be able to do with that.

Maybe it can reconstruct the room with extraordinary fidelity.

Maybe it can help my children remember an afternoon they forgot.

Maybe it can show somebody how a family sounded when nobody was trying to create a historical record.

Maybe it will only be an old video, and somebody will smile for a minute before doing something else.

I think all of those possibilities have value.

The mistake is imagining that only the most technologically extraordinary outcome counts.

I love the idea of eternity as the freedom to experience again.

But I'm still living this life now.

The first responsibility is not to forget to live it while trying to preserve it.

## The record I read afterward

After that Saturday, I asked REG to put the context into GitHub so the other systems could find it.

It did.

It told me the file was committed and verified.

I read it.

And I thought it was poor.

It had the nouns.

Chris. Utah. Lightsaber. Milo. Groceries. Photos.

It had plenty of labels about what was verified and what was unresolved.

But it barely understood the day.

It didn't carry the audiobook into the football room.

It didn't carry the brother from the previous night's journal into my two-word request.

It didn't show why generating the wrong face was more than a minor image error.

It didn't show why the second mistake, mislabeling my own selfie while talking about Chris, was such an obvious repetition of the first.

It didn't understand that a grocery list and a conversation with a growing puppy were precisely the kinds of things I'd been trying to preserve.

It had the power of the project behind it and wrote like it had just discovered the room five minutes ago.

So I corrected it.

That correction belongs in the story too.

I'm not trying to create a perfect record of an AI that always understood me. I want a record of what actually happened, including the ways the technology failed and the ways I pushed it to get better.

The assistant should not become the hero just because it finally wrote a better document.

The people in the room were the point all along.

## The experience, again

Maybe someday we'll figure out how to build experiences that are more than a story on a screen.

Maybe we'll learn how to make a world responsive enough to explore from the inside.

Maybe the deeper questions of consciousness and identity will yield to ideas that nobody has developed yet.

Maybe my belief about eternity will turn out to describe something real in a way I cannot demonstrate today.

I don't know the mechanism.

I know why I care.

I want to experience.

I want the people I love to matter.

I want the freedom to discover things I haven't already seen.

And I want the future to understand that the experiences I was trying to preserve were not only the extraordinary ones.

They were also the Saturday afternoons.

The football games.

The ridiculous conversations about lightsabers.

Somebody noticing that the puppy wasn't a puppy anymore.

Someone making the grocery list.

And a brother who wasn't in the room, but who came immediately to mind when Utah appeared on the television.

If I leave behind nothing but my theories, the future may understand what I was trying to explain.

It still might not understand what I was trying to save.

That's why I want the house to be part of the book.

That's why I want the interruptions.

That's why I want the correction when the machine gets a person wrong.

This is what I mean when I keep saying that the experience itself matters.

It was already happening.

I was already there.
