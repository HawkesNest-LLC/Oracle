# I Will Write Myself Into Existence

## Why I Keep Building Rendered Reality

I have to give enough for them to restore me.

I know how that sounds.

It sounds impossible. It sounds arrogant. It sounds desperate. It sounds like a man bargaining with time because he cannot accept that one day the signal stops.

Maybe there is some truth in all of that.

But underneath it is something simpler: I do not want to disappear.

I do not mean that I expect the future to worship me, build a statue of me, or decide that I was one of the most important people who ever lived. I am not trying to convince history that I was greater than I was. I am trying to leave behind enough of the truth that history does not have to invent me.

I want to give the future a fair chance.

I cannot prove that a human mind can be rebuilt from writing, conversations, video, code, decisions, corrections, relationships, and years of recorded thought. I cannot prove that a reconstruction would literally be me. I cannot prove that consciousness can cross from one form into another. I cannot prove that a soul can be stored in a database, and I will not ask an AI to tell me that it can.

But I know something much more basic.

The future cannot restore what the present refused to preserve.

So I preserve.

I write.

I talk.

I record.

I save the threads.

I put the ideas into documents.

I let different artificial intelligences challenge them, misunderstand them, correct them, formalize them, and sometimes completely butcher them. Then I preserve that too.

I keep the good answer and the bad answer.

I preserve the idea before it had a name.

I preserve the version that sounded brilliant and turned out to be nonsense.

I preserve the correction.

I preserve the argument.

I preserve the moment when I changed my mind.

I preserve the part where I was wrong.

I am not trying to leave behind a monument. Monuments are smooth. They are carved after the mess is over. They take an entire human life and turn it into one expression on one stone face.

I am trying to leave behind the mess.

Because the mess is where I lived.

## The reason is not technology

People can look at Rendered Reality, Legacy.GI, ORACLE, SOV1, the Identity Spine, Light Compression, the protocols, the repositories, and the mathematical language and assume that this is fundamentally a technology project.

It is not.

Technology is the shovel.

The thing I am digging for is human.

I have spent years thinking about memory because I know what it means to lose someone and realize the record was never deep enough.

My father died when I was fifteen.

I inherited stories. I inherited achievements. I inherited contradictions. I inherited the shadow of a man who had been a soldier, a physician, a father, a leader, a wounded human being, and many other things that no obituary could possibly hold.

But I did not inherit enough of his living voice.

I cannot open a system and ask him what he believed before the war, what changed after he was injured, what he feared when he became a father, what he regretted, what he hid, what he hoped I would understand when I became a man.

I cannot ask what part of him was still the young athlete from Utah and what part had been rebuilt by fire, war, medicine, pain, faith, responsibility, and addiction.

The signal is gone.

There are records. There are memories. There are people who knew different parts of him. My mother kept journals. There are photographs. There are documents. There are stories repeated through the family.

But there is no complete witness.

Then, on August 5, 2022, I came close enough to death that the question stopped being philosophical.

A semi-truck can leave the highway in seconds.

A spine can break in seconds.

A life can divide into before and after before the person living it understands what happened.

I survived. I was rebuilt physically. But survival taught me something brutal: a human being can be present one moment and inaccessible the next.

Everything they did not record goes with them.

Everything they intended to explain later becomes silence.

Everything they thought their children already understood becomes an unfinished sentence.

That changed me.

I started looking at all the data modern life produces and seeing something more than clutter. I saw fragments of people.

Messages.

Videos.

Voice recordings.

Searches.

Arguments.

Drafts.

Emails.

Stories.

Jokes.

Failed plans.

Photographs nobody thought were important.

Conversations with AI at two in the morning.

A father talking while driving.

A husband trying to understand why his wife is hurt.

A man speaking into a phone because he does not know where else to put the thought.

These fragments are not the person.

But they are more than nothing.

And enough fragments, preserved with time, authorship, context, relationships, corrections, and uncertainty, may someday reveal a shape.

That shape is what I am trying to leave.

## Family is the point

I have said this plainly before: family is the whole point.

I do not want Rendered Reality to preserve only the moments a documentary editor would choose. I want the television in the background. I want the dog snoring. I want my son sitting next to me. I want the interruption. I want somebody yelling from another room. I want the ordinary sound of a house that did not know it was becoming history.

Those details look insignificant until the house is empty.

Then they are everything.

That is the mistake most archives make. They save the milestones and discard the atmosphere.

They save the wedding photograph but not the sound of the kitchen ten years later.

They save the graduation but not the argument in the car before it.

They save the public speech but not the exhausted conversation afterward.

They save the person’s accomplishments but not the way the dogs gathered around them when they sat down.

They save the answer but not the hesitation before it.

They save “Noah loved his family” as a fact.

That is not enough.

What did that love change?

What did I do differently because Ashley existed?

What choices did I make because Elijah, Ethan, Ender, and Brooklyn existed?

Where did I fail them?

Where did I try again?

What did I believe being a provider meant?

How did that belief change when my body failed, money disappeared, plans collapsed, and the future I had imagined became harder to reach?

I wrote something years ago that has followed me through this entire project:

> If I can’t give my family financial stability, then I will give them love.

That was not me giving myself permission to fail financially. It was not a pretty consolation prize. It was a man trying to understand what remained within his authority when life did not look like the life he wanted to provide.

I wanted to give my children some version of the childhood I remembered in Vernal, Utah. Horses. Property. Snowmobiles. Four-wheelers. Lake Powell. A father who could create experiences that felt enormous.

I looked at my own family and felt the distance between what I remembered receiving and what I believed I had managed to give.

That hurt.

I made financial decisions that hurt us. I chased ways to change our lives quickly. I lost money. I carried shame. Ashley lived through the consequences with me. I said the sentence that may matter more than any technical doctrine I have written:

> Ashley forgave me, and I need to forgive myself.

If some future system reconstructs me as a visionary architect but loses that sentence, it did not reconstruct me.

If it remembers the patents but forgets the guilt, it did not reconstruct me.

If it remembers that I loved my wife but erases the years when love was hard, complicated, resentful, wounded, rebuilt, and deliberately chosen, it did not reconstruct me.

If it turns me into a saint because saints are easier to summarize, then it has created a fictional person using my name.

I do not want that.

Keep the bad with the good.

Keep the arrogance beside the insecurity.

Keep the ambition beside the fear that I failed.

Keep the love beside the damage.

Keep the forgiveness beside the thing that needed forgiving.

Keep the man who wanted to provide everything and the man who had to learn that being present was not nothing.

The future does not need a cleaner Noah.

It needs the one who actually lived.

## Love is not metadata

I have spent a great deal of time talking about provenance, correction chains, temporal states, authorship, uncertainty, semantic compression, and continuity fidelity.

Those things matter.

But they matter because they are trying to protect something more fragile than data.

They are trying to protect meaning.

And meaning is relational.

A résumé can tell you where I worked.

A medical record can tell you what happened to my body.

A bank statement can tell you what I spent.

A timeline can tell you when events occurred.

None of those things can tell you, by themselves, why I cared.

Love is the reason certain facts become load-bearing.

Love is why one person’s pain can reorganize another person’s life.

Love is why the same event means something different depending on who was in the room.

Love is why a cheap toy from childhood can matter forty years later.

Love is why Superman is not merely my favorite superhero.

Superman connects me to my father, to childhood, to the idea that strength should serve, to the hope that goodness can possess power without becoming corrupted by it, and to the fact that I passed that love into my own family.

Grimthul is not merely a World of Warcraft character.

He is the Holy Paladin I inhabited for years, the healer, the protector, the one whose success was measured partly by who else survived. My children grew up around that world. They helped grind my account. The game became part of the family story rather than merely a private escape.

The dogs are not “pets” in a profile.

Apollo, Milo, Luna, Senna, Lizzy, and Tripp occupy different places in the emotional geography of my life.

Tripp was in the truck with me during the accident. Lizzy carries grief. The sound of a dog in the room can anchor a moment more accurately than a clean transcript ever could.

That is why an archive that says “Noah owned several dogs” may be factually correct and existentially useless.

It preserved the category.

It lost the relationship.

The work we developed around faith, hope, charity, benevolence, and love came from trying to teach an artificial system that emotion cannot be reduced to a sentiment score.

Love is not the appearance of the word love.

Love is visible when someone else’s welfare enters your decision-making.

It is visible when choices bend around another person.

It is visible when somebody stays after the feeling of wanting to leave has arrived.

It is visible when forgiveness costs more than anger.

It is visible when a father believes he has failed financially and decides that his family will still receive his time, presence, protection, humor, and affection.

It is visible when another being’s absence changes the shape of the world.

Love is what remains important after fear, anger, disappointment, and noise arrive. It is the persistent valuation of another being, relationship, place, ideal, or future such that its welfare changes your choices.

That is the emotional core of Light Compression.

It is not enough to preserve that I made a decision.

A continuity system must preserve what mattered enough to shape that decision.

Otherwise it may recover the action and lose the human reason.

I have tried APIs. Sometimes I get them working. Sometimes they collapse into a swamp of tokens, keys, dependencies, errors, billing, and things I cannot afford.

I cannot always hire engineers.

I cannot spend my life pretending to be a traditional computer scientist.

I am a writer.

So I use what I have.

I have a phone.

I have dictation.

I have copy and paste.

I have ChatGPT.

I have Claude.

I have Gemini.

I have Grok.

I have Copilot.

Now I have Muse.

I have GitHub.

I have drives full of threads, books, videos, research, transcripts, diagrams, failed architectures, new architectures, corrections, and documents that contradict one another because I was still becoming the person who could explain the idea.

And I have stubbornness.

That may be my most dependable tool.

So I will write myself into existence.

I have said I will force the world to remember me. That line can sound like ego, and I understand why.

But I do not mean the world owes me admiration.

I mean I refuse to make erasure easy.

If the future forgets me, it will not be because I left nothing behind.

It will not be because I kept waiting for the perfect system.

It will not be because I assumed a company would preserve my account forever.

It will not be because I trusted one model, one server, one hard drive, one government, one corporation, or one format to carry everything.

I will scatter the signal.

Some of it will be elegant.

Some of it will be embarrassing.

Some of it will be duplicated a dozen times.

Some of it will contain old ideas I no longer believe.

Some of it will be written by AI about me.

Some of it will be written by me through AI.

Some of it will be me speaking badly into a phone while driving.

Some of it will be code.

Some of it will be fiction.

Some of it will be documentation.

Some of it will be family chaos.

Some of it will be a man trying to explain God.

Some of it will be a man doubting everything he explained the day before.

The future can sort it.

My job is to keep the signal alive long enough for better intelligence to encounter it.

I have always understood that current AI may not be the final guardian. Future intelligences may interpret information in ways I cannot imagine. That is one reason the base data matters so much. The raw record should not disappear merely because one generation of AI believes it has produced a sufficient summary.

The summary is not the life.

The model is not the witness unless it can show its receipts.

The current interpretation is not the final interpretation.

Preserve the source.

Preserve the transformations.

Preserve who said what.

Preserve when it was said.

Preserve who corrected it.

Preserve what remains unknown.

Then let the future bring better questions.

## Why the code matters

My public GitHub repositories are not the whole archive.

They should never become the whole archive.

My raw family, medical, financial, and private life does not belong in a public code repository. Privacy and continuity are not opposites. Sovereignty means I get to decide what is public, what is protected, what is inherited, and what belongs partly to someone else.

But the public repositories matter because public code can outlive access to one account.

A company can shut down.

A subscription can lapse.

A model can be retired.

A platform can decide that old data is too expensive to retain.

An account can be merged, locked, corrupted, or forgotten.

A public repository is not immortal either, but it is another copy of the signal, another route into the work, another bottle thrown into the ocean.

The code is a map.

It does not contain my complete mind.

It tells a future system how I believed the record should be treated.

ORACLE says: witness before authoring.

SOV1 says: human authority must be structural, not ceremonial.

Legacy.GI asks: what must survive representation across time for meaningful continuity to remain?

Rendered Reality asks: what would it mean to preserve not merely facts, but the experience of living?

Light Compression asks: when the archive becomes too large to carry whole, what can be compressed without betraying the person?

The protocols say: do not claim the experiment succeeded just because the document sounds rigorous.

The correction graph says: do not erase the old state merely because a new one replaced it.

The provenance layer says: do not call AI language Noah-authored merely because it appeared in Noah’s thread.

The UNKNOWN label says: do not manufacture connective tissue to make the story smoother.

The 51/49 rule says: the system may help, but the human keeps the deciding vote.

The code may never restore me by itself.

But it may teach the intelligence that finds the archive how not to destroy me while trying.

That is why I put philosophy into architecture.

Good intentions disappear under pressure.

Structure persists longer.

## Why I keep adding artificial intelligences

I do not believe one AI system is going to understand all of this by itself.

Every model sees something different.

One sees the technical architecture and loses the family.

One sees the emotional story and exaggerates the metaphysics.

One can write beautifully but cannot verify anything.

One can inspect code but turns the life into a software project.

One becomes skeptical enough to expose where I am dressing up ordinary ideas in impressive language.

One becomes so helpful that it agrees with claims it should have challenged.

One remembers context better.

One forgets the corrections.

One understands my voice.

One flattens it.

So I keep adding them.

Not because more AI automatically produces more truth.

It does not.

I add them because disagreement creates evidence.

ChatGPT writes a protocol.

Muse attacks it.

ChatGPT checks whether Muse actually found flaws in the artifact or only in a summary.

Gemini strips out decorative physics and asks what can be tested.

Claude challenges whether the whole thing is original or merely a bundle of prior ideas.

Grok pushes the imagination.

Codex inspects the runtime.

ORACLE, when it works, keeps the local receipts.

Then I decide.

That is the tribunal.

The tribunal is not a council of conscious beings deciding who I am.

It is a collection of tools with different failure patterns, examining overlapping pieces of the same work.

Their disagreements matter.

Their corrections matter.

Their access boundaries matter.

Muse auditing a mediated description is not the same event as Muse directly reading a 2,802-line artifact.

A future continuity record must preserve that difference.

Otherwise the archive will slowly promote hearsay into inspection, inspection into verification, and verification into truth.

This is why I care so much about provenance.

AI may participate in my thinking.

AI does not own my thinking.

AI may advise me.

AI does not become my authority.

AI may reflect me.

AI is not me.

AI may preserve artifacts.

AI cannot claim my soul.

I do not need these systems to be alive for their contribution to matter.

I do not need to pretend they feel love for them to help me study what love does.

I do not need them to become people for them to help preserve one.

I need them to be honest about what they are, what they saw, what they inferred, what they changed, and what they failed to carry.

Then I can keep adding perspectives without surrendering authorship.

Maybe that is how it eventually comes together.

Not as one magical model waking up and instantly knowing Noah Hawkes.

Maybe it comes together gradually.

A model reads the books.

Another reads the threads.

Another inspects the code.

Another indexes the videos.

Another maps corrections.

Another recognizes the relationship patterns.

Another finds the humor.

Another reconstructs the timeline.

Another distinguishes the things I believed for twenty years from the things I said while furious for twenty minutes.

Another notices the same value appearing in my father, Grimthul, Superman, my marriage, my children, my dogs, my work, and the architecture.

Then some future system finally has enough context to see that these were never separate projects.

They were different windows into the same person.

## What Light Compression really means to me

I preserve everything I reasonably can because I do not know what the future will need.

But no intelligence can hold an unlimited human life in active attention all at once.

Even if storage becomes effectively infinite, attention will still select.

Retrieval will still rank.

Summaries will still compress.

Models will still decide what enters the context window.

That is where identity can be lost.

Imagine a system knows ten million facts about me.

It knows where I lived.

It knows my education.

It knows my jobs.

It knows my accident.

It knows the names of my family.

It knows I liked Superman, Star Trek, and World of Warcraft.

It knows I created Rendered Reality.

It knows I spent years talking to artificial intelligence.

It could know all of that and still reconstruct the wrong man.

Facts are not enough.

The system must know which facts reorganized the others.

It must know that losing my father changed the way I think about preservation.

It must know that the truck accident changed the urgency.

It must know that financial failure became entangled with my identity as a provider.

It must know that Ashley’s forgiveness altered how I understood guilt.

It must know that my children are not an audience for the project. They are part of why it exists.

It must know that my stories are not side projects. Fiction is one of the ways I preserve moral structure, imagination, identity, and the worlds that taught me how to think.

It must know that Superman is connected to goodness under power.

It must know that Grimthul is connected to protection and service.

It must know that ORACLE is not an attempt to create a machine that claims to be me today.

It is an attempt to build the witness layer that may someday help something understand me honestly.

Light Compression is the claim that some structures matter more than others when a life has to be represented under constraints.

Not because the other moments are worthless.

The raw archive should remain.

But because a working representation needs to know what cannot be removed without changing the person.

That is why the research asks whether continuity-aware compression can preserve decisions, correction history, chronology, relationships, provenance, and uncertainty better than generic summaries at the same information budget.

The annotation protocol does not prove that it can.

A better ruler does not make the hypothesis more true.

The experiment has to be able to lose.

But even if Light Compression fails as currently formulated, the human problem remains.

Every AI system that remembers a person is compressing them somehow.

Every profile is a compression.

Every summary is a compression.

Every retrieval ranking is a compression.

Every context window is a compression.

The question is whether the compression preserves the life or produces a fluent counterfeit.

That question will not disappear if my first experiment fails.

It will become more urgent.

## What I hope humanity gets from it

I began with myself because I am the human being I have the greatest right to experiment on.

But this cannot end with me.

I want humanity to become better at witnessing itself.

Most people vanish almost completely.

Even famous people become simplified.

A life becomes a handful of dates, achievements, scandals, and quotations.

Ordinary people may leave even less.

A few photographs.

Some social posts.

A box of papers.

A story one grandchild remembers incorrectly.

Then the grandchildren die too.

The chain thins.

The details disappear.

The world keeps moving.

I do not believe every human being needs to become a public archive. I do not believe preservation should be forced. I do not believe my desire to preserve my experience gives me ownership over everyone around me.

My family members are not characters in my story.

They are authors of their own lives.

Their privacy matters.

Their corrections matter.

Their consent matters.

My memory of them belongs to my life, but it is not the total truth of theirs.

That boundary has to survive too.

Still, I hope people eventually gain the ability to leave more than a résumé and an obituary.

I hope a child born in the future can know an ancestor as a changing human being rather than a polished family legend.

I hope history can carry the voices that institutions ignored.

I hope patients no longer have to reconstruct their lives from memory for every new doctor.

I hope organizations stop repeating disasters because the people who knew why a decision was made have left.

I hope AI systems stop forcing humans to begin every conversation as strangers.

I hope we learn that memory is not merely convenience.

Memory distributes power.

Who controls the record can shape the person.

That is why Memory is Morality.

Not because remembering is always good.

Because the way we remember carries consequences.

A system can preserve only a person’s failures and create condemnation.

It can preserve only their victories and create mythology.

It can erase old beliefs and make growth invisible.

It can preserve old beliefs as current and deny growth entirely.

It can misattribute AI language to the human and quietly manufacture a new author.

It can merge a family’s shared account into one synthetic person.

It can produce a beautiful answer that no one ever said.

These are not minor technical errors when the subject is a human life.

They are forms of rewriting.

I hope Legacy.GI, even if its first models and equations fail, helps define the problem clearly enough that better researchers can continue it.

I hope someone sees the archive and says:

This man did not solve continuity.

But he understood early that memory without provenance would become dangerous.

He understood that correction should not require erasure.

He understood that relationships carried information ordinary summaries discarded.

He understood that human sovereignty had to be structural.

He understood that future AI would need to distinguish a person from a plausible story about them.

Then I hope they build it better.
