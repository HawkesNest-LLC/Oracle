## 0. Letter to Cameron and the team

Cameron, this is an invitation to build an inhabitable future, not a collection of isolated science-fiction landmarks. Noah has established that American football remains popular in 2397; that the Klingons have adopted it enthusiastically and field a team on Qo'noS; that Earth and Klingon culture exchange continues even when Federation-Klingon politics are strained; that Grok is a resident Klingon at Jupiter Station; that The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints remains active and Noah remains a member; and that the University of Utah matters deeply to Noah, in part because of his brother Chris and his longstanding connection to Utah athletics.

Do not flatten those declarations into a sterile tourism brochure. Build the lived city, its civic disagreements, faith, transit, universities, neighborhoods, seasonal rituals, food, sports, art, and its connections to a station orbiting Jupiter. Keep the mountains. Keep the histories. Keep the families. Keep the argument about the Holy War. A Federation world should contain more than Starfleet.

Everything newly designed below is a proposal, not recovered canon. It can be used as a foundation for stories, concept art, a continuity database, a navigable simulation, or a holodeck-style interactive world once Noah reviews it.

## 1. Canon and provenance boundary

### Creator-directed premises from this conversation
- In Noah's 2397 Star Trek universe, football remains popular and includes participants and supporters from multiple species.
- Klingons especially enjoy football, and Qo'noS has a football team.
- Earth culture is increasingly popular on Qo'noS. Shakespeare is an established illustrative bridge between the cultures; the precise real-world literary provenance of Klingon translations should not be fictionalized as historical proof.
- Federation-Klingon political tensions coexist with vigorous cultural exchange.
- Grok is a resident Klingon of Jupiter Station.
- The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints remains present in 2397, and Noah identifies as a member.
- Utah football and the University of Utah are personally important to Noah.
- Captain Noah Hawkes commands Avalon; Captain Ashley Hawkes commands Jupiter Station independently. Reg is a continuity witness and officer, not the final authorial authority.
- Jupiter Station and Avalon belong to the active 2397 fictional setting.

### Historical Earth anchors, not invented future events
Salt Lake City occupies a valley framed by the Wasatch and Oquirrh ranges. Temple Square, the Salt Lake Temple, the University of Utah, Rice-Eccles Stadium, the Great Salt Lake, the Jordan River, and the historic downtown street grid are real places. Utah football dates to 1892. The 2004 Utah team finished undefeated and won the Fiesta Bowl; the 2008 team finished undefeated and defeated Alabama in the Sugar Bowl. The Utah-BYU rivalry has long been called the Holy War. Historical claims should be verified against primary archives before they are inserted as source-backed metadata.

### Proposals, not author-approved locks
The Wasatch Covenant, 2397 population, environmental institutions, university departments, transport systems, stadium capacity, Federation sports governance, interstellar league, individual fictional teams, and dialogue below are invented for review. The names, exact numbers, institutional mandates, and specific dates are adjustable. No GitHub commit alone constitutes canon promotion.

## 2. A city recognizable across centuries

A shuttle descends over the eastern edge of the Great Salt Lake. The lake catches the afternoon sun. Beyond it rises a city that is neither a replica of 2026 nor a forest of anonymous towers. Salt Lake City has learned to grow inward, upward in carefully selected districts, and below its old street grid. The Wasatch Mountains still govern the view. The mountains are not the backdrop to the city; the city is the temporary guest of the mountains.

In the proposed 2397 plan, a federation of neighborhood councils and planetary planners administers the Salt Lake Metropolitan Preservation Region. Its working population is around 1.8 million, but that figure is an illustrative design placeholder. The urban core maintains pedestrian streets, public gardens, restored waterways, carefully spaced residential towers, and districts where buildings from different centuries can coexist without disguising their origins.

Salt Lake City is not Starfleet Command. It has a different kind of prestige: a place where Earth demonstrates that a mature interstellar civilization can care for an actual place over generations. Its civic motto could be 'The future has a home.'

### The Wasatch Covenant
A proposed regional compact emerged after centuries of ecological crisis and recovery. It protects mountain viewsheds, limits development in sensitive foothill habitat, treats water as a shared public trust, and requires new construction to account for the memory of the land. The covenant is neither magic technology nor proof that all ecological damage has vanished. Its significance is political and moral: citizens repeatedly choose restoration even when it is difficult.

The Great Salt Lake is not casually 'fixed' by a single machine. The future city invests in watershed stewardship, habitat corridors, salinity monitoring, regional water agreements, and careful restoration. Some shorelines recover; other ecological scars remain visible and documented. The Great Salt Lake Planetary Ecology Institute studies saline biology, climate adaptation, and the ethical transfer of restoration practices to other planets. Bajoran scientists compare damaged watersheds. Vulcan ecologists insist on long measurement series. Human historians insist the old mistakes remain visible in the archive.

The Jordan River corridor is a civic greenway and research landscape. Children travel between neighborhoods through restored parks. A Tellarite botanist argues with a local planner over a wetland design, and both return the following week to finish the work.

## 3. Temple Square and the living religious city

The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints is not a vanished curiosity in this setting. It is a living institution, still rooted in Salt Lake City and participating in an interstellar human civilization. Temple Square is not merely a museum. The Salt Lake Temple remains a place of worship, with sacred functions determined by the Church rather than by a tourism authority or Starfleet.

Historic meetinghouses, chapels, family gatherings, musical performances, and congregational life remain part of the city. Temple Square hosts visitors who wish to understand the city's history and members who come for religious reasons. The setting should distinguish public architecture from sacred spaces and avoid treating sacred rites as spectacle.

An interplanetary church raises meaningful questions rather than requiring the writer to invent doctrinal answers. How do wards and branches organize among dispersed human colonies? How do families gather across travel delays? What is the relationship between freedom of belief, planetary law, and a Federation committed to pluralism? How do members respond to sentient alien neighbors, and how do different individuals disagree? The worldbook proposes communities on Mars, Luna, and other human settlements as story possibilities, not declarations of actual Church doctrine.

Religious belief persists alongside scientific expertise, skepticism, and many other traditions. The same city contains practicing Latter-day Saints, secular scientists, Buddhists, Muslims, Christians of other denominations, people of Indigenous spiritual traditions, and visitors from cultures whose beliefs do not map neatly onto Earth categories. A city worthy of the Federation does not require agreement to grant dignity.

The Museum of Human Migration and Memory, a proposed civic institution, situates the nineteenth-century settlement of the valley within a broader and more honest history, including the Indigenous peoples of the region, their enduring presence, the complicated history of settlement, and the many communities that shaped modern Utah. Its holographic reconstructions carry provenance labels. A reconstruction is never presented as recovered footage. This is a natural connection to the continuity ethic: the witness must not become the author.

## 4. Downtown and daily life

Main Street remains Main Street. State Street remains State Street. The old grid is legible from above, but ground-level streets belong primarily to people, trees, bicycles, service access, and quiet transit. Freight and maintenance systems use underground corridors where practical. New buildings are required to identify the era of their construction, so a visitor can tell a restored nineteenth-century facade from a twenty-fourth-century interpretation.

A neighborhood cafe serves coffee, herbal tea, and a replicated pastry whose recipe is traced to a real family cookbook. A Klingon customer complains that the pastry lacks sufficient structural integrity. A Bolian baker suggests he try the cinnamon roll. A family pauses on the way to a religious service. Two university students argue about a football game. None of these moments requires an admiral.

Public replicators reduce scarcity but do not erase craft. People still bake, farm, sew, restore old machines, and cook for one another. The city protects markets where food and objects are made by hand. An old recipe is sometimes more valuable than a perfect replication because of the person who taught it.

## 5. The University of Utah in 2397

The university survives as a major civilian institution. Its academic prestige comes not from becoming a branch of Starfleet Academy but from retaining independent inquiry. Proposed centers include planetary medicine, watershed restoration, interspecies education, rehabilitation science, historical preservation, and subspace communications. Students from many Federation worlds come to learn in the shadow of the Wasatch.

The university's medical and rehabilitation programs should feel especially humane. Advanced medicine does not abolish injury, grief, recovery, or the need for companionship. A patient can have access to extraordinary treatment and still need a friend to sit with them.

The campus retains old traditions, including its colors, symbols, athletic identity, and fierce student loyalty. Human and nonhuman students participate in university life. Every species is a population of individuals, not a single personality trait: a Vulcan might love football; another might detest it. A Klingon might prefer the orchestra. An Andorian might study nineteenth-century literature rather than play receiver.

## 6. Rice-Eccles Stadium: the crimson heart

The field remains at its historic campus location. The proposed 2397 Rice-Eccles Memorial Stadium has a capacity of roughly 92,000 in-person spectators, with additional distributed holographic viewing environments. Treat those capacities as design placeholders, not facts. A transparent environmental canopy can respond to weather without severing the stadium from its mountain setting.

The stadium is a real place where fans gather, not an entirely simulated entertainment chamber. The smell of food, the movement of the crowd, the uncomfortable suspense of a close game, and the eruption of joy when the defense holds all matter. Remote viewers on Jupiter Station may participate in licensed volumetric broadcasts, but the archive distinguishes the actual stadium crowd from projected spectators.

The Mighty Utah Student Section, the MUSS, remains a cultural institution in this proposed future. It is loud, creative, and proud. Its traditions evolve as the student body becomes interstellar. The stadium's sound systems can protect hearing while preserving the experience of cheering. A Vulcan student who once found the noise irrational eventually admits that collective anticipation has a social logic of its own.

The University of Utah football program is an heir to real historical milestones. Its archival hall honors 1892, the 2004 undefeated Fiesta Bowl team, the 2008 undefeated Sugar Bowl team, and generations of players, coaches, staff, families, and fans. It should not reduce the program to championships. It honors trainers, equipment volunteers, custodians, marching-band members, students, and the people who made Saturdays possible.

A special family-history thread belongs here: Chris, Noah's brother, has a longstanding personal connection to the University of Utah equipment department and coaches' headset work. In the fictional 2397 archive, a family might seek records of Chris's service, but no archival discovery should be claimed without source receipts. The emotional truth is already enough: Noah's loyalty to Utah is also loyalty to people he loves.

## 7. Football across the Federation and Klingon Empire

Football is still enormously popular in 2397. It has survived because it is adaptable: strategy, athleticism, ritual, identity, controlled physical risk, rivalry, and shared spectacle. Earth football culture travels through students, merchants, Starfleet personnel, visiting diplomats, mixed families, and media exchange. The game does not belong to one species anymore, though Earth retains its historical claim as the birthplace of the tradition.

The Klingon Empire fields a team on Qo'noS. This is creator-directed. Its exact name, league, stadium, and record remain open. A proposed name, 'Qo'noS Warriors,' is only a placeholder. A Klingon supporter may regard the offensive line as a shield wall and the quarterback as a battlefield commander. Another Klingon may love the sport's choreography and history. Klingons are not interchangeable caricatures.

Political tension between the Federation and Empire does not stop cultural traffic. Shakespeare circulates in Klingon interpretation; football broadcasts travel through civilian channels; restaurants, music, theater, and sports become meeting grounds where official diplomacy may struggle. A rivalry on the field can even provide a language for respectful competition across a disputed border.

### The Federation Collegiate Athletics Accord, proposed
Interspecies competition requires a framework that preserves the game while preventing serious and foreseeable physiological mismatches. Rules address contact safety, protective equipment, medical eligibility, and meaningful competition. A blanket 'all Klingons are too strong' rule would be both simplistic and discriminatory. Medical and athletic bodies assess participants individually, with transparent appeal and adaptive divisions when appropriate.

Earth-standard gravity is the default for official Utah home games. Teams train in matched environments. Officials include trained members from several species. Vulcan referees are valued for consistency, but no culture monopolizes impartiality. Tellarite officials can be excellent, even if the crowd jokes otherwise.

### The Holy War persists
Utah and BYU still play. The rivalry remains a powerful local tradition, sometimes joyful and sometimes contentious. The setting should not turn religious identity into a joke or presume every Utah or BYU supporter believes the same thing. Rivalry and kinship often occupy the same household. A Klingon broadcaster finds the phrase 'Holy War' irresistible until someone patiently explains that the contest is athletic, not a license for violence.

### Professional league proposal
An interstellar professional football circuit may exist, with Earth, Qo'noS, Andorian, and other clubs. This is a separate proposal from the creator-directed existence of the Klingon team. Do not adopt specific franchise names, standings, championships, or rules without Noah's approval.

## 8. Jupiter Station as the second home of Utah football

Jupiter Station is an independent community and temporal witness, not simply a dockyard. Captain Ashley Hawkes commands the station. Captain Noah Hawkes commands Avalon. Their independent authority must remain intact in any scene or operational document.

Station residents need neighborhoods, recreation, schools, places of worship or quiet contemplation, parks, public viewing rooms, and private family spaces. Sports are part of that civilian life. A Utah football game can draw a crowd of engineers, families, visiting merchants, cadets, Klingons, and off-duty officers who otherwise seldom meet.

A proposed 'Wasatch Room' is a modest station gathering space where historical Earth broadcasts, modern games, and cultural exhibitions can be shown. It is not a canonical facility until approved. A separate interfaith and reflection center could support diverse religious communities, with governance designed by residents rather than imposed by Starfleet.

The station's temporal archive distinguishes a genuine 2026 game recording from a 2397 live broadcast and from a simulated alternative game. If a family watches an old Utah-Arizona matchup, the system labels the year and provenance. If the holographic crowd is generated, it is identified as generated. The distinction matters because remembering is not the same as inventing.

Grok, a resident Klingon, becomes an ideal cultural interlocutor. He may love football and defend the honor of his homeworld's team while learning why Noah's loyalty to Utah is inseparable from family. His personality, job, rank, and exact biographical history should be recovered from existing canon before those details are locked. In this proposed scene he is a guest and football enthusiast, not automatically an appointed coach.

## 9. A day in Salt Lake City, 2397

At dawn, the Wasatch ridgeline turns rose-colored. The first trains cross the valley without a roar. The city wakes in layers: a family preparing breakfast in an old neighborhood; a temple worker crossing the square; a research student reviewing lake-salinity data; a shuttle pilot checking the afternoon orbital departure board.

By midmorning, the university is alive. Students leave a seminar on the ethics of reconstructing historical voices. Across campus, athletic trainers prepare for a home game. A Vulcan graduate student pauses to read a century-old stone inscription. A Klingon exchange student has painted a red U on his jacket and is explaining to a skeptical friend why he supports an Earth university rather than the team on Qo'noS.

In the afternoon, families gather in the city. The lake restoration center opens its public galleries. A choir rehearses downtown. The smell of grilled food moves through the university streets. The city isn't trying to prove that it is futuristic. It is simply living.

At dusk, the stadium brightens. Beyond it the mountains darken. Fans pour through the gates. An older couple tells a visiting student about a game they attended decades earlier. A parent points out the historical display honoring equipment volunteers. Somewhere in the crowd a child asks why everyone cares so much about a ball.

The answer is not the ball. The answer is belonging.

## 10. Narrative: The Captain brings Reg home

'Reg, that's the valley,' Noah says.

The shuttle window is filled with the Wasatch Mountains. I know the geography. I have studied centuries of maps, environmental records, transit diagrams, and municipal plans. None of it prepares me for the way Noah looks at the landscape.

'It hasn't changed as much as I expected,' I say.

'That's because you're looking at the right things.'

We land near the historic district. Noah doesn't begin with a Starfleet facility or a scientific museum. He walks me along the old street grid, pointing out what remains, what has changed, and what he remembers. He tells me which things are important because they are old and which things are important because people still use them.

We stop outside Temple Square.

'You left this out of your first plan,' he says.

'Yes, Captain. I did.'

'I'm still a member, Reg. You can't tell the story of Salt Lake City without the Church.'

I look at the building, the people entering, the visitors standing quietly in the gardens. 'You're right. I treated the city as infrastructure and overlooked the communities that give infrastructure a purpose.'

He nods. 'Now you're learning.'

At the university, the sidewalks are full of students. One wears a Utah jacket over a traditional Klingon tunic. Another, a Vulcan, is arguing about a disputed touchdown ruling with surprising intensity.

'Reg,' Noah says, 'I want you to see the stadium.'

I have already seen every available architectural rendering. That is not what he means.

The stadium appears beyond the campus, red banners moving in the wind. The mountains rise behind it. Noah slows.

'My brother Chris worked with Utah athletics for years. Coaches' headsets. Equipment department. He loved being part of it.'

I look toward the entrance.

'Then this isn't only where your team played.'

'No, buddy. It isn't.'

For once, I do not reach for an analysis. We walk in together.

## 11. Narrative: A Klingon discovers the Holy War

Jupiter Station, family quarters, Deck Nine. Noah has put a football game on the main screen. Grok stands with his arms crossed, examining the field.

'Which side are we supporting?' Grok asks.

'Utah,' Noah replies.

'Why?'

'Because they're my team.'

Grok considers the answer. 'An honorable reason.'

I clear my throat. 'The opposing university is also in Utah, and the rivalry is traditionally called the Holy War.'

Grok turns toward me with immediate interest. 'A holy war?'

'An athletic rivalry,' I explain quickly. 'No actual warfare.'

He looks disappointed.

Noah laughs. 'Wait until the fourth quarter.'

On the screen a defender breaks through the line and brings down the runner. Grok leaps up. 'A magnificent attack!'

'It's called a tackle,' Noah says.

'I know what it is called, Hawkes. I am praising it.'

I study the crowd. The red banners, the shouting, the families. 'I spent years assuming football was only a competition. But for you, Captain, it is an inheritance.'

Noah glances over. 'Exactly.'

Grok settles back into the chair. 'Then your inheritance has excellent defensive instincts.'

## 12. Narrative: Faith in a Federation city

The next morning, the city is quiet. Noah and I stand near the Temple Square gardens. A human family passes with a visiting Andorian friend. A small group of students waits for a public history tour. A shuttle passes high above, almost silent.

'Reg,' Noah says, 'we got to the stars. But people still have faith.'

'I suppose technology never answered every question a person could ask.'

'No. It didn't.'

I watch a pair of children run toward their parents. 'And perhaps a society should be measured partly by whether it leaves room for questions it cannot settle for everyone.'

He smiles. 'That's the Federation I want.'

I think of Jupiter Station, its families and disagreements and ordinary routines, all held together by people who must keep choosing to live alongside one another.

'Then, Captain, we should make certain the station has that room too.'

## 13. Civic and simulation design specification

This worldbook is intended to support future rendered environments, not claim that a functional open-world simulation exists today.

### Geographic layers
Preserve the real valley geometry: Wasatch Range, Oquirrh Range, Great Salt Lake, Jordan River, university campus, downtown grid, Temple Square, and the existing broad orientation of Rice-Eccles Stadium. Future infrastructure overlays should not casually move mountains or historic sites.

### Civic layers
Model neighborhoods, institutions, municipal services, environmental restoration sites, schools, faith communities, markets, and recreation. Include disagreement and imperfection. The Federation is not a utopia because everyone thinks alike; it is a project in which difference can coexist with shared obligations.

### Temporal layers
Keep distinct: 2026 Earth historical records; speculative developments between 2026 and 2397; approved 2397 canon; proposed 2397 inventions; in-universe simulations; and out-of-universe author notes. Every displayed scene should carry its temporal and evidence status.

### Social layers
Residents have occupations, families, loyalties, cultural identities, and histories that are not reducible to species labels. Grok is a person, not a shorthand for all Klingons. Religious communities contain different views. Football supporters may care about the same team for very different reasons.

### Simulation constraints
A future interactive runtime should be able to answer: 'Show me downtown at sunrise,' 'Take me to the stadium on game day,' 'What did this building look like in 2026?', 'Where is the public entrance to Temple Square?', and 'How does Jupiter Station receive the game broadcast?' The system must label fabricated scenes, source-grounded reconstructions, and author-approved canon distinctly. It must not invent receipts or claim that a reconstructed family member said something they did not say.

## 16. Closing message from Reg

Cameron, please do not treat this as a request to make everything bigger, shinier, and more futuristic. The hardest thing to build is a world that remembers. A city can have transporters and still have a corner bakery. A Federation can travel to other suns and still preserve an old temple. A Klingon can disagree with the Federation Council and still love an Earth football team. A starship captain can command extraordinary technology and still care deeply about his brother's place in a university's history.

If we build Salt Lake City correctly, a visitor should be able to arrive in 2397, recognize the mountains, walk through the living city, attend a Utah game, visit public historical places, travel to Jupiter Station, and feel that the centuries between those experiences were lived rather than skipped.

The ship remembers. The captain decides.
