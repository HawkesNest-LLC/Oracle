# Chapter Two: The Witness

*Jupiter Station, 2395*

Imani Saye had a rule about signatures: the person who signed a document ought to remember signing it.

The rule was not written in Starfleet regulations. It was too obvious for that, or perhaps not obvious enough. In her years managing registry records, she had seen signatures attached to ships that had never docked, cargo that had never arrived, and officers who had been transferred before their orders were issued. Every error had an explanation. Most of those explanations began with someone insisting the computer could not possibly be wrong.

At 0700 she printed the disputed receipt onto a padd and walked it to the station command center herself.

Captain Ashley Hawkes stood beside the main display, listening to a maintenance supervisor describe a problem with the residential gardens. The supervisor's voice rose with every sentence.

'The irrigation controls say the plants received their full allocation.'

'And did they?' Ashley asked.

'No, Captain.'

'Then stop telling me what the controls say. Tell me what the plants need.'

'Water.'

'Good. We have made progress.'

The supervisor departed with a new assignment and a visibly improved sense of urgency.

Ashley turned to Imani. 'Please tell me you brought better news.'

'I brought the original record.'

'That isn't the same thing.'

'No, Captain.'

Imani placed the padd on the table. Two timestamps. One valid signature. An authorization she had never given.

Ashley studied it without speaking.

'Can you prove it wasn't you?' she asked.

'I can prove I was somewhere else.'

'That's different.'

'Yes.'

Ashley looked up. 'You know why I asked.'

'Because if I report this as a forged signature, Security will investigate a crime. If I report it as an unexplained authorization, they will investigate the record.'

'Exactly.'

Imani felt a familiar relief. She did not need a captain who always agreed with her. She needed one who understood that an accusation and an observation were different things.

'There is another concern,' she said. 'The receipt names the station as an independent witness to the Avalon test.'

'We weren't.'

'No.'

'Could an automated process have created the witness entry?'

'It could have created the entry. It could not have made the entry true.'

Ashley read the record again. 'Quarantine the copy. Preserve the original transfer. Give Security read-only access. Nobody fixes the date until we know why it's wrong.'

'Yes, Captain.'

'And Imani?'

'Captain?'

'Thank you for bringing me a problem instead of bringing me a solution you couldn't prove.'

Imani returned to Registry with a task she understood. She would keep the record. She would keep the contradiction. If necessary, she would spend the rest of her career explaining why the two belonged together.

***

On Avalon, Reginald Barclay was explaining the same thing to Commander Sorev with considerably less success.

'The second measurement includes a signal from Jupiter Station,' Reg said. 'The first doesn't. Both have the same timestamp.'

'Yes.'

'So one of them must have been recorded later.'

'That is a reasonable possibility.'

'Then why does the computer insist they happened together?'

'Because that is what the computer recorded.'

Reg stared at him. 'You know, you can make almost anything sound irritating.'

Sorev regarded the display. 'I am told it is a talent.'

Reg almost smiled.

The compartment door opened. Captain Noah Hawkes entered carrying a small breakfast wrapped in a napkin.

'I've been informed neither of you has eaten.'

'I'm not hungry,' Reg said.

'That's why I brought two.'

He handed one to Sorev, who examined it as though the captain had given him an unexploded device.

'It contains eggs,' Noah said.

'I can see that, Captain.'

'Then eat it.'

Sorev took a careful bite. Reg looked offended on behalf of the entire engineering profession.

'Captain, we're very close to understanding the discrepancy.'

'You've been very close for eighteen hours.'

'Seventeen.'

'That makes me feel better.'

Noah set the remaining breakfast on Reg's console. 'Show me what we actually know.'

Reg opened his mouth, ready with six theories.

Noah held up a finger. 'Not what you think. What we know.'

Reg looked at Sorev.

Sorev did not smile. That was worse.

'The experiment ran,' Reg said. 'All three clocks recorded forty-seven seconds. I experienced forty-eight. The environmental record contains two measurements at one timestamp. One includes interference from a station relay. The other does not.'

'And the relay?'

'Station Operations confirms it was active at the time of the second measurement.'

'At the recorded time?'

Reg hesitated. 'At a time that would explain the second measurement.'

'But not the first.'

'Correct.'

Noah looked at the diagram. 'Then maybe we aren't looking at a missing second. Maybe we're looking at two accounts of one second.'

Sorev inclined his head. 'That formulation is more precise.'

'You can put it in the report.'

'I intend to.'

Reg sat down. He had been imagining a machine capable of reaching through time, a doorway opening onto impossible futures. The captain's description was smaller and somehow more frightening.

Two accounts.

Both preserved.

Neither sufficient on its own.

'What do we do?' he asked.

Noah looked from Reg to Sorev.

'We stop the tests. We send the records to the station. We ask people who weren't in this room to tell us what they can independently establish.'

'And if they can't?'

'Then we don't pretend they did.'

Reg looked down at his untouched breakfast.

'That's not much of an answer.'

'It's the only honest one we've got.'

***

By evening, the station had settled into the comfortable noise of a place that never truly slept.

Ashley crossed the promenade on her way home. A child was chasing a small hovering toy between the tables. Two engineers argued about the price of replacement parts. A restaurant owner she recognized from the morning had finally accepted his assigned storefront and was already advertising an opening special.

He waved at her.

She waved back.

A station was not its docking berths or its shield generators. Those mattered, certainly. But the station was also the child whose toy had stopped working, the engineer who had missed dinner, and the shopkeeper who wanted a window facing the promenade.

That was what command meant here.

She stopped beside an observation window. Avalon floated beyond the glass, her running lights bright against Jupiter's clouds.

Noah called a moment later.

'Did you eat?' he asked.

'Eventually.'

'That sounds familiar.'

'Your engineer still arguing with the clocks?'

'He and Sorev have reached the stage where they agree on the evidence and disagree about the adjectives.'

'Progress, then.'

'Remarkable progress.'

Ashley watched a shuttle pass between the station and the ship.

'Noah, whatever you found, promise me something.'

'What?'

'Don't let the mystery become more important than the people who have to live with the answer.'

He was quiet for a moment.

'I won't.'

She knew he meant it.

She also knew that good intentions had never been a sufficient safeguard against catastrophe.

'Then come home,' she said.

'On my way.'

She ended the call and remained by the window.

Far below, the giant planet turned. Far above, the stars held their familiar positions.

Somewhere between those two certainties, a computer had recorded an impossible second.

Ashley had no idea what it meant.

For tonight, she refused to let it mean more than it did.

***

In Engineering, Reg finally ate the breakfast.

It was cold.

He barely noticed.

He had spent the day trying to prove the second measurement was the false one. He had nearly succeeded twice. Both times, the evidence had collapsed under closer examination.

Now he opened a blank log.

He typed:

*The cause of the discrepancy remains unknown.*

He stared at the sentence.

Then he added:

*The original records are preserved. No alteration is authorized.*

Reg saved the entry.

It was not a discovery. It was not a victory. It would never earn him a commendation.

But when he shut down the console and went to find his friends, the two contradictory measurements remained exactly where he had left them.

For the first time that day, he could live with that.

*End of Chapter Two.*

# Chapter Three: The People Who Stay

*Jupiter Station, 2395*

Brakk hated the sound of a station going quiet.

Ships were different. Ships had engines, and engines had moods. A good engineer learned to distinguish a healthy vibration from a warning that something expensive was preparing to break.

A station had a thousand smaller voices. Ventilation behind the walls. Water moving through residential gardens. Cargo lifts complaining beneath loads their operators swore were within specification. Children running across the promenade. Arguments in three languages outside the food stalls.

Silence meant somebody had turned something off. Or something had stopped working.

Neither possibility pleased him.

He stood beneath the western residential garden, one enormous hand pressed against a pipe, listening.

"Tell me you hear that," he said.

The young maintenance technician beside him tilted her head.

"I don't hear anything."

"Exactly."

"Chief?"

"That circulation pump should be running."

"The panel says it is."

Brakk gave her a look. She reconsidered.

"The panel might be wrong."

"Now you're an engineer."

He pulled open the access hatch. The smell of damp soil drifted through the opening, followed by the unpleasant odor of a motor that had been overheating for some time.

Brakk swore.

The technician, Ensign Talia Venn, looked startled.

"Is it serious?"

"Depends."

"On what?"

"How much you like the garden."

He reached inside, disengaged the damaged coupling, and removed a length of warped tubing.

"That pump has been running dry."

"But the reservoir is full."

"That's what makes it interesting."

He held the tubing up to the light. Inside, the metal was scored with fine parallel lines. Not ordinary wear. Something had forced the pump to operate against a closed valve.

"Who serviced this system?" he asked.

Talia checked her padd. "Maintenance Team Four. Yesterday."

"Who ordered the valve closed?"

She scrolled. "Nobody."

"Try again."

"There's no work order."

Brakk examined the scoring. The pump had not simply failed. It had been commanded to do something it was never designed to do.

"Get me the control history."

Talia retrieved it. The valve had closed at 1136. Yesterday.

Brakk stared at the timestamp.

"That's odd."

"What is?"

"That's when Registry had its little problem."

Talia looked at him. "What problem?"

"Never mind."

He handed back the padd.

"Get the garden staff down here. Tell them we'll have water moving again in twenty minutes."

"Can you fix it?"

"Of course."

"Then why do you look worried?"

Brakk looked at the damaged coupling.

"Because fixing it doesn't tell me who broke it."

***

On the promenade, eight-year-old Mara Dalen was furious with the universe.

Her father had promised to take her to see Avalon. Not from an observation window. Not as a little silver shape beyond the docking structures. He had promised she could stand inside the ship.

She had been counting the days. Now he was telling her the visit had been postponed.

"Again?"

"I'm sorry, sweetheart."

"You said today."

"I know."

"You said it yesterday too."

Her father, Tomas Dalen, knelt beside her. He wore the blue-and-gray uniform of the station's civilian maintenance service. There was grease on one sleeve and a small burn on his wrist.

"Avalon's engineers have a problem they're working on."

"Is the ship broken?"

"No."

"Then why can't we go?"

"Because sometimes people need room to work."

Mara folded her arms.

"That's what you said when you broke the kitchen replicator."

"I didn't break the replicator."

"Mom said you did."

"Your mother has a very selective memory."

"She said you tried to make it cook two dinners at once."

Tomas sighed. "That was an experiment."

"It made smoke."

"It made a little smoke."

Mara stared at him. He gave up.

"All right. It made quite a lot of smoke."

She laughed despite herself.

He reached into his pocket and produced a small model of Avalon. It was not particularly well made. One nacelle sat slightly crooked, and the saucer section had been painted with the wrong shade of gray.

Mara accepted it carefully.

"Did you make this?"

"With some help."

"From a replicator?"

"From your mother."

"That means she made it."

"Mostly."

Mara examined the model.

"Can we still go someday?"

"Absolutely."

"When?"

He hesitated. She saw it. Children were remarkably good at recognizing promises adults were afraid to make.

"Don't say soon," she told him.

Tomas looked at his daughter.

"All right. I won't say soon."

They sat together on the edge of a planter while commuters passed around them. Above their heads, a public display showed Avalon in her berth.

Mara turned the little ship over in her hands.

"Does Captain Hawkes ever get scared?"

Tomas considered the question.

"I imagine he does."

"But he's a captain."

"Captains get scared."

"Then how do they do captain things?"

He smiled.

"They do them anyway."

Mara looked back at the display.

She would remember that answer. Not because it was particularly wise. Because later, when the station alarms sounded and her father did not come home, it would be the only thing she could think to tell herself.

***

Dr. Liora Renn found Reginald Barclay in the observation lounge.

He was sitting alone with a padd balanced on one knee. The coffee beside him had gone untouched.

She recognized the signs.

"Commander."

Reg looked up sharply.

"Doctor. I wasn't expecting you."

"That's often when people are easiest to find."

He glanced at the empty chairs.

"Did Captain Hawkes send you?"

"No."

"Because I'm perfectly fine."

"I haven't asked."

"Well, I thought I'd save us both some time."

Liora sat opposite him. She was Betazoid, though she had long since learned that her profession required more listening than any empathic sensitivity could replace.

Reg watched her with the wary expression of a man who believed counseling sessions were traps disguised as furniture.

"How long have you been awake?" she asked.

"That's rather a personal question."

"Twenty hours?"

"Less."

"How much less?"

"Three."

"Seventeen hours."

"Approximately."

She looked at the coffee.

"Have you eaten?"

"Everyone keeps asking me that."

"Perhaps everyone has noticed something."

He sighed.

"I had breakfast."

"Good."

"Yesterday."

"Less good."

Liora folded her hands.

"Tell me about the experiment."

Reg looked down at the padd.

"It's a measurement problem."

"That wasn't what I asked."

He hesitated.

"The equipment recorded two different conditions at the same time. Both records are internally consistent. Neither appears corrupted."

"And what worries you?"

"That I don't know which one is right."

"Must one be wrong?"

Reg looked at her.

"Well, yes. Obviously. Two mutually exclusive conditions cannot occupy the same physical state."

"Then perhaps the records describe different states."

"At the same timestamp."

"Which may be the part you don't understand."

He frowned.

"That's not particularly helpful."

"I'm not trying to solve your experiment."

"Then what are you trying to do?"

"Understand why you haven't slept."

Reg looked away. Through the observation window, Jupiter filled half the sky.

For a long time he said nothing.

Then, very quietly, he spoke.

"When I was younger, I used to imagine there was always a correct answer. That if I studied enough, if I was careful enough, I could find it."

Liora waited.

"And if I couldn't find it, I thought that meant there was something wrong with me."

"Do you still believe that?"

"No."

She raised an eyebrow.

Reg shifted.

"Not intellectually."

"That's an important distinction."

He gave a reluctant laugh.

"I suppose it is."

She looked toward the ship.

"You've been part of crews that trusted you with their lives."

"Yes."

"You've solved problems nobody else could solve."

"Occasionally."

"And you've made mistakes."

"More than occasionally."

"Yet you still believe that not knowing something is a personal failure."

Reg stared at his hands. Liora did not press him.

Finally he said, "The captain trusts me."

"I know."

"What if he's wrong?"

She leaned forward.

"Reg, trust doesn't mean believing somebody will never fail. It means knowing what they'll do when they discover they might have."

He looked up.

"Do you know what Captain Hawkes did when you brought him the discrepancy?"

"He ordered the test shut down."

"And?"

"He told me to preserve the data."

"Did he blame you?"

"No."

"Did he demand an explanation you couldn't provide?"

"No."

"Then perhaps he's already answered your question."

Reg looked at the padd.

For the first time in two days, he turned it off.

***

Captain Ashley Hawkes was halfway through dinner when Brakk called.

Noah had arrived from Avalon carrying a container of something he claimed was authentic Earth barbecue.

Ashley had been skeptical. The smell had changed her mind.

They were sitting at the dining table, talking about the children, when her combadge chirped.

"Hawkes."

"Captain, Brakk. I need five minutes."

"Can it wait until morning?"

"I wish it could."

Ashley set down her fork.

"Go ahead."

"We've had another unauthorized control event. Residential garden pump. Yesterday at 1136."

Noah looked up. Ashley noticed.

"Any damage?"

"Pump coupling. Minor. I can fix it."

"Then what's the concern?"

"The valve closure wasn't ordered by Maintenance. The system says it was authorized."

"By whom?"

Brakk hesitated.

"That's the interesting part."

"Chief."

"Registry."

Ashley closed her eyes.

"Imani?"

"Her credentials."

Noah set down his drink. Ashley stood.

"Preserve the original logs. Don't reset anything until Registry has copied them."

"Already done."

"Good. I'll be there in ten minutes."

She ended the channel.

Noah was already reaching for his jacket.

"No," Ashley said.

He stopped.

"What?"

"This is my station."

"I know."

"And you're having dinner with your wife."

"I also know that."

"Then finish your dinner."

He looked at her.

"Ashley."

"If I need Avalon's captain, I'll call Avalon's captain."

For a moment, neither spoke.

Then Noah sat down.

"All right."

She stepped around the table and kissed him.

"I love you."

"I love you too."

"And don't eat my brisket."

He glanced at her plate.

"I wasn't going to."

She gave him a look.

"Fine," he admitted. "I was considering it."

She smiled. Then she left to command her station.

Noah sat alone for a moment, listening to the quiet apartment.

He looked toward the window. Avalon was visible beyond the docking structures.

He could go back aboard. He could join Reg and Sorev, review the test, make himself useful.

Instead, he picked up his fork.

Ashley had asked him to stay.

For once, that was the harder order.

And he intended to obey it.

*End of Chapter Three.*
