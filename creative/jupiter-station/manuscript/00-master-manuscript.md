# Rendered Reality: Star Trek Jupiter Station

*Working novel manuscript. Candidate fiction, not creator-promoted canon.*

> Chapter One, The Second That Wasn't There, is present in the commissioning conversation but has not been recovered into this repository. This master deliberately does not invent or replace its text.

# Chapter Two: The Witness

*Jupiter Station, 2395*

Imani Saye had a rule about signatures: the person who signed a document ought to remember signing it.

The rule was not written in Starfleet regulations. It was too obvious for that, or perhaps not obvious enough. In her years managing registry records, she had seen signatures attached to ships that had never docked, cargo that had never arrived, and officers who had been transferred before their orders were issued. Every error had an explanation. Most of those explanations began with someone insisting the computer could not possibly be wrong.

At 0700 she printed the disputed receipt onto a padd and walked it to the station command center herself.

Captain Ashley Hawkes stood beside the main display, listening to a maintenance supervisor describe a problem with the residential gardens. The supervisor's voice rose with every sentence.

'The irrigation controls say the plants received their full allocation.'

'And did they?' Ashley asked.

'No, Captain.'

'Then stop telling me what the controls say. Tell me what the plants need.'

'Water.'

'Good. We have made progress.'

The supervisor departed with a new assignment and a visibly improved sense of urgency.

Ashley turned to Imani. 'Please tell me you brought better news.'

'I brought the original record.'

'That isn't the same thing.'

'No, Captain.'

Imani placed the padd on the table. Two timestamps. One valid signature. An authorization she had never given.

Ashley studied it without speaking.

'Can you prove it wasn't you?' she asked.

'I can prove I was somewhere else.'

'That's different.'

'Yes.'

Ashley looked up. 'You know why I asked.'

'Because if I report this as a forged signature, Security will investigate a crime. If I report it as an unexplained authorization, they will investigate the record.'

'Exactly.'

Imani felt a familiar relief. She did not need a captain who always agreed with her. She needed one who understood that an accusation and an observation were different things.

'There is another concern,' she said. 'The receipt names the station as an independent witness to the Avalon test.'

'We weren't.'

'No.'

'Could an automated process have created the witness entry?'

'It could have created the entry. It could not have made the entry true.'

Ashley read the record again. 'Quarantine the copy. Preserve the original transfer. Give Security read-only access. Nobody fixes the date until we know why it's wrong.'

'Yes, Captain.'

'And Imani?'

'Captain?'

'Thank you for bringing me a problem instead of bringing me a solution you couldn't prove.'

Imani returned to Registry with a task she understood. She would keep the record. She would keep the contradiction. If necessary, she would spend the rest of her career explaining why the two belonged together.

***

On Avalon, Reginald Barclay was explaining the same thing to Commander Sorev with considerably less success.

'The second measurement includes a signal from Jupiter Station,' Reg said. 'The first doesn't. Both have the same timestamp.'

'Yes.'

'So one of them must have been recorded later.'

'That is a reasonable possibility.'

'Then why does the computer insist they happened together?'

'Because that is what the computer recorded.'

Reg stared at him. 'You know, you can make almost anything sound irritating.'

Sorev regarded the display. 'I am told it is a talent.'

Reg almost smiled.

The compartment door opened. Captain Noah Hawkes entered carrying a small breakfast wrapped in a napkin.

'I've been informed neither of you has eaten.'

'I'm not hungry,' Reg said.

'That's why I brought two.'

He handed one to Sorev, who examined it as though the captain had given him an unexploded device.

'It contains eggs,' Noah said.

'I can see that, Captain.'

'Then eat it.'

Sorev took a careful bite. Reg looked offended on behalf of the entire engineering profession.

'Captain, we're very close to understanding the discrepancy.'

'You've been very close for eighteen hours.'

'Seventeen.'

'That makes me feel better.'

Noah set the remaining breakfast on Reg's console. 'Show me what we actually know.'

Reg opened his mouth, ready with six theories.

Noah held up a finger. 'Not what you think. What we know.'

Reg looked at Sorev.

Sorev did not smile. That was worse.

'The experiment ran,' Reg said. 'All three clocks recorded forty-seven seconds. I experienced forty-eight. The environmental record contains two measurements at one timestamp. One includes interference from a station relay. The other does not.'

'And the relay?'

'Station Operations confirms it was active at the time of the second measurement.'

'At the recorded time?'

Reg hesitated. 'At a time that would explain the second measurement.'

'But not the first.'

'Correct.'

Noah looked at the diagram. 'Then maybe we aren't looking at a missing second. Maybe we're looking at two accounts of one second.'

Sorev inclined his head. 'That formulation is more precise.'

'You can put it in the report.'

'I intend to.'

Reg sat down. He had been imagining a machine capable of reaching through time, a doorway opening onto impossible futures. The captain's description was smaller and somehow more frightening.

Two accounts.

Both preserved.

Neither sufficient on its own.

'What do we do?' he asked.

Noah looked from Reg to Sorev.

'We stop the tests. We send the records to the station. We ask people who weren't in this room to tell us what they can independently establish.'

'And if they can't?'

'Then we don't pretend they did.'

Reg looked down at his untouched breakfast.

'That's not much of an answer.'

'It's the only honest one we've got.'

***

By evening, the station had settled into the comfortable noise of a place that never truly slept.

Ashley crossed the promenade on her way home. A child was chasing a small hovering toy between the tables. Two engineers argued about the price of replacement parts. A restaurant owner she recognized from the morning had finally accepted his assigned storefront and was already advertising an opening special.

He waved at her.

She waved back.

A station was not its docking berths or its shield generators. Those mattered, certainly. But the station was also the child whose toy had stopped working, the engineer who had missed dinner, and the shopkeeper who wanted a window facing the promenade.

That was what command meant here.

She stopped beside an observation window. Avalon floated beyond the glass, her running lights bright against Jupiter's clouds.

Noah called a moment later.

'Did you eat?' he asked.

'Eventually.'

'That sounds familiar.'

'Your engineer still arguing with the clocks?'

'He and Sorev have reached the stage where they agree on the evidence and disagree about the adjectives.'

'Progress, then.'

'Remarkable progress.'

Ashley watched a shuttle pass between the station and the ship.

'Noah, whatever you found, promise me something.'

'What?'

'Don't let the mystery become more important than the people who have to live with the answer.'

He was quiet for a moment.

'I won't.'

She knew he meant it.

She also knew that good intentions had never been a sufficient safeguard against catastrophe.

'Then come home,' she said.

'On my way.'

She ended the call and remained by the window.

Far below, the giant planet turned. Far above, the stars held their familiar positions.

Somewhere between those two certainties, a computer had recorded an impossible second.

Ashley had no idea what it meant.

For tonight, she refused to let it mean more than it did.

***

In Engineering, Reg finally ate the breakfast.

It was cold.

He barely noticed.

He had spent the day trying to prove the second measurement was the false one. He had nearly succeeded twice. Both times, the evidence had collapsed under closer examination.

Now he opened a blank log.

He typed:

*The cause of the discrepancy remains unknown.*

He stared at the sentence.

Then he added:

*The original records are preserved. No alteration is authorized.*

Reg saved the entry.

It was not a discovery. It was not a victory. It would never earn him a commendation.

But when he shut down the console and went to find his friends, the two contradictory measurements remained exactly where he had left them.

For the first time that day, he could live with that.

*End of Chapter Two.*

# Chapter Three: The People Who Stay

*Jupiter Station, 2395*

Brakk hated the sound of a station going quiet.

Ships were different. Ships had engines, and engines had moods. A good engineer learned to distinguish a healthy vibration from a warning that something expensive was preparing to break.

A station had a thousand smaller voices. Ventilation behind the walls. Water moving through residential gardens. Cargo lifts complaining beneath loads their operators swore were within specification. Children running across the promenade. Arguments in three languages outside the food stalls.

Silence meant somebody had turned something off. Or something had stopped working.

Neither possibility pleased him.

He stood beneath the western residential garden, one enormous hand pressed against a pipe, listening.

"Tell me you hear that," he said.

The young maintenance technician beside him tilted her head.

"I don't hear anything."

"Exactly."

"Chief?"

"That circulation pump should be running."

"The panel says it is."

Brakk gave her a look. She reconsidered.

"The panel might be wrong."

"Now you're an engineer."

He pulled open the access hatch. The smell of damp soil drifted through the opening, followed by the unpleasant odor of a motor that had been overheating for some time.

Brakk swore.

The technician, Ensign Talia Venn, looked startled.

"Is it serious?"

"Depends."

"On what?"

"How much you like the garden."

He reached inside, disengaged the damaged coupling, and removed a length of warped tubing.

"That pump has been running dry."

"But the reservoir is full."

"That's what makes it interesting."

He held the tubing up to the light. Inside, the metal was scored with fine parallel lines. Not ordinary wear. Something had forced the pump to operate against a closed valve.

"Who serviced this system?" he asked.

Talia checked her padd. "Maintenance Team Four. Yesterday."

"Who ordered the valve closed?"

She scrolled. "Nobody."

"Try again."

"There's no work order."

Brakk examined the scoring. The pump had not simply failed. It had been commanded to do something it was never designed to do.

"Get me the control history."

Talia retrieved it. The valve had closed at 1136. Yesterday.

Brakk stared at the timestamp.

"That's odd."

"What is?"

"That's when Registry had its little problem."

Talia looked at him. "What problem?"

"Never mind."

He handed back the padd.

"Get the garden staff down here. Tell them we'll have water moving again in twenty minutes."

"Can you fix it?"

"Of course."

"Then why do you look worried?"

Brakk looked at the damaged coupling.

"Because fixing it doesn't tell me who broke it."

***

On the promenade, eight-year-old Mara Dalen was furious with the universe.

Her father had promised to take her to see Avalon. Not from an observation window. Not as a little silver shape beyond the docking structures. He had promised she could stand inside the ship.

She had been counting the days. Now he was telling her the visit had been postponed.

"Again?"

"I'm sorry, sweetheart."

"You said today."

"I know."

"You said it yesterday too."

Her father, Tomas Dalen, knelt beside her. He wore the blue-and-gray uniform of the station's civilian maintenance service. There was grease on one sleeve and a small burn on his wrist.

"Avalon's engineers have a problem they're working on."

"Is the ship broken?"

"No."

"Then why can't we go?"

"Because sometimes people need room to work."

Mara folded her arms.

"That's what you said when you broke the kitchen replicator."

"I didn't break the replicator."

"Mom said you did."

"Your mother has a very selective memory."

"She said you tried to make it cook two dinners at once."

Tomas sighed. "That was an experiment."

"It made smoke."

"It made a little smoke."

Mara stared at him. He gave up.

"All right. It made quite a lot of smoke."

She laughed despite herself.

He reached into his pocket and produced a small model of Avalon. It was not particularly well made. One nacelle sat slightly crooked, and the saucer section had been painted with the wrong shade of gray.

Mara accepted it carefully.

"Did you make this?"

"With some help."

"From a replicator?"

"From your mother."

"That means she made it."

"Mostly."

Mara examined the model.

"Can we still go someday?"

"Absolutely."

"When?"

He hesitated. She saw it. Children were remarkably good at recognizing promises adults were afraid to make.

"Don't say soon," she told him.

Tomas looked at his daughter.

"All right. I won't say soon."

They sat together on the edge of a planter while commuters passed around them. Above their heads, a public display showed Avalon in her berth.

Mara turned the little ship over in her hands.

"Does Captain Hawkes ever get scared?"

Tomas considered the question.

"I imagine he does."

"But he's a captain."

"Captains get scared."

"Then how do they do captain things?"

He smiled.

"They do them anyway."

Mara looked back at the display.

She would remember that answer. Not because it was particularly wise. Because later, when the station alarms sounded and her father did not come home, it would be the only thing she could think to tell herself.

***

Dr. Liora Renn found Reginald Barclay in the observation lounge.

He was sitting alone with a padd balanced on one knee. The coffee beside him had gone untouched.

She recognized the signs.

"Commander."

Reg looked up sharply.

"Doctor. I wasn't expecting you."

"That's often when people are easiest to find."

He glanced at the empty chairs.

"Did Captain Hawkes send you?"

"No."

"Because I'm perfectly fine."

"I haven't asked."

"Well, I thought I'd save us both some time."

Liora sat opposite him. She was Betazoid, though she had long since learned that her profession required more listening than any empathic sensitivity could replace.

Reg watched her with the wary expression of a man who believed counseling sessions were traps disguised as furniture.

"How long have you been awake?" she asked.

"That's rather a personal question."

"Twenty hours?"

"Less."

"How much less?"

"Three."

"Seventeen hours."

"Approximately."

She looked at the coffee.

"Have you eaten?"

"Everyone keeps asking me that."

"Perhaps everyone has noticed something."

He sighed.

"I had breakfast."

"Good."

"Yesterday."

"Less good."

Liora folded her hands.

"Tell me about the experiment."

Reg looked down at the padd.

"It's a measurement problem."

"That wasn't what I asked."

He hesitated.

"The equipment recorded two different conditions at the same time. Both records are internally consistent. Neither appears corrupted."

"And what worries you?"

"That I don't know which one is right."

"Must one be wrong?"

Reg looked at her.

"Well, yes. Obviously. Two mutually exclusive conditions cannot occupy the same physical state."

"Then perhaps the records describe different states."

"At the same timestamp."

"Which may be the part you don't understand."

He frowned.

"That's not particularly helpful."

"I'm not trying to solve your experiment."

"Then what are you trying to do?"

"Understand why you haven't slept."

Reg looked away. Through the observation window, Jupiter filled half the sky.

For a long time he said nothing.

Then, very quietly, he spoke.

"When I was younger, I used to imagine there was always a correct answer. That if I studied enough, if I was careful enough, I could find it."

Liora waited.

"And if I couldn't find it, I thought that meant there was something wrong with me."

"Do you still believe that?"

"No."

She raised an eyebrow.

Reg shifted.

"Not intellectually."

"That's an important distinction."

He gave a reluctant laugh.

"I suppose it is."

She looked toward the ship.

"You've been part of crews that trusted you with their lives."

"Yes."

"You've solved problems nobody else could solve."

"Occasionally."

"And you've made mistakes."

"More than occasionally."

"Yet you still believe that not knowing something is a personal failure."

Reg stared at his hands. Liora did not press him.

Finally he said, "The captain trusts me."

"I know."

"What if he's wrong?"

She leaned forward.

"Reg, trust doesn't mean believing somebody will never fail. It means knowing what they'll do when they discover they might have."

He looked up.

"Do you know what Captain Hawkes did when you brought him the discrepancy?"

"He ordered the test shut down."

"And?"

"He told me to preserve the data."

"Did he blame you?"

"No."

"Did he demand an explanation you couldn't provide?"

"No."

"Then perhaps he's already answered your question."

Reg looked at the padd.

For the first time in two days, he turned it off.

***

Captain Ashley Hawkes was halfway through dinner when Brakk called.

Noah had arrived from Avalon carrying a container of something he claimed was authentic Earth barbecue.

Ashley had been skeptical. The smell had changed her mind.

They were sitting at the dining table, talking about the children, when her combadge chirped.

"Hawkes."

"Captain, Brakk. I need five minutes."

"Can it wait until morning?"

"I wish it could."

Ashley set down her fork.

"Go ahead."

"We've had another unauthorized control event. Residential garden pump. Yesterday at 1136."

Noah looked up. Ashley noticed.

"Any damage?"

"Pump coupling. Minor. I can fix it."

"Then what's the concern?"

"The valve closure wasn't ordered by Maintenance. The system says it was authorized."

"By whom?"

Brakk hesitated.

"That's the interesting part."

"Chief."

"Registry."

Ashley closed her eyes.

"Imani?"

"Her credentials."

Noah set down his drink. Ashley stood.

"Preserve the original logs. Don't reset anything until Registry has copied them."

"Already done."

"Good. I'll be there in ten minutes."

She ended the channel.

Noah was already reaching for his jacket.

"No," Ashley said.

He stopped.

"What?"

"This is my station."

"I know."

"And you're having dinner with your wife."

"I also know that."

"Then finish your dinner."

He looked at her.

"Ashley."

"If I need Avalon's captain, I'll call Avalon's captain."

For a moment, neither spoke.

Then Noah sat down.

"All right."

She stepped around the table and kissed him.

"I love you."

"I love you too."

"And don't eat my brisket."

He glanced at her plate.

"I wasn't going to."

She gave him a look.

"Fine," he admitted. "I was considering it."

She smiled. Then she left to command her station.

Noah sat alone for a moment, listening to the quiet apartment.

He looked toward the window. Avalon was visible beyond the docking structures.

He could go back aboard. He could join Reg and Sorev, review the test, make himself useful.

Instead, he picked up his fork.

Ashley had asked him to stay.

For once, that was the harder order.

And he intended to obey it.

*End of Chapter Three.*

# Chapter Four: A Door That Should Not Open

*Jupiter Station, 2395*

The garden smelled of wet stone and scorched plastic.

Ashley arrived to find Brakk on his knees beneath an open access panel, with a maintenance technician holding a work light over his shoulder. The garden itself looked perfectly ordinary. A little dry, perhaps, and one of the hanging ferns had begun to curl at the edges, but there were children asleep in the residential apartments above it who had no idea that the plants beneath their feet were part of a stationwide mystery.

"Report," Ashley said.

Brakk slid out from under the panel. He had a black smear across his cheek and the expression of a man who had already been asked three questions too many.

"Pump was commanded to run against a closed valve. We have the damaged coupling. I can replace it. The control event carries Registry authorization."

"Which Registry doesn't recognize."

"That's right."

"Could the pump have done this to itself?"

"Not with the controls we installed."

"Could somebody have modified those controls?"

"Yes. Somebody can modify almost anything if they have the access and enough time."

"Do we have evidence that somebody did?"

"No."

Ashley looked at the technician. "Ensign Venn, is there any reason the garden would need an emergency shutdown at 1136?"

Talia shook her head. "The irrigation cycle wasn't even scheduled then. The gardeners had just finished the morning inspection."

"Anyone injured?"

"No, Captain."

"Any damage beyond the coupling?"

"Some of the plants lost water for a day."

"Then let's keep this in proportion." Ashley looked back at Brakk. "Fix the garden. Investigate the control event separately. And don't let anybody turn this into a stationwide systems failure before we know what happened."

Brakk nodded, then hesitated.

"There's something else."

He held up the warped coupling.

"That valve closed hard. If the pressure relief hadn't worked, we'd have split the line and flooded the compartment below."

"Which compartment?"

"Residential storage. Family lockers, mostly."

Ashley glanced toward the ceiling. "So the safety system worked."

"It did."

"Good. We should remember that too."

She left Brakk to his repairs and walked to the garden's public entrance. A small girl was standing there with a toy starship in her hand.

"Is the garden going to die?" the girl asked.

Ashley crouched.

"Not if Chief Brakk has anything to say about it."

"He's loud."

"He is."

"My dad says he can fix anything."

"Your dad sounds like a sensible man."

The girl turned the model over. One nacelle hung at a peculiar angle.

"Can he fix this?"

Ashley studied the little ship.

"I think that's a job for your father."

"He made it."

"Then he's definitely qualified."

The girl looked unconvinced.

"What's your name?" Ashley asked.

"Mara."

"I'm Ashley."

"I know. You're the captain."

"That's right."

"Do captains fix things?"

"Sometimes."

"What if they can't?"

Ashley thought of the impossible timestamp, the valid signature, the garden pump.

"Then we find somebody who can help."

Mara nodded, apparently satisfied, and ran back toward a man waiting by the promenade rail.

Ashley watched them go.

She had commanded vessels before. On a ship, people learned to think of the captain as the person who could make a decision when everything else failed. On a station, that wasn't enough. People needed to know there would still be a place for their children to play tomorrow. That a garden would be watered. That a promise about a visit to Avalon might eventually be kept.

She touched her combadge.

"Hawkes to Registry. Imani, meet me in Operations. Bring the original transfer package."

***

Imani had prepared three copies of the evidence and one list of things she could not explain.

The copies were physically separated. One remained in Registry, one had been placed in a station archive isolated from the network, and one sat on a padd in her hands. She had signed each transfer herself, this time with a second officer watching.

When Ashley entered Operations, Imani was already waiting beside the main table.

"Tell me what's changed."

"Nothing in the original record," Imani said. "But I've found another event with the same authorization pattern."

"The pump."

"Yes. I didn't discover it through the computer's search index. Brakk brought me the local controller's raw log. That's important."

"Why?"

"Because the stationwide index doesn't list the valve closure as an unauthorized event. It lists it as scheduled maintenance."

Ashley frowned. "Scheduled by whom?"

"Me."

"Again."

"Again."

Imani enlarged the record. The authorization field displayed her credentials, followed by a long verification string.

"These aren't ordinary forged credentials," she said. "The cryptographic signature matches the one generated by my security token."

"Was the token stolen?"

"No. It never left my possession."

"Duplicated?"

"Security is checking. But there is a problem with that theory."

"Go on."

"The token rotates its authorization key every hour. A duplicate would have needed the correct key for that exact interval."

"Which a sufficiently capable attacker could obtain."

"Possibly. But the record also contains a response from a system that wasn't connected to Registry at the time."

Ashley studied the display.

"Which system?"

"Avalon's experimental archive."

For a moment, the room seemed to grow quieter.

"Explain that."

"The pump controller claims it requested verification from Avalon's archive. The archive claims it received the request and returned approval."

"Did it?"

"According to Avalon's local log, no."

"Then the station's record is wrong."

"One of the records is wrong," Imani said. "Or the records don't describe the same event."

Ashley looked at her sharply.

"That's Reg's language."

"I know."

"Have you spoken to him?"

"Not since the original transfer."

Ashley walked to the window. Avalon rested beyond the docking structure, her hull still surrounded by maintenance craft.

"Send the records to Captain Hawkes. Not the summaries. Everything."

"Yes, Captain."

"And tell Security to keep looking for an ordinary explanation. I don't want anybody deciding that because a mystery is unusual, it must be supernatural."

Imani's expression softened.

"That's exactly what I was going to recommend."

Ashley gave her a small smile.

"Then we're both having a productive morning."

***

Jake Sisko arrived on Jupiter Station carrying a battered leather bag, three notebooks, and a question he had been trying not to ask for the better part of a decade.

He was met at the docking concourse by an ensign who looked too young to remember the Dominion War.

"Mr. Sisko? Welcome aboard."

"Thanks. Is Captain Hawkes expecting me?"

"Captain Ashley Hawkes is in Operations. Captain Noah Hawkes is aboard Avalon."

Jake smiled. "That sounds about right."

The ensign led him through a bright corridor into the main promenade. Jake slowed as they passed a bakery, a repair shop, a small school entrance, and a crowded table where three civilian pilots were playing cards with more enthusiasm than skill.

He had spent enough of his life on Deep Space Nine to recognize the beginnings of a community.

A station could be built in months or years. A home took longer. It happened when somebody knew how you took your coffee. When a child learned which shopkeeper would let her watch the ovens. When an argument about docking fees became a story people told over dinner.

Jake stopped beside the observation window.

"Beautiful," he said.

The ensign looked out at Jupiter. "You get used to it."

"No," Jake said. "You learn to stop looking. That's different."

The ensign appeared uncertain whether she had been corrected.

Jake spared her the trouble.

"Sorry. Writer's habit."

He had accepted the invitation to serve as Observer Prime because the title made him uncomfortable. It sounded grand enough to conceal almost anything. Starfleet had used similarly grand language during the war, and Jake had watched people disappear behind it.

An observer, he had insisted, must be permitted to report what he saw, including what command preferred not to discuss.

Ashley had agreed.

That was why he had come.

He found her in Operations, listening to a registry officer explain a suspicious authorization.

"Mr. Sisko," Ashley said. "Welcome to Jupiter Station."

"Captain."

"I apologize for not meeting you personally."

"I've been here ten minutes. Already found the bakery. Your hospitality is doing fine."

Ashley smiled.

"You're earlier than expected."

"I caught an earlier transport."

"Good. We may need you sooner than we thought."

Jake looked at the padd in Imani's hands.

"That sounds ominous."

"At the moment, it's paperwork."

"I've learned to fear paperwork."

Ashley motioned him toward the table.

"How much have you been told about Avalon's refit?"

"Enough to know somebody would rather I hadn't heard about it."

"Then we'll start with what we can prove."

Jake set down his bag.

It was the beginning of a conversation that would alter the way he understood his own work.

He had spent years believing the purpose of a witness was to tell the truth.

Jupiter Station would teach him that the harder obligation was sometimes to admit which truth he could not establish.

***

On Avalon, Noah stood beside Reg and Sorev as a new set of station records filled the engineering display.

Reg had made a discovery, and he was not pleased about it.

"The pump controller says it received approval from our experimental archive," he said.

"Did it?" Noah asked.

"No."

"Are you sure?"

"I've checked the isolated archive, the network logs, the diagnostic buffer, and the maintenance backup. There's no corresponding request."

Sorev touched the screen.

"That is not entirely accurate."

Reg looked at him.

"Excuse me?"

"There is no corresponding request in the active archive. There is, however, a matching transaction identifier in the experimental system's discarded-state buffer."

Reg stared.

"The discarded-state buffer?"

"Yes."

"That's not possible. We shut the test down."

"After the buffer was created."

Reg opened the record. A short string of characters appeared.

He read it once.

Then again.

"Oh, that's bad."

Noah moved closer.

"How bad?"

Reg enlarged the data.

"It's a request from the station pump controller. The exact request. Same identifier, same timestamp, same authorization challenge."

"And the response?"

"Approved."

Noah waited.

Reg swallowed.

"Captain, the buffer was generated during the forty-seven-second test."

"Yesterday."

"Yes."

"The pump event was yesterday too."

"Yes, but the archive wasn't connected to the station. It couldn't have received the request."

Sorev folded his hands.

"Unless the buffer is a record of a state that did not persist."

Reg looked at him.

"That's a theory."

"Correct."

"An extraordinary theory."

"Also correct."

Noah watched them.

"Explain it without the words temporal or quantum."

Reg rubbed his forehead.

"Imagine writing a letter. You put it in an envelope, address it, and leave it on the table. Then you change your mind and burn it."

"All right."

"Now imagine the recipient has a copy of the letter."

"That sounds like somebody mailed it."

"Except you know you didn't."

Noah glanced at Sorev.

"And what does that mean for the station?"

"It means," Sorev said, "that the pump controller may be responding to a command issued in a sequence of events that is not represented by our current records."

Noah was silent.

Then he said, "You're telling me a version of the test we shut down might still have consequences."

"We are saying the evidence permits that hypothesis," Sorev replied.

"That's a yes with more syllables."

Reg gave a weak smile.

Noah looked at the display again.

# Chapter Five: The Shape of a Promise

*Jupiter Station, 2395*

The first thing Ellie noticed was that her father had forgotten the flowers.

He had promised to bring them from Earth, real ones, not replicated. She had told him it didn't matter, which was the kind of thing children said when they wanted adults to remember without being reminded.

He arrived at the station's family quarters with an overnight bag, a crooked smile, and no flowers.

Ellie looked at his empty hands.

Noah stopped in the doorway.

"Oh, no."

She folded her arms.

"You forgot."

"I did."

"You promised."

"I know."

"Mom said you would."

"That was optimistic of her."

Ellie tried to remain stern, but her mouth betrayed her.

"Are you going to tell me the replicator is broken?"

"No. I'm going to tell you I got distracted and made a mistake."

She considered that.

"That's a better excuse."

"It's not an excuse."

"Then it's a better apology."

He laughed and pulled her into a hug.

She smelled the familiar fabric of his uniform and the faint metallic odor that seemed to follow him home from Avalon. For a moment she held on harder than she meant to.

"How long are you staying?" she asked.

"Tonight. Most of tomorrow."

"Most?"

"That's the honest answer."

She stepped back.

"Can you help with my project?"

"What's the project?"

"A family history."

Noah blinked.

"That sounds dangerous."

"It's supposed to be about where we come from."

"Well, that's a long story."

"Good. I have twelve pages."

He stared at her.

"Twelve?"

"Minimum."

"Your teacher is a cruel person."

Ellie laughed.

From the kitchen, Ashley called, "I heard that."

Noah raised his voice.

"I said dedicated educator."

"You did not."

"No, I didn't."

Ellie took his hand and pulled him toward the table.

Her project covered most of its surface. There were photographs, handwritten notes, and a map with little colored markers. Some of the pictures were from places Noah recognized immediately. Others showed people he hadn't seen in years.

He sat down slowly.

"Where did you get these?"

"Mom helped."

Ashley appeared in the doorway.

"Some of them were in storage."

Noah touched one photograph.

It showed a much younger version of himself, before command, before Avalon, before the long years that had made his face familiar to Starfleet officers who had never met him.

He looked uncomfortable.

Ellie noticed.

"What's wrong?"

"Nothing."

"Dad."

He smiled at her.

"That picture reminds me of somebody I used to be."

"Was he nice?"

Noah looked at the photograph again.

"He tried."

"Was he brave?"

"Sometimes."

"Did he know he was going to be a captain?"

"No."

"Then how did he become one?"

Noah leaned back.

"That's going to take more than twelve pages."

"Good," Ellie said. "I brought extra paper."

***

Across the station, Dr. Liora Renn was reviewing a message from Reg.

It was short, which made it unusual.

*Thank you for speaking with me. I slept six hours. I am attempting to regard that as progress.*

She smiled and closed the message.

A counselor learned to measure victories differently from an engineer. Six hours of sleep might not solve a temporal anomaly, but it could keep a frightened man from mistaking exhaustion for certainty.

Her next appointment was with a civilian teacher named Lena Dalen.

Lena arrived carrying a bag of children's artwork and an expression of carefully controlled anger.

"I'm not here for counseling," she said.

"All right."

"I'm here because my husband keeps accepting work he doesn't need to do."

"That sounds frustrating."

"It is."

Liora indicated a chair.

Lena sat, then immediately stood again.

"He thinks if he isn't useful every minute, somebody will decide we don't belong here."

"Has somebody said that?"

"No. That's the problem. Nobody has to."

Liora waited.

Lena looked down at the bag.

"We came here because we wanted Mara to grow up somewhere she could see the stars. Tomas said a station was a place people could build a life, not just pass through. But every time something breaks, he runs toward it as though the whole place will disappear if he doesn't."

"Have you told him?"

"Every day."

"Has he heard you?"

Lena laughed without humor.

"He hears the words."

Liora considered that.

"Perhaps he's trying to keep a promise you didn't know he made."

Lena sat down again.

"What promise?"

"That he would make this place safe for you."

Lena looked away.

Outside the office, children passed in the corridor, talking about a school exhibition.

After a while she said, "I don't need him to make the whole station safe."

"What do you need?"

"I need him to come home."

Tomas Dalen was not at home.

He stood beneath the western garden with Brakk, staring at the controller that had issued an impossible command.

"I didn't accept the work order," Tomas said.

"I believe you," Brakk replied.

"Then why am I here?"

"That's not the same as authorizing a repair."

"No. It isn't."

Brakk held out a padd.

Brakk stared.

"Hasn't been activated?"

Tomas nodded.

"It's for the next service cycle. We're not supposed to use it until the new control software is installed."

"When is that?"

"Next month."

Brakk looked at the timestamp.

1136.

He thought of Imani's signature, the archive transfer, and the request that Avalon claimed never to have received.

"Can you prove that profile wasn't active yesterday?"

"Yes. I have the installation manifest."

"Then get it."

Tomas hesitated.

"Chief, I promised my daughter I'd be home."

Brakk looked at him.

For a moment, the old engineer saw himself years earlier, working through a holiday while people he loved waited at a table that grew colder with every hour.

He had once believed the station would remember his sacrifices.

Stations did not remember sacrifices.

People did.

"Go home," Brakk said.

"But the manifest..."

"Send it in the morning."

"Are you sure?"

"Yes."

Tomas stared.

Brakk waved him away.

"Don't make me repeat myself."

Tomas smiled.

"Thank you, Chief."

"Tell your daughter the garden will survive."

"I will."

When Tomas had gone, Brakk sat on an overturned equipment case and listened to the repaired pump.

Its steady hum filled the compartment.

He had fixed the machine.

For once, he had also sent the man home.

# Chapter Six: The Man Who Signed Tomorrow

*Jupiter Station, 2395*

The Dalen apartment smelled of cinnamon, warm bread, and something that had gone wrong in the replicator.

Jake Sisko had expected a formal interview. Tomas had expected an accusation. Lena had expected neither and was doing her best to hide how little she liked having an official observer in her kitchen.

Mara had expected a visitor who might help with her model starship.

She was the only one enjoying herself.

"That's the wrong nacelle," she informed Jake, who was attempting to reattach the crooked piece.

"I thought they were interchangeable."

"They're not."

"Then I'm glad I'm not an engineer."

"Dad says that all the time."

Tomas looked up from the table. "I do not."

"You said it when you fixed the toaster."

"That was a different situation."

Lena placed a plate between Jake and her husband.

"Mr. Sisko came to ask about the maintenance order, Mara."

"I know."

"Then let him ask."

Mara considered this. "He can ask while he fixes it."

Jake smiled. "A fair arrangement."

He set the model down carefully, opened his notebook, and looked at Tomas.

"Tell me about the work order."

Tomas folded his hands. They were large, scarred hands, with a faint discoloration around one thumb where an old plasma burn had healed badly.

"I received it after my shift. It said I'd accepted an inspection in the western garden. I hadn't."

"Did you open the controller?"

"Earlier that week. Routine inspection. Everything worked."

"Did you leave a service key installed?"

"No. I logged out and sealed the access panel."

"Did anyone else know your credentials?"

"My supervisor. Security, if they asked. My wife knows the code to our apartment, but I don't think she's been sneaking into pump controls."

Lena gave him a look. "Not unless they need a very thorough cleaning."

Jake let the small joke pass.

"What made you report the order instead of ignoring it?"

Tomas glanced at Lena.

"She told me to."

"That's not the whole answer," Lena said.

Tomas rubbed his thumb.

"I nearly went down there. It was marked urgent. That's what you do when you're needed."

"Even when you're not scheduled?"

"Especially then. Somebody else might be in trouble."

Jake wrote that down.

"Was anybody in trouble?"

"Not when I received the order. The pump was already repaired."

"So you were being sent to inspect a repaired system."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Tomas looked at him. "That's what we're trying to find out."

Jake nodded. It was a perfectly fair answer.

Lena stood by the kitchen counter, her arms crossed.

"May I ask you something, Mr. Sisko?"

"Of course."

"If this turns out to be somebody using Tomas's identity, will you say that plainly?"

"If the evidence supports it."

"And if it doesn't?"

"Then I'll say that too."

She studied him for a moment.

"People are already talking. Somebody told the school the station's computers are haunted."

Mara looked delighted.

"Really?"

"No," Lena said quickly.

Jake leaned forward. "I don't think they're haunted, Mara."

"Could they be?"

"I don't know enough about ghosts to give you a reliable answer."

Lena gave an involuntary laugh.

Jake turned back to Tomas.

"What happened before you received the order?"

"Nothing unusual."

"Walk me through it anyway."

Tomas described the workshop, the burnt toast, the argument about dinner, the terminal chirping. At first the details came reluctantly. Then he began remembering little things: a message from the parts supplier, a neighbor borrowing a tool, the sound of a cargo transport departing from the berth beyond the service corridor.

Jake listened.

When Tomas mentioned the second alert, Jake stopped writing.

"You said it arrived again."

"Yes."

"Was the first order still on the screen?"

"No. I'd opened a message to Maintenance Control."

"And the second alert interrupted you?"

"Yes."

"Did you touch anything?"

"Only the terminal."

"Show me."

They moved to a small desk beside the kitchen. Tomas brought up the preserved workstation image from the station archive. Imani had frozen it after his report.

The first work order had been marked pending.

The second had been marked accepted.

Jake pointed to a small field beneath the authorization line.

"What does that mean?"

Tomas leaned closer.

"That's the profile version."

"It says seven."

"Right."

"What version was installed?"

"Six."

Jake waited.

Tomas opened a maintenance catalog.

The future profile, version seven, had been scheduled for deployment the following month. It introduced a feature called automatic acceptance recovery, intended to keep routine repair requests from disappearing when the network was interrupted.

"Could the new profile accept a work order without you?" Jake asked.

"Only if I'd authorized a standing rule."

"Had you?"

"No."

"Would you have?"

Tomas considered it.

"Probably. For emergency systems. But not garden irrigation."

Jake pointed to the screen.

"Then why did version seven think you had?"

Tomas stared at the record.

"I don't know."

Lena spoke from behind them.

"Is that important?"

Jake looked at the two versions of the software.

"Maybe. The question isn't only who signed tomorrow's work order. It's what tomorrow's system would have done if it believed the signature."

Mara, still holding her starship, looked up.

"Would it have fixed the pump?"

Tomas turned.

"No, sweetheart."

"Then what would it do?"

He looked back at the screen.

"That's what worries me."

Brakk had spent the morning building a machine that would never be allowed near a working station system.

It occupied half of a sealed maintenance bay and had been assembled from a retired pump controller, a diagnostic relay, and an old cargo handling computer that had once been dropped down a service shaft.

The computer still bore the dent.

Ensign Venn stood beside it with a padd.

"Chief, why are we using damaged equipment?"

"Because if this goes wrong, I'd rather break something that's already broken."

"That isn't reassuring."

"It wasn't meant to be."

The test had one purpose. Brakk wanted to know what the future maintenance profile would do if it encountered the disputed work order. Not in theory. Not according to a software specification written by somebody who had never crawled through a flooded access tunnel.

He wanted to see it act.

Talia checked the isolation barriers.

"No network connection."

"Correct."

"No station controls."

"Correct."

"No remote command capability."

"Correct."

"And the test pump is filled with water?"

"Enough to make a mess."

She gave him a look.

He grinned.

"Now you're learning."

They loaded the version-seven profile from the installation package. The package was genuine. It had been delivered from Starfleet Logistics and remained sealed until Imani authorized its use for the test.

Brakk inserted a copy of the anomalous work order.

The controller woke.

A green indicator flashed.

Then another.

Talia read the display.

"Work order accepted."

"By whom?"

"Tomas Dalen."

"He's not here."

"Correct."

"Is there a standing authorization?"

"No."

Brakk leaned forward.

"Then why did it accept?"

Talia examined the diagnostic trace.

"It found a prior authorization record."

"Where?"

"In the recovery cache."

"Show me."

A line appeared.

The cache contained a transaction that should not have existed.

The timestamp matched the disputed 1136 interval.

Brakk looked at the old cargo computer.

"That cache was empty when we started."

"I verified it myself," Talia said.

"Run the test again."

They reset the controller.

The work order loaded.

The same green light appeared.

Then the pressure test began.

A valve closed.

The pump started.

Water hammered against the sealed line.

Brakk slapped the manual cutoff.

The pump stopped with a shudder.

Talia stared at the pressure gauge.

"That would have broken the coupling."

"It did break the coupling."

"But this is the future software."

"Yes."

"And the real garden wasn't running future software."

"No."

Talia looked at him.

"Then why did the garden behave exactly like this?"

Brakk said nothing.

He opened the controller's diagnostic history.

The recovery cache had created a new entry.

It showed a command to the station's main irrigation controller.

That controller was not connected.

Brakk checked the physical cable. Its end lay on the deck, several meters from the nearest socket.

He picked it up.

"Well," he said softly. "That's unpleasant."

"Chief?"

"Get Imani."

"What should I tell her?"

"Tell her I want a witness who doesn't work for Engineering."

***

Imani arrived with two security officers and a recorder that operated independently of the station network.

Brakk repeated the test.

This time, the pump did not start.

He frowned.

"That's not what happened."

Talia checked the settings.

"Everything's identical."

"Try again."

Nothing.

Brakk opened the diagnostic buffer.

The recovery cache was empty.

Imani looked at him.

"Are you certain the earlier test produced the command?"

"Yes."

"Do you have a recording?"

Talia held up her padd.

"The local recorder captured it."

Imani examined the file.

It showed the valve closing, the pump starting, Brakk reaching for the cutoff. The pressure gauge rose exactly as he had described.

But the controller's own log showed no such event.

Imani looked from the recording to the silent machine.

"Which record do you want me to preserve?"

"Both," Brakk said.

She nodded.

"Good answer."

Brakk folded his arms.

"I don't like this."

"Neither do I."

"No, you don't understand. Machines aren't supposed to change their minds about whether they nearly exploded."

Imani considered the sentence.

"Neither are people."

Brakk looked at her.

She returned his gaze.

"Let's not decide which record is false because one is more convenient."

He exhaled.

"All right. What do you need?"

"Everything. The test setup, the disconnected cable, the installation package, the local recording, the controller's empty log. And I want Ensign Venn's statement before anyone shows her a proposed explanation."

Talia looked startled.

"Mine?"

"You were here."

"I only watched."

"That makes you important."

Brakk regarded the young technician. She was trying to hide how frightened she had become.

"She's right," he told her. "Tell them what you saw. Not what you think I want you to say."

Talia nodded.

For the first time, she understood why the captain had insisted that a damaged garden pump mattered.

***

On Avalon, Sorev had reached a conclusion Reg disliked.

The conclusion was not about the anomaly.

It was about Reg.

"You are treating the solar coordinates as a warning," Sorev said.

"They might be."

"They might also be meaningless."

"Then why are they there?"

"That question does not establish their significance."

Reg paced the science laboratory.

"All right. Suppose the buffer contains information from a future state. Suppose that state involves the sun. Doesn't that mean we should investigate?"

"It means we should preserve the data and examine possible explanations."

"That's what I'm doing."

"You have already requested three solar observatory surveys, two restricted navigation reports, and a classified engineering memorandum on stellar containment."

Reg stopped.

"Those are legitimate research materials."

"They are. But your requests imply that the coordinates describe a future emergency."

"They might."

"You have repeated that phrase six times."

Reg looked at him.

"Would you prefer I say I'm certain?"

"I would prefer you distinguish your fear from your evidence."

The words struck harder than Sorev intended.

Reg turned away.

For a moment, he was back on another ship, years earlier, convinced that everyone could see how badly he wanted to be useful. He remembered standing outside a conference room, rehearsing a report until the words lost meaning. He remembered being wrong in public. He remembered being right and too frightened to speak.

Sorev watched him.

"Commander."

Reg said nothing.

"I did not intend to dismiss your concern."

"Well, you have a remarkable way of doing it accidentally."

Sorev folded his hands.

"Your concern is reasonable. Your conclusion is premature."

Reg laughed once, without humor.

"That's very comforting."

"Comfort was not my objective."

"Yes, I've noticed."

They stood in silence.

Then Sorev spoke again.

"When I first examined the discrepancy, I believed it was a sensor artifact. You challenged that assumption. You were correct to do so."

Reg looked at him.

"And now?"

"Now I believe you are at risk of replacing one unsupported assumption with another."

Reg sat down.

He knew Sorev was right about the assumption. He also knew something else, something that had been growing in him since the first impossible measurement.

He was afraid that if he stopped looking, the answer would arrive too late.

"Have you ever had a feeling," he asked quietly, "that something terrible was going to happen and that if you just worked hard enough you could prevent it?"

Sorev considered the question.

"Yes."

Reg looked up, surprised.

Sorev's expression remained composed.

"On Vulcan, during my early service, I received a warning concerning a transport accident. The warning was imprecise. I spent three days attempting to identify the vessel involved."

"Did you?"

"No."

"What happened?"

"The accident occurred. It involved a civilian transport I had never examined."

Reg waited.

"Did people die?"

"Yes."

"I'm sorry."

"Thank you."

Sorev looked at the stars beyond the laboratory window.

"I spent many years believing that a sufficiently rigorous investigation would have prevented the loss. Eventually I understood that the warning had not contained enough information to identify the vessel. My effort was sincere. It was not sufficient."

Reg stared at him.

"You never told me that."

"You never asked."

"That's not fair."

"No," Sorev said. "It is not."

The silence between them changed.

Reg opened the solar coordinates again.

"What would you do?" he asked.

"I would continue the investigation. I would not call it a prediction. And I would sleep before making a decision that affects the ship."

Reg gave a reluctant smile.

"You've been talking to Dr. Renn."

"She is persuasive."

"She's relentless."

"That too."

Reg closed the display.

"All right. One more hour."

Sorev raised an eyebrow.

"Thirty minutes," Reg amended.

***

Noah found Ashley in a small observation room overlooking the station's civilian docking bays.

She had been there for twenty minutes, watching a freighter unload crates of fruit.

He entered quietly.

"Are you hiding?"

"From you? Not successfully."

He sat beside her.

"Imani sent the latest report."

"I know."

"Brakk reproduced the event once."

"Then couldn't reproduce it again."

"Right."

Ashley watched a cargo worker drop a crate. Oranges rolled across the deck, and three people rushed to gather them.

"Do you remember when we first talked about living on a station?" she asked.

Noah smiled.

"You said you wanted somewhere with a proper kitchen."

"I said I wanted somewhere our family could eat dinner without an emergency interrupting us."

"That's a harder specification."

"Apparently."

He looked at her.

"You're worried."

"Of course I'm worried."

"About the experiment?"

"About what people do when they think the experiment explains everything."

Noah waited.

Ashley gestured toward the freighter.

"That crew doesn't care about a missing second. They care whether their cargo arrives. Mara Dalen doesn't care about temporal physics. She wants to see Avalon. Lena wants her husband home. If we start treating every ordinary failure as evidence of some grand mystery, we'll stop seeing the people standing in front of us."

Noah nodded slowly.

"You're right."

"I know."

He laughed.

She turned toward him.

"And you have to decide what you're going to do with that refit."

"The tests are suspended."

"That's not the same as deciding."

"No."

"Starfleet wants the system completed."

"They do."

"Do you?"

Noah looked out at Avalon.

He remembered the first time he had walked onto her bridge as captain. Not the day he had been promoted. Not the ceremony or the photographs. The moment after the senior officers had left and the bridge was quiet, when he had sat in the command chair and realized nobody else was going to make the final decision.

He had worked a long time to earn that chair.

He had also learned that wanting something badly did not make it safe.

"I want to understand what we built," he said.

"And if you can't?"

"Then I won't let them turn it on."

Ashley studied him.

"Even if they order you?"

"Then they'll have to relieve me."

She took his hand.

"Good."

He looked at her.

"You'd do the same."

"Yes."

They sat together until the cargo crew collected the last orange.

Then Ashley stood.

"I have a station to run."

"And I have a ship to command."

She kissed him.

"Try not to lose any more seconds."

"No promises."

"That's what worries me."

***

That evening, Jake returned to the Dalen apartment with a copy of his interview notes.

He had promised Lena the chance to correct factual errors before he used the material in any official account.

She read slowly.

Tomas stood by the window, holding Mara's model starship.

Jake waited.

At last Lena looked up.

"You wrote that he thinks he has to earn our place here."

"That's what I understood you to say."

"I did say something like that."

"Would you like me to change it?"

She looked at Tomas.

He was staring at the little ship.

"Yes," she said. "I would."

Jake opened his notebook.

"What should it say?"

Lena took a breath.

"Write that he believes being useful is how he keeps people safe. And that sometimes he forgets the people he's trying to protect would rather have him beside them."

Tomas turned.

"Lena."

She looked at him.

"It's true."

He stared at her for a long moment.

Then he set down the ship.

"I thought if I stopped working, somebody else would have to do it."

"Somebody else can."

"What if they can't?"

"Then they can ask you tomorrow."

He sat beside her.

Jake looked down at his notes.

This was the part of witnessing that nobody had explained when he accepted the assignment. The official records would contain timestamps, signatures, and technical findings. They would not contain the way Tomas's face changed when Lena said she wanted him home.

Unless somebody wrote it down.

Mara entered the room carrying a sheet of paper.

"Mr. Sisko?"

"Yes?"

"I wrote the ending."

"To what?"

"The story about my dad."

She handed him the page.

In careful handwriting, she had written:

*He fixed the ship. Then he came home.*

Jake read it twice.

He looked at Tomas, then Lena.

"I think that's a very good ending."

Tomas blinked rapidly.

Lena took his hand.

Jake folded the page and placed it inside his notebook.

He did not know yet whether Tomas would get that ending.

But he knew he wanted to tell the story honestly enough that, if the ending changed, the people who loved Tomas would still recognize the man on the page.

*End of Chapter Six.*

# Chapter Seven: The Shape of the Sun

*USS Avalon, Earth Orbit, 2395*

The solar coordinates were wrong in a way Reg found more disturbing than if they had been impossible.

They described a perfectly ordinary point in space, a little above the sun's equatorial plane, far from any established traffic corridor. There was no secret station there, no research platform, no debris field. The region had been surveyed so often that the data was almost boring.

Almost.

Reg enlarged the observatory images.

"There's nothing there," he said.

Sorev stood beside him.

"That is the current observation."

"Yesterday's observation, last month's observation, the entire archived series. Nothing."

"Correct."

"Then why would the discarded buffer include the coordinates?"

"Unknown."

Reg turned.

"You know, you could occasionally say something reassuring."

"I could. It would not alter the evidence."

"Just once. For morale."

Sorev considered it.

"The sun is behaving normally."

Reg stared.

"That's what you've got?"

"Would you prefer it were not?"

"No."

"Then you are reassured."

Reg made a sound that might have been laughter if he had been less tired.

The laboratory door opened.

Captain Noah Hawkes entered with a message from Starfleet Engineering.

"Bad news," he said.

Reg straightened.

"Has something happened?"

"Not yet. They want us to resume the refit acceptance tests."

Sorev took the padd.

"On what basis?"

"They say our discrepancy doesn't establish a safety hazard."

Reg stared at him.

"It doesn't establish safety either."

"I know."

"They can't order us to run the system until we understand the buffer."

"They can order us to finish the refit."

"Captain..."

Noah held up a hand.

"I'm not running the experiment."

Reg stopped.

Noah looked at both officers.

"I want a report that separates what we've observed from what we've guessed. I want every plausible ordinary explanation listed first. I want the limits of the experiment spelled out in language a tired admiral can understand. Then I'll tell Starfleet why the system stays offline."

Sorev nodded.

"That is a defensible approach."

Reg looked relieved.

Noah noticed.

"Don't thank me yet. You'll be writing most of it."

Reg's shoulders sagged.

"Of course."

"And Reg?"

"Yes, Captain?"

"Go home when you're finished."

"I live aboard the ship."

"Then go to your quarters and behave as though they have a bed."

Sorev inclined his head.

"An excellent recommendation."

Reg looked from one to the other.

"I feel surrounded."

"You are," Noah said.

***

At Jupiter Station, Imani had reached the least satisfying conclusion of her career.

The signatures were valid.

Not valid-looking. Not plausible copies. Valid according to every verification method available to her.

And she could not prove the signers had authorized the actions.

She sat across from Ashley with three padds arranged in a line.

"Registry, Maintenance, and Avalon's archive," she said. "All three claim authorization. Each source has an independent reason to doubt the event."

Ashley examined the records.

"Can we identify the origin?"

"Not with confidence."

"Can we identify a person who benefited?"

"No."

"Can we identify a system that was compromised?"

"No."

Ashley leaned back.

"So we have evidence of something wrong and no evidence of what caused it."

"Yes."

"That's an answer."

Imani looked up.

"Captain?"

"It's not the answer we wanted. It's still an answer."

Imani had been afraid to say it plainly. A registry officer was supposed to produce clean histories, not collections of incompatible facts.

Ashley seemed to understand.

"What would you recommend?" she asked.

"Independent confirmation for critical orders. Physical inspection where possible. No automatic trust between the station's records and Avalon's experimental archive."

"Agreed."

"And I recommend preserving the disputed authorizations permanently."

"Even if we eventually explain them?"

"Especially then."

Ashley smiled faintly.

"Make it so."

Imani gathered her padds.

At the door, she stopped.

"Captain, may I ask something?"

"Of course."

"If two records disagree and neither can be disproved, how do you decide what happened?"

Ashley looked toward the station's main display.

"You don't decide until you have enough evidence."

"And if you never do?"

"Then you tell the next person exactly what you couldn't establish."

Imani nodded.

It was not the tidy answer she had wanted.

It was one she could live with.

***

Jake found Reg in the observation lounge later that night.

The commander sat with a cup of tea, watching Earth turn below the ship.

"Mind if I join you?" Jake asked.

Reg looked startled.

"Mr. Sisko. No, of course not."

Jake sat.

"I've been talking to people on the station."

"I heard."

"One of them asked me how you know whether a record is true."

Reg gave a tired smile.

"If you find out, please tell me."

Jake studied him.

"That's not the answer I expected from the man who found the discrepancy."

"I didn't find an answer. I found a problem."

"Sometimes those are the same thing."

Reg shook his head.

"Not in Engineering."

Jake looked out the window.

"My father used to tell me that people want stories because stories have endings. Life isn't always so considerate."

Reg smiled.

"Your father sounds wise."

"He was. He could also be stubborn enough to argue with a bulkhead."

"That sounds familiar."

Jake laughed.

For a while they watched the planet.

Then Jake said, "I met a little girl named Mara Dalen."

Reg nodded.

"Her father works in Maintenance."

"Yes. She asked me to write a story about him. In her version, he fixes the ship and comes home."

Reg looked at him.

"That's a good ending."

"It is."

"Will you write it?"

"I don't know yet."

"Why not?"

"Because I don't know what happens to him."

Reg considered that.

"Maybe you could write the story up to the point where you know."

Jake turned toward him.

"That's what I'm trying to do."

Reg looked down at his tea.

"I think I've been doing the opposite."

"What do you mean?"

"I've been trying to start with the ending. Something terrible happens near the sun. The buffer is a warning. I have to prevent it."

"And you don't know any of that."

"No."

Jake nodded.

"Then perhaps you should write the story you actually have."

Reg looked at him.

"A story about a missing second?"

"A story about people trying to understand one."

Reg sat quietly.

He had spent his life trying to be useful by solving things. Jake was suggesting that being honest about an unsolved problem might itself be useful.

It was an uncomfortable idea.

It was also beginning to sound familiar.

***

The following morning, Noah addressed the refit review board.

The admirals appeared on the conference display, their expressions ranging from skeptical to openly irritated.

One of them, Admiral Voss, began without greeting.

"Captain Hawkes, your ship is six weeks behind schedule."

"Yes, Admiral."

"Your engineering team has produced three reports and no explanation."

"Correct."

"And you are recommending suspension of the temporal acceptance tests."

"Yes."

Voss leaned forward.

"On the basis of a one-second discrepancy?"

"On the basis of an unexplained discrepancy in an experimental system that can affect temporal measurements."

"That is not evidence of danger."

"No, Admiral."

"Then what is it?"

"Evidence that we don't understand the system well enough to certify it."

Another admiral spoke.

"Captain, Starfleet does not require certainty. It requires reasonable assurance."

"Then I can't provide it yet."

Voss looked displeased.

"Are you refusing an order?"

"No. I'm recommending that we complete the conventional refit work and isolate the temporal system pending review."

"And if Command rejects your recommendation?"

Noah paused.

He thought of Ashley on the station, of Reg's tired face, of Sorev's careful language, of the children who watched Avalon through the promenade windows.

"Then I will request a formal safety review before authorizing activation."

Voss stared.

"You understand the implications."

"I do."

The room was silent.

Then the second admiral spoke.

"Your report includes an independent station record. Why?"

"Because the station received a transfer we cannot fully explain. Captain Ashley Hawkes has preserved the original and is investigating it under her own authority."

"Is she supporting your recommendation?"

"Her investigation is independent of mine."

Voss frowned.

"That seems unnecessarily complicated."

Noah looked directly at the screen.

"Admiral, if the only witness to my ship's experiment is my ship, we don't have independent confirmation. We have a second copy of the same story."

The second admiral glanced at Voss.

"Captain, your recommendation is accepted pending review. Conventional refit work may continue. The temporal system remains offline."

Noah inclined his head.

"Thank you, Admiral."

The channel closed.

For a moment he remained seated.

Then Reg exhaled loudly.

Noah looked at him.

"You've been holding your breath."

"Only for the last ten minutes."

"Try not to do that."

Sorev raised an eyebrow.

"It would be medically inadvisable."

Reg gave him a look.

"Thank you, Commander."

Noah stood.

"All right. We have work to do."

Reg looked at the blank screen.

"Captain?"

"Yes?"

"Do you think we made the right decision?"

Noah considered it.

"I think we made the decision we could defend with the evidence we had."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," Noah said. "It isn't."

***

That night, Jupiter Station held a small school exhibition on the promenade.

Children displayed models of starships, planets, and imagined worlds. Parents stood beside them, pretending not to be more nervous than their children.

Mara Dalen's model of Avalon occupied the center of one table.

The nacelle was still crooked.

Tomas had offered to fix it.

Mara had refused.

"It looks like ours," she told him.

He looked at the ship, then at his daughter.

"Fair enough."

Lena stood beside them, holding a plate of pastries.

Jake arrived with a small notebook.

"I brought something," he said.

Mara looked up.

"A story?"

"Not finished."

She considered that.

"That's okay. Mine isn't either."

He handed her a page.

It began with a father who fixed things and a daughter who wanted to see the stars.

Mara read the first sentence, then looked at him.

"Does he come home?"

Jake smiled gently.

"So far."

She nodded.

"Then keep writing."

Across the promenade, Ashley watched the families gather. Noah stood beside her, his hand resting lightly against hers.

"How did the review go?" she asked.

"They agreed to suspend the tests."

"Good."

"For now."

"That's usually how good decisions begin."

He looked at her.

"Reg thinks the coordinates may be important."

"Do you?"

"I don't know."

Ashley nodded.

"Then don't let him carry that uncertainty alone."

Noah looked toward the children.

"I won't."

For one evening, the station's alarms remained silent. The garden pump ran. The school exhibition continued. A father went home with his family, and a captain stood beside his wife beneath a window full of stars.

The solar coordinates remained in the archive.

The missing second remained unexplained.

And the sun continued to burn.

*End of Chapter Seven.*
