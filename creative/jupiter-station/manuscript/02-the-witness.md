# Chapter Two: The Witness

*Jupiter Station, 2395*

Imani Saye had a rule about signatures: the person who signed a document ought to remember signing it.

The rule was not written in Starfleet regulations. It was too obvious for that, or perhaps not obvious enough. In her years managing registry records, she had seen signatures attached to ships that had never docked, cargo that had never arrived, and officers who had been transferred before their orders were issued. Every error had an explanation. Most of those explanations began with someone insisting the computer could not possibly be wrong.

At 0700 she printed the disputed receipt onto a padd and walked it to the station command center herself.

Captain Ashley Hawkes stood beside the main display, listening to a maintenance supervisor describe a problem with the residential gardens. The supervisor's voice rose with every sentence.

'The irrigation controls say the plants received their full allocation.'

'And did they?' Ashley asked.

'No, Captain.'

'Then stop telling me what the controls say. Tell me what the plants need.'

'Water.'

'Good. We have made progress.'

The supervisor departed with a new assignment and a visibly improved sense of urgency.

Ashley turned to Imani. 'Please tell me you brought better news.'

'I brought the original record.'

'That isn't the same thing.'

'No, Captain.'

Imani placed the padd on the table. Two timestamps. One valid signature. An authorization she had never given.

Ashley studied it without speaking.

'Can you prove it wasn't you?' she asked.

'I can prove I was somewhere else.'

'That's different.'

'Yes.'

Ashley looked up. 'You know why I asked.'

'Because if I report this as a forged signature, Security will investigate a crime. If I report it as an unexplained authorization, they will investigate the record.'

'Exactly.'

Imani felt a familiar relief. She did not need a captain who always agreed with her. She needed one who understood that an accusation and an observation were different things.

'There is another concern,' she said. 'The receipt names the station as an independent witness to the Avalon test.'

'We weren't.'

'No.'

'Could an automated process have created the witness entry?'

'It could have created the entry. It could not have made the entry true.'

Ashley read the record again. 'Quarantine the copy. Preserve the original transfer. Give Security read-only access. Nobody fixes the date until we know why it's wrong.'

'Yes, Captain.'

'And Imani?'

'Captain?'

'Thank you for bringing me a problem instead of bringing me a solution you couldn't prove.'

Imani returned to Registry with a task she understood. She would keep the record. She would keep the contradiction. If necessary, she would spend the rest of her career explaining why the two belonged together.

***

On Avalon, Reginald Barclay was explaining the same thing to Commander Sorev with considerably less success.

'The second measurement includes a signal from Jupiter Station,' Reg said. 'The first doesn't. Both have the same timestamp.'

'Yes.'

'So one of them must have been recorded later.'

'That is a reasonable possibility.'

'Then why does the computer insist they happened together?'

'Because that is what the computer recorded.'

Reg stared at him. 'You know, you can make almost anything sound irritating.'

Sorev regarded the display. 'I am told it is a talent.'

Reg almost smiled.

The compartment door opened. Captain Noah Hawkes entered carrying a small breakfast wrapped in a napkin.

'I've been informed neither of you has eaten.'

'I'm not hungry,' Reg said.

'That's why I brought two.'

He handed one to Sorev, who examined it as though the captain had given him an unexploded device.

'It contains eggs,' Noah said.

'I can see that, Captain.'

'Then eat it.'

Sorev took a careful bite. Reg looked offended on behalf of the entire engineering profession.

'Captain, we're very close to understanding the discrepancy.'

'You've been very close for eighteen hours.'

'Seventeen.'

'That makes me feel better.'

Noah set the remaining breakfast on Reg's console. 'Show me what we actually know.'

Reg opened his mouth, ready with six theories.

Noah held up a finger. 'Not what you think. What we know.'

Reg looked at Sorev.

Sorev did not smile. That was worse.

'The experiment ran,' Reg said. 'All three clocks recorded forty-seven seconds. I experienced forty-eight. The environmental record contains two measurements at one timestamp. One includes interference from a station relay. The other does not.'

'And the relay?'

'Station Operations confirms it was active at the time of the second measurement.'

'At the recorded time?'

Reg hesitated. 'At a time that would explain the second measurement.'

'But not the first.'

'Correct.'

Noah looked at the diagram. 'Then maybe we aren't looking at a missing second. Maybe we're looking at two accounts of one second.'

Sorev inclined his head. 'That formulation is more precise.'

'You can put it in the report.'

'I intend to.'

Reg sat down. He had been imagining a machine capable of reaching through time, a doorway opening onto impossible futures. The captain's description was smaller and somehow more frightening.

Two accounts.

Both preserved.

Neither sufficient on its own.

'What do we do?' he asked.

Noah looked from Reg to Sorev.

'We stop the tests. We send the records to the station. We ask people who weren't in this room to tell us what they can independently establish.'

'And if they can't?'

'Then we don't pretend they did.'

Reg looked down at his untouched breakfast.

'That's not much of an answer.'

'It's the only honest one we've got.'

***

By evening, the station had settled into the comfortable noise of a place that never truly slept.

Ashley crossed the promenade on her way home. A child was chasing a small hovering toy between the tables. Two engineers argued about the price of replacement parts. A restaurant owner she recognized from the morning had finally accepted his assigned storefront and was already advertising an opening special.

He waved at her.

She waved back.

A station was not its docking berths or its shield generators. Those mattered, certainly. But the station was also the child whose toy had stopped working, the engineer who had missed dinner, and the shopkeeper who wanted a window facing the promenade.

That was what command meant here.

She stopped beside an observation window. Avalon floated beyond the glass, her running lights bright against Jupiter's clouds.

Noah called a moment later.

'Did you eat?' he asked.

'Eventually.'

'That sounds familiar.'

'Your engineer still arguing with the clocks?'

'He and Sorev have reached the stage where they agree on the evidence and disagree about the adjectives.'

'Progress, then.'

'Remarkable progress.'

Ashley watched a shuttle pass between the station and the ship.

'Noah, whatever you found, promise me something.'

'What?'

'Don't let the mystery become more important than the people who have to live with the answer.'

He was quiet for a moment.

'I won't.'

She knew he meant it.

She also knew that good intentions had never been a sufficient safeguard against catastrophe.

'Then come home,' she said.

'On my way.'

She ended the call and remained by the window.

Far below, the giant planet turned. Far above, the stars held their familiar positions.

Somewhere between those two certainties, a computer had recorded an impossible second.

Ashley had no idea what it meant.

For tonight, she refused to let it mean more than it did.

***

In Engineering, Reg finally ate the breakfast.

It was cold.

He barely noticed.

He had spent the day trying to prove the second measurement was the false one. He had nearly succeeded twice. Both times, the evidence had collapsed under closer examination.

Now he opened a blank log.

He typed:

*The cause of the discrepancy remains unknown.*

He stared at the sentence.

Then he added:

*The original records are preserved. No alteration is authorized.*

Reg saved the entry.

It was not a discovery. It was not a victory. It would never earn him a commendation.

But when he shut down the console and went to find his friends, the two contradictory measurements remained exactly where he had left them.

For the first time that day, he could live with that.

*End of Chapter Two.*
